¶1The bakery on Magnolia Street opened at five, but Theo’s grandmother arrived at four, the way she had for thirty-one years. Theo, sixteen and not yet convinced he wanted any part of it, trailed behind her with his hands in his pockets. The summer job had been his mother’s idea.

¶2“Flour first,” his grandmother said, as if continuing a conversation they had already started. She did not measure. She scooped, tapped the side of the bowl twice, and moved on. Theo reached for a measuring cup; she slid it back onto the shelf without looking at him.

¶3By the third week, his arms ached and his shirts smelled permanently of yeast. He had also begun to notice things: the dough that sprang back too quickly needed more rest; the oven ran hot on the left side; regulars who ordered “the usual” expected her to remember, and she always did.

¶4On the last morning of August, she handed him the scoop and stepped back. Theo filled it, hesitated, and tapped the side of the bowl twice. His grandmother said nothing, but she left the measuring cup on the shelf, where it had stayed all summer, and went to unlock the front door.

Natural science · original KCE passage

¶1When a honeybee finds a good patch of flowers, it returns to the hive with more than nectar. It also brings directions. In the 1940s, the Austrian scientist Karl von Frisch showed that foraging bees share the location of food through a movement he called the waggle dance.

¶2On the vertical surface of the honeycomb, the dancing bee runs a short straight line while shaking its body from side to side, then circles back and repeats. The angle of that straight run, measured from straight up, matches the angle between the sun and the food source. A run pointed straight up tells the other bees to fly directly toward the sun.

¶3Distance is coded too. The longer the bee waggles during each run, the farther away the flowers are. Nearby bees follow the dancer closely, and many of these recruits then leave the hive and fly to the spot the dance describes.

¶4Von Frisch’s claims were not accepted right away. Some researchers argued that recruits simply followed the scent of the flowers on the dancer’s body. Later experiments, including studies that tracked individual bees with radar, supported von Frisch: recruits generally flew in the direction the dance indicated. In 1973 he shared the Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine for his work on animal behavior.

The angle of that straight run, measured from straight up, matches the angle between the sun and the food source. A run pointed straight up tells the other bees to fly directly toward the sun.

¶3Distance is coded too. The longer the bee waggles during each run, the farther away the flowers are. Nearby bees follow the dancer closely, and many of these recruits then leave the hive and fly to the spot the dance describes.

¶4Von Frisch’s claims were not accepted right away. Some researchers argued that recruits simply followed the scent of the flowers on the dancer’s body. Later experiments, including studies that tracked individual bees with radar, supported von Frisch: recruits generally flew in the direction the dance indicated. In 1973 he shared the Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine for his work on animal behavior.
