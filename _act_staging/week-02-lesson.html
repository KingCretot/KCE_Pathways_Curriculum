00 Pre-lesson

Warm-up: Week 1 review

Six questions from last week: two English rules, two word roots and two formulas from your Week 1 sheet. Pick an answer. If it’s wrong, you’ll get a hint and one more try. Miss it twice, and the answer and its logic appear.

Have ready: paper, a pencil and your Week 1 Roots & Formulas sheet if you want to check yourself afterward. No calculator needed today.
Week 1 review · Joining sentencesQ1
The bus was late again, so we walked to school.
Not yet — one more try
Cover the punctuation. Is there a FANBOYS word joining the two sentences? If so, where does the comma go?

“The bus was late again” and “we walked to school” are both complete sentences. A comma plus so (a FANBOYS word) joins them correctly, so NO CHANGE.

The traps: “again, we” is a comma splice, “again we” is a run-on, and “so,” puts the comma after the joining word instead of before it.

Week 1 review · Say it onceQ2
Maria repeated the steps again before she left.
Not yet — one more try
What does “repeated” already mean?

“Repeated” already means “did again,” so “again” says it twice. “Repeated the steps” says it once.

Week 1 roots · dictQ3
The root dict means “say.” Based on its parts, what does contradict mean?
Not yet — one more try
“Contra” means against. Put it together with “say.”

contra (against) + dict (say) = to say against, or to say the opposite. On ACT Reading, a wrong answer often contradicts the passage.

Week 1 roots · cisQ4
The root cis means “cut.” A precise answer is one that is:
Not yet — one more try
Think “cut.” What happens to extra material when something is cut to size?

pre (before) + cis (cut): cut to size ahead of time. A precise answer is exact, with nothing extra.

Week 1 formulas · SlopeQ5
What is the slope of the line through (1, 4) and (3, 10)?
Not yet — one more try
Slope is rise over run: (y₂ − y₁) ÷ (x₂ − x₁).

Rise: 10 − 4 = 6. Run: 3 − 1 = 2. Slope: 6 ÷ 2 = 3.

The trap: 6 is the rise alone. Always divide by the run.

Week 1 formulas · Circle areaQ6
A circle has a radius of 5. What is its area?
Not yet — one more try
Area is πr². Square the radius first.

A = πr² = π × 5² = 25π.

The trap: 10π is the circumference, 2πr. Area squares the radius; circumference doubles it.

01 Subject–verb agreement

Find the real subject

A singular subject takes a singular verb (the box sits); a plural subject takes a plural verb (the boxes sit). The ACT hides the subject behind extra words so the wrong verb sounds right.

Cross out “of” phrases

Phrases starting with of, in, on, for, with are never the subject. Cross them out and read what’s left.

The box of photos sit.
The box of photos sits.

Always singular

Each, every, everyone, everybody, either, neither are singular, even when a plural word follows.

Each of the students needs a card.

Or / nor: match the nearer one

With either … or and neither … nor, the verb matches the subject closer to it.

Neither the coach nor the players were ready.

“There” is never the subject

In “There is / There are” sentences, the subject comes after the verb. Flip the sentence to check.

There are three reasons. (Three reasons are…)
Agreement · Find the real subjectQ7
The box of old photographs sit in the attic.
Not yet — one more try
Cross out “of old photographs.” What is left as the subject?

Cross out the “of” phrase: “The box … sit.” The subject is box, which is singular, so the verb is sits.

The trap: “photographs” sits right next to the verb and sounds plural, but it isn’t the subject.

Agreement · Neither / norQ8
Neither the coach nor the players was ready for the storm delay.
Not yet — one more try
With “neither … nor,” the verb matches whichever subject is closer to it.

With either/or and neither/nor, the verb agrees with the nearer subject. “Players” is closer, and it’s plural, so were.

Solve it another way
Check it flip the order: “Neither the players nor the coach was ready.” Now “coach” is closer, so the verb is singular.
Agreement · Subject after the verbQ9
There is three reasons the plan failed.
Not yet — one more try
In a “There is / There are” sentence, the subject comes after the verb. What is it here?

“There” is never the subject. The real subject is reasons, which is plural, so are. Flip it to check: “Three reasons are …”

Agreement · Find the real subjectQ10
The list of supplies for the camping trip are taped to the fridge.
Not yet — one more try
Cross out both prepositional phrases: “of supplies” and “for the camping trip.”

Strip the phrases: “The list … is taped to the fridge.” List is singular, so is.

The trap: two plural-sounding words, “supplies” and “trip,” come between the subject and the verb. The ACT stacks phrases like this on purpose.

Agreement · Each, every, everyoneQ11
Each of the students needs a library card.
Not yet — one more try
What is the subject: “each” or “students”?

The subject is each, which is always singular. “Each needs” is correct, so NO CHANGE.

Remember: NO CHANGE is a real answer. Test it with the same rule as every other choice.

02 Pronouns

Right pronoun, right job

A pronoun stands in for a noun. It has to match that noun in number (one or more than one) and do the right job in the sentence.

Say the contraction

Read it’s as “it is,” they’re as “they are,” who’s as “who is.” If that doesn’t fit, you need the version with no apostrophe.

The company changed it’s logo.
The company changed its logo.

Match the noun

Find the noun the pronoun refers to. One thing: it, its. More than one: they, their.

The two bakeries lowered their prices.

Me or I

Drop the other person and listen. You’d never say “gave it to I.”

to Jada and I
to Jada and me

Who or whom

Answer the question with he or him. He → who. Him → whom. (Both end in m.) Use which only for things.

Pronouns · Its / it’sQ12
The company changed it’s logo last year.
Not yet — one more try
Read “it’s” as “it is”: “The company changed it is logo.” Does that work?

It’s means “it is” or “it has.” To show something belongs to it, use its, with no apostrophe. A company is one thing, so “their” doesn’t fit.

Pronouns · Their / there / they’reQ13
The twins said their planning to visit in June.
Not yet — one more try
Try “they are” in the blank: “The twins said they are planning …”

The sentence needs “they are,” and the contraction is they’re. “Their” shows belonging; “there” is a place.

Pronouns · AgreementQ14
The two bakeries on Oak Street lowered its prices.
Not yet — one more try
Find the noun the pronoun refers to. Is it one thing or more than one?

The pronoun refers to bakeries, which is plural, so it needs a plural pronoun: their prices.

Pronouns · Me or IQ15
Our coach gave the awards to Jada and I.
Not yet — one more try
Drop “Jada and.” Would you say “gave the awards to I”?

Take out the other person: “gave the awards to me.” After a word like “to,” use me, not “I.”

The trap: “myself” only works when you already appear as the subject: “I gave myself a break.”

Pronouns · Who / whomQ16
The volunteer whom organized the food drive was thanked at the assembly.
Not yet — one more try
Answer the question “who organized it?” with “he” or “him.”

He organized the drive, not “him.” If “he” fits, use who; if “him” fits, use whom. “Which” is for things, not people.

03 Verbs

Keep time straight

Verb tense tells the reader when something happened. The ACT’s rule is simple: stay in the tense the passage is using unless the time really changes.

Stay steady

Look at the other verbs around the underline. If they’re past, this one is past too.

Last summer he worked and repairs boats.
Last summer he worked and repaired boats.

Had + past form

When two things happened in the past, use had for the one that happened first.

The crew had finished before the rain started.

Never “would of”

It’s would have, could have, should have. “Of” is never part of a verb.

Irregular forms

After has, have or had, use the third form: freeze, froze, has frozen; run, ran, had run; come, came, had come.

Verbs · Keep tense steadyQ17
Last summer, Andre worked at a marina, where he repairs boats and sold bait.
Not yet — one more try
Look at the other verbs in the sentence. When did all of this happen?

“Last summer,” “worked” and “sold” all point to the past, so the verb stays in the past: repaired.

Verbs · Had + past participleQ18
By the time the rain started, the crew finish the roof.
Not yet — one more try
Two past actions. Which one happened first?

The roof was finished before the rain started. For the earlier of two past actions, use had + the past form: had finished.

Verbs · Would haveQ19
If I had known about the sale, I would of bought more paint.
Not yet — one more try
“Would of” is never correct. What does it sound like it should be?

“Would of” is a spelling of how “would’ve” sounds. The correct form is would have, and the past form of “buy” is bought.

Verbs · Irregular formsQ20
The pond has froze over twice this winter.
Not yet — one more try
After “has,” you need the form you’d use in “it has ___.”

After has, have or had, use the past participle. For “freeze,” that’s frozen: freeze, froze, has frozen.

04 Punctuation

The punctuation rulebook

Last week you learned how to join two sentences. This week adds the rest of what the ACT tests: commas, dashes, colons and apostrophes. Every mark needs a reason.

Extra information: pairs

Information you could lift out gets matching marks on both sides: two commas or two dashes, never one of each.

The lighthouse, built in 1888, still stands.

No lone comma

Never put a single comma between a subject and its verb, or between “such as” and its list.

The students who finished early, went home.

Colons

A colon comes only after a complete sentence. It introduces a list or an explanation.

We need three things: flour, sugar, and eggs.

Possessives

Decide singular or plural first, then add the apostrophe. One player’s; twelve players’.

When in doubt, leave it out. The ACT punishes extra commas more often than missing ones. If you can’t name the rule for a comma, it probably doesn’t belong.
Punctuation · Commas in pairsQ21
The lighthouse, which was built in 1888 still guides ships into the harbor.
Not yet — one more try
The extra information opens with a comma. What has to close it?

“which was built in 1888” is extra information. It opens with a comma, so it must close with a comma: 1888,.

Solve it another way
Check it lift out the extra part: “The lighthouse still guides ships into the harbor.” It still works, so the commas go around it.
Punctuation · Subject and verbQ22
The students who finished early, went to the library.
Not yet — one more try
Find the subject and the verb. Should a single mark ever separate them?

The subject is “the students who finished early,” and the verb is “went.” A single comma never separates a subject from its verb, so early went.

Why no commas here: “who finished early” tells you which students. It isn’t extra, so it doesn’t get commas.

Punctuation · ColonQ23
The recipe needs three things: flour, sugar, and eggs.
Not yet — one more try
Is “The recipe needs three things” a complete sentence on its own?

A colon comes after a complete sentence and introduces what follows. “The recipe needs three things” is complete, so the colon is right: NO CHANGE.

The traps: a colon never follows “such as,” a semicolon can’t introduce a list, and no punctuation runs the list into the sentence.

Punctuation · Plural possessiveQ24
All twelve players uniforms arrived a week late.
Not yet — one more try
How many players? Make the word plural first, then show ownership.

Twelve players, so start with the plural, players. It already ends in s, so add only an apostrophe: players’.

The rule: one player’s uniform; twelve players’ uniforms.

Punctuation · Dashes in pairsQ25
The trail—the steepest in the park, climbs 1,200 feet in two miles.
Not yet — one more try
The extra information opens with a dash. What has to close it?

Pairs match. If the extra information opens with a dash, it closes with a dash: park—climbs.

Punctuation · Unneeded commasQ26
The shop sells tools such as, hammers, drills, and saws.
Not yet — one more try
Does anything belong between “such as” and the first item?

Nothing goes between “such as” and its list. as hammers, is correct.

When in doubt: the ACT punishes extra commas more than missing ones. If you can’t name the rule for a comma, it probably doesn’t belong.

05 Passage Practice

All of it, the way the ACT shows it

On the real test, these rules come mixed together inside an essay. Read the short essay below; each numbered underline is one question. Name the rule before you pick.

Short essay · the underlined parts are numbered

¶1Every Friday in October, the parking lot behind the old train depot [1] turn into a night market. Farmers, bakers, and a man who sharpens knives set up [2] they’re tables before sunset.

¶2The market was started by Rosa Delgado, [3] who sold honey there for eleven years. At first, only six vendors [4] come; last year, more than forty did.

¶3Rosa says the best part isn’t the [5] food it’s the neighbors who finally learn each other’s names.

Passage · AgreementQ27
[1] … the parking lot behind the old train depot turn into a night market.
Not yet — one more try
Cross out “behind the old train depot.” What is the subject?

The subject is lot, which is singular: “the parking lot … turns.” The phrase in between is a trap.

Passage · PronounsQ28
[2] Farmers, bakers, and a man who sharpens knives set up they’re tables before sunset.
Not yet — one more try
Read “they’re” as “they are.” Does “set up they are tables” work?

The tables belong to the vendors, so use the possessive their. “They’re” means “they are,” and “there” is a place.

Passage · Who / whomQ29
[3] The market was started by Rosa Delgado, who sold honey there for eleven years.
Not yet — one more try
Who sold honey: “she” or “her”?

She sold honey, so who is correct: NO CHANGE. “Which” is for things, and “whom” would match “her.”

Passage · TenseQ30
[4] At first, only six vendors come; last year, more than forty did.
Not yet — one more try
“At first” and “did” both point to the past. Which choice matches?

The sentence is about the past (“At first,” “last year … did”), so the verb is came.

The trap: “had came” is never correct. After “had,” the form is “come”: had come.

Passage · Joining sentencesQ31
[5] Rosa says the best part isn’t the food it’s the neighbors who finally learn each other’s names.
Not yet — one more try
Cover the punctuation. Could each side stand alone as a sentence?

“Rosa says the best part isn’t the food” and “it’s the neighbors …” are both complete. Nothing joins them now (a run-on), and a comma alone makes a splice. A semicolon joins them.
