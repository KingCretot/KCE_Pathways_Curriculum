Part 1 · Agreement & verbs

Find the real subject, keep tense steady, and use the right verb form.

Agreement · Find the real subjectQ1
The flock of geese fly south every October.
Not yet — one more try
Cross out “of geese.” What is the subject?

The subject is flock, one group, so the verb is singular: flies.

Agreement · Either / orQ2
Either the parents or the principal have the final say.
Not yet — one more try
Which subject is closer to the verb?

With “either … or,” match the nearer subject. Principal is closer and singular, so has.

Agreement · Subject after the verbQ3
Along the fence grows rows of sunflowers.
Not yet — one more try
Flip the sentence around: “Rows of sunflowers ___ along the fence.”

Flipped: “Rows of sunflowers grow along the fence.” The subject is rows, which is plural.

Agreement · EveryoneQ4
Everyone in the choir knows the words by heart.
Not yet — one more try
Is “everyone” singular or plural?

Everyone is singular, like each and every. “Everyone knows” is correct, so NO CHANGE.

Verbs · Keep tense steadyQ5
Yesterday, the storm knocked out power, and the whole street stays dark until midnight.
Not yet — one more try
“Yesterday” and “knocked” set the time. Which choice matches?

The sentence is in the past, so the verb stays in the past: stayed.

Verbs · Irregular formsQ6
The runners had ran five miles before sunrise.
Not yet — one more try
After “had,” use the form from “they have ___.”

After had, use the past participle. For “run,” it’s run: run, ran, had run.

Verbs · Would haveQ7
If Carlos had studied the map, he wouldn’t of gotten lost.
Not yet — one more try
“Of” is never part of a verb. What is “would of” trying to say?

“Would of” should be would have. The sentence imagines a past that didn’t happen, so wouldn’t have gotten.

Part 2 · Pronouns

Its and it’s, their and they’re, me and I, who and whose.

Pronouns · Its / it’sQ8
The museum reopened it’s doors after the repairs.
Not yet — one more try
Read it as “it is”: “reopened it is doors.”

The doors belong to the museum, so use the possessive its, with no apostrophe.

Pronouns · Their / there / they’reQ9
The Garcias parked there truck by the barn.
Not yet — one more try
Whose truck is it?

The truck belongs to the Garcias, so use the possessive their. “There” is a place.

Pronouns · Me or IQ10
Between you and I, the answer is B.
Not yet — one more try
After a word like “between,” would you say “I” or “me”?

After words like between, to, for and with, use me: “between you and me.”

The trap: “between you and I” sounds formal, but it’s incorrect.

Pronouns · Whose / who’sQ11
The student who’s essay won the contest goes to our school.
Not yet — one more try
Read “who’s” as “who is”: “The student who is essay won.” Does that work?

Who’s means “who is.” The essay belongs to the student, so use the possessive whose.

Pronouns · AgreementQ12
The volunteers brought their own tools.
Not yet — one more try
Who owns the tools, and how many of them are there?

Volunteers is plural, and the tools belong to them, so their is correct: NO CHANGE.

Part 3 · Punctuation

Pairs, colons, possessives, and no extra commas.

Punctuation · Commas in pairsQ13
Our neighbor, a retired pilot still flies on weekends.
Not yet — one more try
The extra information opens with a comma. Where does it end?

“a retired pilot” is extra information. It opens with a comma, so it closes with one: pilot,.

Punctuation · Subject and verbQ14
The dog that lives next door, barks at every mail truck.
Not yet — one more try
Find the subject and the verb. Should anything separate them?

The subject is “the dog that lives next door,” and the verb is “barks.” No single comma between them: door barks.

Punctuation · ColonQ15
I need two things from the store, milk and bread.
Not yet — one more try
Is “I need two things from the store” a complete sentence? What mark introduces a list after one?

The first part is a complete sentence, and a list follows. A colon introduces it: store:.

Punctuation · Plural possessiveQ16
Both dog’s tails wagged when the mail carrier arrived.
Not yet — one more try
“Both” means more than one dog. Make it plural first.

Two dogs: plural first, dogs, then the apostrophe: dogs’ tails.

Punctuation · Dashes in pairsQ17
The recipe—my grandmother’s favorite, calls for three lemons.
Not yet — one more try
How does the extra information open? It has to close the same way.

It opens with a dash, so it closes with a dash: favorite—calls.

Punctuation · Joining sentencesQ18
It rained all morning, but the game went on anyway.
Not yet — one more try
Two complete sentences joined by a FANBOYS word. Where does the comma go?

Comma before the FANBOYS word, none after it: morning, but. That’s NO CHANGE.

Part 4 · Roots & review

This week’s roots, plus two Week 1 math skills to keep fresh.

Week 2 roots · sequQ19
The root sequ means “follow.” Consecutive days are days that:
Not yet — one more try
Think of the word “sequence.”

con (together) + sequ (follow): consecutive days follow one after another, like Monday, Tuesday, Wednesday.

Week 2 roots · cedQ20
A pronoun’s antecedent is the noun it refers to. Using the roots ante (before) and ced (go), the antecedent usually:
Not yet — one more try
Put the two roots together: “goes ___.”

ante (before) + ced (go) = goes before. The antecedent usually comes before the pronoun: “Rosa said she …”

Week 1 review · PercentQ21
A $60 jacket is 25% off. What is the sale price?
Not yet — one more try
25% off means you pay 75%. Multiply 60 by 0.75.

60 × 0.75 = $45.

The trap: $15 is the discount, not the price.

Week 1 review · AverageQ22
What is the average of 72, 85, and 92?
Not yet — one more try
Add the three numbers, then divide by 3.

72 + 85 + 92 = 249, and 249 ÷ 3 = 83.
