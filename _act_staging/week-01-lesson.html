Warm-up

Six quick questions to wake up the skills we’ll use today. Pick an answer. If it’s wrong, you’ll get a hint and one more try. Miss it twice, and the answer and its logic appear. Read them, because that’s how the skill sticks. No calculator for these.

Have ready: paper, two pencils, your own calculator for later in the lesson, and your phone put away. The ACT gives you no built-in calculator and no formula sheet, so practice with what you’ll bring on test day.
English · Joining sentencesQ1
My sister loves chemistry, she wants to become a pharmacist.
Not yet — one more try
Cover the punctuation. Could “My sister loves chemistry” stand alone as a sentence? Could “she wants to become a pharmacist”?

Both halves are complete sentences, so a comma alone can’t join them. That’s a comma splice. A semicolon can join them.

The traps: no punctuation at all makes a run-on, and “and,” with a comma after it puts a comma in the wrong place.

English · Say it onceQ2
The meeting was postponed until later at a future date.
Not yet — one more try
What does “postponed” already mean? Does the sentence need to say it again?

“Postponed” already means moved to a later time, so every other choice repeats the idea. Deleting the phrase leaves “The meeting was postponed.” It’s short, clear and correct.

Math · PercentQ3
What is 15% of 60?
Not yet — one more try
Change 15% to a decimal and multiply: 0.15 × 60. Or find 10% and 5% and add them.

“Percent” means “out of 100,” so 15% is 0.15. “Of” means multiply: 0.15 × 60 = 9.

Solve it another way
Another way: 10% of 60 is 6, and 5% is half of that, 3. Then 6 + 3 = 9.
Math · Linear equationsQ4
If 5x − 8 = 27, what is x?
Not yet — one more try
Undo the steps in reverse order: add 8 to both sides first, then divide by 5.

Add 8 to both sides: 5x = 35. Divide by 5: x = 7.

Solve it another way
Check it 5(7) − 8 = 35 − 8 = 27. ✓
Math · RatesQ5
A car travels 150 miles in 2.5 hours. What is its average speed, in miles per hour?
Not yet — one more try
Speed = distance ÷ time. Which number goes on top?

150 ÷ 2.5 = 60 miles per hour.

The trap: 375 comes from multiplying 150 × 2.5 instead of dividing.

Solve it another way
Check it 60 × 2.5 = 150. ✓
Math · ExpressionsQ6
Which expression is equivalent to (x + 3)²?
Not yet — one more try
Squaring means multiplying it by itself: (x + 3)(x + 3). Multiply every term by every term.

(x + 3)(x + 3) = x² + 3x + 3x + 9 = x² + 6x + 9.

The trap: x² + 9 leaves out the two middle terms. You can’t square each piece separately.

Solve it another way
Check it try x = 1. (1 + 3)² = 16, and 1 + 6 + 9 = 16. ✓
01 Orientation

The ACT, on one screen

Three required sections in a fixed order, each scored 1–36. Science and the essay are optional add-ons at the end. There’s no penalty for a wrong answer, so never leave a blank.

English · 50 Q / 35 min

Edit underlined parts of short essays. About 42 seconds a question, the fastest pace on the test.

Math · 45 Q / 50 min

Four answer choices, your own calculator, no formula sheet. About 67 seconds a question.

Reading · 36 Q / 40 min

Four passages of about nine questions each, in any order you choose. About 10 minutes per passage.

Science · optional · 40 Q / 40 min

Graphs, tables and experiments. Reported on its own; it does not change your composite.

Three rules of the road

Same test for everyone

The ACT doesn’t adapt. Every question is worth the same, so an easy one counts as much as a hard one.

Never leave a blank

A blank is a guaranteed zero; a guess is a 1-in-4 chance. Fill every bubble.

Letters alternate

Odd questions use A B C D, even use F G H J. It helps you catch a skipped line.

Some questions don’t count. A few in each section are trial questions for future tests. You can’t tell which ones, so treat every question as if it counts.
PacingQ7
ACT English gives you 50 questions in 35 minutes. About how much time is that per question?
Not yet — one more try
Change 35 minutes into seconds first, then split that time across 50 questions.
  1. 35 minutes × 60 = 2,100 seconds.
  2. 2,100 ÷ 50 = 42 seconds per question.

That's the fastest pace on the whole test, which is why English gets its own pacing plan.

Test formatQ8
Which statement about the ACT Science section is true?
Not yet — one more try
Think about which three sections make up the composite score.

Your composite comes from English, Math and Reading only. Science is optional and reported on its own. Whether to take it depends on the colleges you apply to, not on your composite.

Test strategyQ9
You have 1 minute left in Math and 4 questions you haven't read yet. What's the best move?
Not yet — one more try
Ask yourself: what does a wrong answer cost on the ACT?

A wrong answer costs nothing, but a blank is a guaranteed zero. With four choices, each guess has a 1-in-4 chance of being right. Four guesses usually earn you about one extra point. Pick one letter, fill in all four, and finish.

02 Scoring

How your score is built

Each section is scored 1–36. Your composite is the average of English, Math and Reading, rounded to the nearest whole number, with .5 rounding up.

Worked example. English 22, Math 18, Reading 21. Add: 22 + 18 + 21 = 61. Divide by 3: 20.33. Round: composite 20. A strong section can carry a weaker one. Your goal is to raise the total of the three.
ScoringQ10
A student scores English 24, Math 20, Reading 25. What is the composite?
Not yet — one more try
Add the three scores, divide by 3, then round.
  1. 24 + 20 + 25 = 69.
  2. 69 ÷ 3 = 23 exactly.

So the composite is 23. No rounding needed this time.

ScoringQ11
To earn a composite of 24, what is the smallest total the three section scores can add up to?
Not yet — one more try
The average gets rounded. Find the smallest total that, divided by 3, rounds to 24.
  1. 72 ÷ 3 = 24 exactly, but we can go lower.
  2. 71 ÷ 3 = 23.67, which rounds up to 24. ✓
  3. 70 ÷ 3 = 23.33, which rounds down to 23. ✗

So 71 is the smallest total. A student at 19 has sections adding up to about 57, so reaching 24 takes 14 more section points spread across three sections. For example: +5 English, +4 Math, +5 Reading.

03 English

Three rules that pay right away

ACT English shows you an essay with parts underlined. For each one, pick the best version, or keep it. The first choice is always NO CHANGE. English follows a short list of rules, which makes it the fastest section to improve.

Rule 1 · Joining sentences

A comma alone can’t join two complete sentences. Use a period, a semicolon, or a comma plus FANBOYS (for, and, nor, but, or, yet, so).

It rained, we stayed in.
It rained; we stayed in.
It rained, so we stayed in.

Rule 2 · Say it once

When two choices are both correct, the one that says it clearly in fewer words wins. Repeated ideas are wrong.

the annual yearly harvest
the annual harvest

Rule 3 · NO CHANGE is real

Sometimes the original is already right. Test it by the same rules as every other choice. Don’t avoid it, and don’t pick it just because you’re unsure.

The cover test

Cover the punctuation with a finger. Could each side stand alone as a sentence? If both can, a comma by itself can’t hold them together. Say “sentence / sentence” in your head every time.

Want to see Rule 1 again? SupertutorTV has a short, clear walkthrough of comma splices that explains complete sentences and the quickest fixes. Watch: Comma splice errors (SupertutorTV)
English · Joining sentencesQ12
The library opened a new study room, students can reserve it online.
Not yet — one more try
Cover the comma. Could the words on each side stand alone as full sentences? If so, what can't a comma do by itself?
  1. “The library opened a new study room” is a complete sentence.
  2. “students can reserve it online” is a complete sentence.
  3. Two complete sentences can be joined by a semicolon, so room; students works.

NO CHANGE is a comma splice. “room students” has no punctuation at all, so it's a run-on. “room, which students can reserve it” doesn't work either, because “which” already means the room, so “it” repeats it.

English · Say it onceQ13
Each spring, the town holds a festival that celebrates the annual yearly harvest.
Not yet — one more try
“Annual” and “yearly” mean the same thing. Which choice says it only once?

“Annual” already means “every year,” so adding “yearly” or “every year” repeats the idea. the annual harvest says it once and clearly. Every other choice repeats itself.

English · Joining sentencesQ14
Marcus finished his shift at the hospital, and then he drove straight to class.
Not yet — one more try
Check each choice against Rule 1. A comma needs a FANBOYS word to join two sentences. Is “then” one of them?
  1. Both halves are complete sentences, so they need a proper joint.
  2. The original uses a comma plus “and,” which is one of the FANBOYS. That's correct.

“then” is not a FANBOYS word, so “, then he drove” is a comma splice, and “then he drove” with no comma is a run-on. A semicolon already joins the sentences, so “; and” doubles up. NO CHANGE was right all along.

English · Joining sentencesQ15
Dr. Alvarez studies coral reefs, her research takes her to the Florida Keys every summer.
Not yet — one more try
Two complete sentences meet at the comma. What does a comma need with it to join them?

“Dr. Alvarez studies coral reefs” and “her research takes her to the Florida Keys” are both complete. Comma plus a FANBOYS word works: reefs, and her. The comma goes before “and,” not after it. The original is a comma splice, and “reefs her” is a run-on.

English · Say it onceQ16
At this point in time, the team is currently reviewing the proposal.
Not yet — one more try
“At this point in time,” “at this time” and “currently” all mean “now.” How many times should the sentence say “now”?

Once. The team is currently says “now” one time and reads cleanly. The other choices say it two times over.

04 Math

Two moves that beat four choices

ACT Math gives four answer choices, and the right one is always sitting there. That means you can often work backward instead of solving from scratch.

Your toolbox

Backsolve

Plug the choices into the problem. Start with a middle one. Too big? Go smaller. Too small? Go bigger.

Pick numbers

When the question uses letters (“if n is odd…”), choose an easy number such as n = 3 and test each choice.

Percent, one step

Up 25% is × 1.25; down 25% is × 0.75. Do the discount first, then add the tax to the sale price. Write each step down.

Worked example. If 3(x − 4) = 2x + 5, what is x?  (A) 9  (B) 13  (C) 17  (D) 21. Try 13: 3(9) = 27, but 2(13) + 5 = 31. No match. Try 17: 3(13) = 39 and 2(17) + 5 = 39. They match, so x = 17. The algebra gives the same: 3x − 12 = 2x + 5, so x = 17. Use whichever is faster for you.
Math · PercentQ17
A number increased by 40% is 84. What is the number?
Not yet — one more try
Increasing by 40% means multiplying by 1.4. Test a middle choice: which one times 1.4 gives 84?

Backsolve: 60 × 1.4 = 84. ✓

The trap: taking 40% off 84 gives 50.4, close to choice 50. But the question starts with the unknown number and adds 40% to it.

Solve it another way
Another way: call the number n. Then 1.4n = 84, so n = 84 ÷ 1.4 = 60.
Math · BacksolvingQ18
Adult tickets cost $12 and student tickets cost $7. A group bought 10 tickets for $95. How many were adult tickets?
Not yet — one more try
Backsolve: pick a number of adult tickets. The rest of the 10 are student tickets. Does the total come to $95?

Try 5 adults: 5 × $12 = $60. That leaves 5 students: 5 × $7 = $35. Total: $60 + $35 = $95. ✓

Solve it another way
Another way: call the adult tickets a. Then the student tickets are 10 − a, and 12a + 7(10 − a) = 95. That simplifies to 5a + 70 = 95, so a = 5.
Math · PercentQ19
An $80 jacket is on sale for 25% off. A 6% sales tax is then added to the sale price. What is the total cost?
Not yet — one more try
Go in order: first take off the discount (× 0.75), then add the tax to that sale price (× 1.06).
  1. Discount: $80 × 0.75 = $60.00.
  2. Tax: $60.00 × 1.06 = $63.60.

The traps: $60.00 forgets the tax. $64.80 comes from combining the percents (25% off and 6% on, as 19% off, then 80 × 0.81). You can't combine them, because the tax is taken on the sale price, not the original price.

Math · Pick numbersQ20
If n is an odd integer, which of the following must be even?
Not yet — one more try
Pick an odd number, like n = 3, and work out each choice.

With n = 3: 2(3) + 1 = 7 (odd), 3 + 2 = 5 (odd), 3 × 3 = 9 (odd), 3 + 1 = 4 (even).

The rule behind it: odd + 1 is always even.

Math · RatesQ21
A printer prints 18 pages per minute. How many minutes will it take to print 405 pages?
Not yet — one more try
Minutes = total pages ÷ pages per minute.

Minutes = total pages ÷ pages per minute. So 405 ÷ 18 = 22.5 minutes. The printer finishes 18 pages every minute, and 405 pages take 22 full minutes plus half of one more.

Solve it another way
Check it 18 × 22.5 = 405. ✓ Checking with multiplication takes 5 seconds and catches calculator slips.
Math · ProportionsQ22
If x/4 = 9/12, what is x?
Not yet — one more try
Simplify 9/12 first. What is it equal to, with a 4 on the bottom?

9/12 simplifies to 3/4. So x/4 = 3/4, which means x = 3.

Solve it another way
Another way: cross-multiply. 12x = 36, so x = 3.
05 Reading

Prove it from the passage

Four passages, about nine questions each, 40 minutes, so about 10 minutes per passage. You can do the passages in any order, so start with the type you’re strongest at.

Too extreme

“Always,” “never,” “completely” when the passage is more careful.

True but off-target

It’s in the passage, but it doesn’t answer this question.

Twisted

Uses the passage’s own words but changes what they mean.

The fix for all three

Before you click, point to the line that proves your answer. No line, no pick.

Literary narrative · read it once, then answer

¶1Nia's grandfather taught driving the way he fixed engines: slowly, and with more listening than talking. On the first Saturday he handed her the keys to his pickup, he said nothing for three full miles of State Road 70. When she finally asked whether she was doing it right, he only tapped the dashboard. “Truck will tell you,” he said.

¶2At first Nia thought he was being difficult. But by the second week she had started to hear what he meant. The engine climbed in pitch when she waited too long to shift, and the steering wheel shivered when her tires drifted onto the rough edge of the road. The truck, it turned out, was talking constantly. She had simply never been quiet enough to notice.

¶3By the end of the month, her grandfather had stopped riding along for the short trip to the feed store. Nia missed the silence more than she expected. Driving alone, she caught herself tapping the dashboard twice, lightly, the way he did, before she pulled onto the highway.

Reading · Main ideaQ23
The passage mainly focuses on:
Not yet — one more try
A main-idea answer has to fit all three paragraphs, not just one.

¶1: he teaches by listening. ¶2: she learns to notice what the truck is “saying.” ¶3: she carries his habit with her. The thread through all three is learning to pay attention.

The grandfather gives no rules (¶1: “said nothing”), so the first choice is wrong. The truck's sounds are normal signals, not problems. Her frustration shows up only “at first” in ¶2, so it's too narrow for the main idea.

Reading · DetailQ24
According to the passage, the steering wheel “shivered” when:
Not yet — one more try
Go back to ¶2 and find the word “shivered.” Read that exact sentence.

¶2 says “the steering wheel shivered when her tires drifted onto the rough edge of the road.” That's a direct match.

Waiting too long to shift made the engine climb in pitch, a different signal from the same sentence. This is the “true but off-target” trap.

Reading · Meaning in contextQ25
The grandfather's words “Truck will tell you” (¶1) mean that Nia should:
Not yet — one more try
Read ¶2. What did Nia start noticing once she understood him?

¶2 explains his meaning. She began to hear the engine's pitch and feel the steering wheel shiver: “The truck… was talking constantly.” So he means she should learn from its sounds and movements.

The passage never mentions warning lights, which makes the second choice a twisted trap. Nothing suggests asking someone else, and he isn't telling her to stop caring; he's telling her where to look for the answer.

Reading · InferenceQ26
The final paragraph suggests that Nia:
Not yet — one more try
Look closely at how the last sentence describes her tapping the dashboard.

She taps the dashboard “twice, lightly, the way he did.” Those last four words show she has taken on his habit.

She “missed the silence,” so she clearly still thinks of him. Nothing says she prefers company. Nervousness is a guess the passage doesn't support; the tap is described as his habit, not as fear.

06 Pace Practice

Think in 42-second chunks

On test day you get about 42 seconds per English question. These six are here to build a steady rhythm: read the underlined part, run the rule, pick, and move on. Don’t stop to second-guess. Two of them preview rules from next week, so trust your instincts and then read the logic.

Where the clock comes in. You’ll practice with a real timer on the full-length practice exams. For now, focus on doing each question the same way every time. Speed comes from having a routine.
English · Joining sentencesQ27
The bakery on Oak Street sells out of bread by noon, customers line up before it opens.
Not yet — one more try
Cover the comma: are both sides complete sentences?

Both sides are complete sentences, so a semicolon joins them: noon; customers. The original is a comma splice. “noon customers” is a run-on. “, so,” adds an extra comma after “so”; the fix is “, so customers” with no comma after “so.”

English · Say it onceQ28
In my opinion, I think that the new schedule works better.
Not yet — one more try
“In my opinion” and “I think” mean the same thing.

Say it once: I think the new schedule works better. Every other choice says the same idea twice.

English · Subject–verb agreementQ29
The coach, along with her assistants, are reviewing game film tonight.
Not yet — one more try
Cross out the words between the commas. Who is doing the reviewing?
  1. Cross out “along with her assistants.” It's extra information set off by commas.
  2. What's left: “The coach ___ reviewing game film tonight.”
  3. “Coach” is one person, so the verb is is.

“are,” “were” and “have been” are all plural forms. Subject–verb agreement gets its own full lesson next week.

English · Joining sentencesQ30
Because the storm knocked out power, classes were canceled for two days.
Not yet — one more try
Cover the comma. Is “Because the storm knocked out power” a complete sentence?

“Because the storm knocked out power” is not a complete sentence; “because” makes it depend on the rest. When a part like that comes first, a comma separates it from the main sentence, so NO CHANGE is correct.

A semicolon or a period would leave “Because the storm knocked out power” standing alone as a fragment. With no comma, the two parts run together.

English · Word choiceQ31
The students turned in there projects on Friday.
Not yet — one more try
Which word shows that the projects belong to the students?

their shows ownership: their projects. “there” is a place (over there), “they're” means “they are,” and “theirs” stands alone without a noun after it (“the projects are theirs”).

English · Joining sentencesQ32
She returned the book to the library it was two weeks overdue.
Not yet — one more try
Two complete sentences are touching here. Which choice gives them a proper joint?

“She returned the book to the library” and “it was two weeks overdue” are both complete. A semicolon joins them: library; it. The original is a run-on, and a comma alone makes a comma splice. The last choice adds a comma after “it” that doesn't belong there.
