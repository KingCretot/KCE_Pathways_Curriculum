Unit 3: The Power of Poetry · Week 12 · ELA · Teacher Plan

Sound and Structure in Poetry

Lesson length: 60 minutes live, plus the after-class interactive lesson

Objectives

  • Identify rhyme, rhyme scheme, rhythm, repetition, alliteration, assonance and onomatopoeia.
  • Explain how a poem's sound imitates or supports its meaning.
  • Describe poetic forms: stanzas, couplets, refrains, the sonnet, free verse and haiku, and how form shapes meaning.
  • Compare how poets from Scotland, England, India and Japan use sound and structure.
  • Use the roots son, phon, poe and metr, and write a poem whose rhythm imitates a motion.

Standards

  • ELA.6.R.3.1: Explain how figurative language contributes to tone and meaning in text(s).
  • ELA.6.R.1.4: Describe the impact of various poetic forms on meaning and style.
  • ELA.6.V.1.2: Apply knowledge of Greek and Latin roots and affixes to determine meanings of words and phrases in grade-level content.
  • ELA.6.C.1.2: Write personal or fictional narratives using narrative techniques, precise words and phrases, and figurative language. (Applied to a short poem that tells a moment.)
  • ELA.K12.EE.1.1: Cite evidence to explain and justify reasoning.

Materials

  • The interactive lesson
  • A table, drum, or pot and wooden spoon
  • Space to walk
  • A phone to record (optional)
  • Printable practice page

Vocabulary

  • rhyme scheme: The pattern of end rhymes in a poem, marked with letters (aabb, abab).
  • rhythm: The beat of a poem, created by stressed and unstressed syllables.
  • stanza: A group of lines in a poem, like a paragraph in prose.
  • couplet: Two lines in a row that rhyme.
  • refrain: A line or phrase repeated at regular places in a poem or song.
  • alliteration: Repeating the same beginning consonant sound in nearby words.
  • onomatopoeia: A word that imitates the sound it names, such as buzz.
  • roots son, phon, poe, metr: son = sound (sonic); phon = sound (symphony); poe = make (poem, onomatopoeia: "name-making"); metr = measure (meter).

Key questions to ask

  • What do you hear?
  • How does the sound match the meaning?
  • What is repeated, and why?
  • How is the poem built?

Common mistakes to watch for

  • All poems must rhyme.
  • Rhythm means rhyme.
  • Haiku in English must always be 5-7-5.
  • Repetition is lazy writing (it creates emphasis and music).

Support (if the student is struggling)

  • Read every poem aloud twice, once slowly and once at natural speed.
  • Provide a rhyme scheme template.
  • Clap syllables of a line before marking stresses.

Challenge (if the student is ready for more)

  • Read Edgar Allan Poe's "The Bells" and find four kinds of onomatopoeia.
  • Translate a short poem from another language your family speaks and explain one choice you made between sound and meaning.

Background for the teacher

Stevenson's "From a Railway Carriage" appeared in A Child's Garden of Verses (1885). Sarojini Naidu's "Palanquin Bearers" appeared in The Golden Threshold (1905); Naidu (1879 to 1949) was president of the Indian National Congress (1925) and became Governor of the United Provinces in 1947, the first woman governor in India. Bashō's frog haiku dates to 1686 and Issa's fly haiku to 1821; the translations here are KingCretot Experience's own. All originals are in the public domain. ELA.6.R.1.4 names the sonnet and the villanelle as its forms; the sonnet is taught here and the villanelle in Week 14. Shakespeare’s Sonnet 18 was published in 1609.

Answer key: interactive lesson

Reconnect

  1. Name the figurative language.
    Answer: implied; personification
    Sandburg shows the fog as a cat without saying so (implied metaphor); trees cannot bow their heads (personification).
  2. What is tone? Give the tone of Dunbar's "Sympathy" in one or two words.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Tone: the speaker's attitude toward the subject; Sorrowful, pleading, pained (or similar)
    Model answer: Tone is the speaker's attitude toward the subject. "Sympathy" has a sorrowful, pleading tone.
  3. Dunbar repeats the line "I know" in every stanza of "Sympathy." What effect does that repetition have?
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Emphasizes that the speaker shares and understands the bird's pain; builds feeling; ties the stanzas together
    Model answer: The repetition keeps reminding us that the speaker truly understands the bird's pain from experience, and it makes the feeling grow stronger with each stanza.
  4. Which root means "feeling," as in sympathy and empathy?
    Answer: path
    path = feeling. Today's roots are about sound.

Learn 1: The Sound Toolkit

  1. Name the sound device.
    Answer: alliteration; onomatopoeia; assonance
    Repeated p sounds (alliteration); sizzled and popped imitate sounds (onomatopoeia); repeated long o sound (assonance).
  2. Why do you think rhyme, rhythm and repetition were so important when poems were mostly spoken or sung, not written?
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: They help people memorize and pass poems on; They make poems pleasant and powerful to hear
    Model answer: Before most people could read, poems had to be remembered. Rhyme, rhythm and repetition make words easier to memorize and pass on, and they make a poem enjoyable to hear.
  3. Which root, meaning "sound," appears in symphony, phonics and telephone?
    Answer: phon
    phon = sound. Telephone: tele (far) + phon (sound).

Learn 2: Rhythm and Rhyme That Move: Stevenson

  1. What is the rhyme scheme of lines 9 to 12 (the start of stanza 2) of "From a Railway Carriage"? Type the four letters with no spaces.
    Answer: aabb
    scrambles/brambles (a), gazes/daisies (b).
  2. Explain how the rhythm and the long list of sights help the poem feel like a fast train ride.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: The galloping beat imitates wheels clattering on the rails; The rapid list of sights, each seen for an instant, mimics scenery flashing past; Uses evidence from the poem
    Model answer: The beat, FAS-ter than FAIR-ies, sounds like wheels clattering on the tracks. The poem also rushes through a list of sights, bridges, hedges, a child, a cart, without stopping on any of them, so we see each one for only a moment, just like looking out the window of a speeding train.
  3. Find one example of onomatopoeia or alliteration in the poem, and explain what it adds.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: A real example (whistle; faster/fairies; hedges/houses; clambers and scrambles); Explains its effect (sound of the train, speed, liveliness)
    Model answer: "Painted stations whistle by" uses onomatopoeia: whistle sounds like the rush of air as the train passes, so we can hear the speed.

Learn 3: Repetition and Refrain: Naidu

  1. Read "Palanquin Bearers" aloud. How does its rhythm imitate the motion of carrying a palanquin?
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: A steady, swaying beat like walking in step; The repeated rocking pattern (LIGHT-ly, O LIGHT-ly) mimics the palanquin swaying
    Model answer: The rhythm has a steady, rocking beat, LIGHT-ly, O LIGHT-ly we BEAR her a-LONG, like the bearers walking in step and the palanquin swaying gently on their shoulders.
  2. Which line is repeated at the end of both stanzas as a refrain? Type the last six words.
    Answer: like a pearl on a string
    "We bear her along like a pearl on a string."
  3. The bearers say they carry the bride "like a pearl on a string." What does that simile tell us about how they feel about her and their task?
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: She is precious, valuable and delicate; They carry her carefully, proudly and smoothly
    Model answer: A pearl is precious and delicate, so the simile shows the bearers treat the bride as something valuable and carry her with great care and pride.
  4. Both Stevenson's and Naidu's poems use rhythm to imitate motion. How are the two motions and their rhythms different?
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Stevenson: fast, rushing, clattering motion of a train; Naidu: gentle, swaying, steady motion of walking bearers; Rhythm speed and feel differ to match
    Model answer: Stevenson's poem rushes with a quick, clattering beat to match a speeding train. Naidu's poem sways with a slower, gentle, steady beat to match men walking in step while carrying a bride.

Learn 4: A Fixed Form: The Sonnet

  1. What is the rhyme scheme of the first quatrain (lines 1 to 4) of Sonnet 18? Type the four letters with no spaces.
    Answer: abab
    day/May (a), temperate/date (b).
  2. Where does the sonnet turn, and what changes at that point?
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Line 9, at "But"; From summer's flaws and how beauty fades to the beloved's eternal beauty that will not fade
    Model answer: It turns at line 9 with "But." Before that, the speaker shows how summer and all beauty fade; after it, he says the person he loves will never fade.
  3. What does the final couplet claim, and how does the sonnet's form help it end with force?
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: As long as people live and read, the poem lives and keeps the beloved alive; The rhyming couplet comes after three quatrains, closing the argument like a final punch line
    Model answer: It claims that as long as people breathe and read, this poem will live and keep the beloved's beauty alive. After twelve lines of building, the rhyming couplet snaps the argument shut like a final punch line.

Learn 5: Structure: Stanzas, Free Verse and Haiku

  1. Bashō's haiku has only three short lines. How does the sound of the frog's splash change the feeling of the old pond?
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: The sudden sound breaks the silence; The contrast makes the stillness feel deeper or the moment more vivid
    Model answer: The pond is old and quiet, and the sudden splash breaks the silence for just a moment. That contrast makes us notice how still and peaceful the pond is.
  2. Why do you think Issa repeats the word "wrings"? What does the fly seem to be doing?
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: The fly rubbing its legs looks like a person wringing hands, as if begging; Repetition makes us look closely and feel compassion
    Model answer: Flies rub their legs together, which looks like wringing hands, as if the fly is begging not to be swatted. Repeating "wrings" makes us look closely and feel sorry for it.
  3. What is the name for poetry with no regular rhyme or meter?
    Answer: free verse
    Free verse, like Sandburg's "Fog."

Hands-On

  1. Write your final poem with a title, and mark its rhyme scheme with letters at the end of each line.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: 8 to 12 lines with a title; A rhythm that imitates the chosen motion; A rhyme scheme marked correctly; A refrain, alliteration and onomatopoeia
    Model answer: Swing Song Up to the sky and back to the ground, (a) Whoosh goes the wind with a whistling sound, (a) Toes touch the treetops, then down I fall, (b) Past the fence and the garden wall. (b) Up to the sky and back again, (c) Swinging and singing in sunshine or rain. (c) Up to the sky and back to the ground, (a) Whoosh goes the wind with a whistling sound. (a)
  2. Explain how your rhythm imitates your motion, and label your refrain, alliteration and onomatopoeia. How did your family react to the performance?
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Explains the link between rhythm and motion; Labels each required device; Reports audience reaction
    Model answer: The beat goes up and back like a swing: UP to the SKY and BACK to the GROUND. Refrain: "Up to the sky and back to the ground." Alliteration: "swinging and singing in sunshine." Onomatopoeia: "whoosh." My sister started swaying while I read it.

Think Deeper

  1. Choose "From a Railway Carriage" or "Palanquin Bearers." Write a paragraph explaining how the poem's sound and structure support its meaning. Discuss at least two devices with quotations.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Clear claim about how sound supports meaning; At least two devices (rhythm, rhyme, repetition, refrain, alliteration, assonance) with quotations; Explains each effect; Organized paragraph
    Model answer: In "Palanquin Bearers," Sarojini Naidu uses sound to make readers feel the gentle, proud work of carrying a bride. The rocking rhythm of "Lightly, O lightly we bear her along" imitates the bearers walking in step while the palanquin sways on their shoulders. The repetition of "Lightly, O lightly" and "Softly, O softly" sounds like a work song the bearers sing together to keep their steps even. Alliteration such as "gaily we glide" adds a smooth, flowing sound. Finally, the refrain "We bear her along like a pearl on a string" closes each stanza with the same image, reminding us how precious she is. Through its sounds, the poem lets us feel the motion and the care the bearers give.
  2. English translations of haiku usually cannot keep the 5-7-5 sound pattern. Is it more important for a translator to keep a poem's sound pattern or its meaning? Explain your view with an example from today.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: A clear position; Reasoning about trade-offs between sound and meaning; An example from Bashō, Issa or another poem
    Model answer: I think meaning matters more, because if a translation changes the image, readers lose the poem. In Bashō's haiku, the important part is the silence broken by the frog's splash. A translator could add extra words to make 5-7-5, but that might clutter the simple moment, so it is better to keep the meaning and the feeling.

Exit Check

  1. Mark the rhyme scheme: "The cat sat down (a) / upon the mat, / she wore a crown / and a feathered hat." Type the letters for all four lines.
    Answer: abab
    down/crown (a), mat/hat (b).
  2. In Sonnet 18, the final couplet rhymes. Type the last word of line 13 and the last word of line 14.
    Answer: see; thee
    see / thee: the gg couplet.
  3. What is the term for a word that imitates a sound, such as buzz or splash?
    Answer: onomatopoeia
    Onomatopoeia: Greek for "name-making."
  4. How does rhythm help a poem's meaning? Give one example from today.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Rhythm can imitate motion or feeling; Example: train clatter (Stevenson) or swaying bearers (Naidu)
    Model answer: Rhythm can imitate a motion. In Stevenson's poem, the quick beat sounds like train wheels on the track.

    Answer: The pattern of end rhymes, marked with letters; lines 1 and 2 rhyme (a), lines 3 and 4 rhyme (b).
