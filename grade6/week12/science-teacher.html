Unit 3: The Diversity of Life · Week 12 · Life Science · Teacher Plan

Classification and Scientific Names

Lesson length: 60 minutes live, plus the after-class interactive lesson

Objectives

  • Explain why scientists classify organisms and how classification groups organisms by shared traits.
  • Name the eight levels of classification in order.
  • Write scientific names correctly using binomial nomenclature.
  • Describe the three domains and six kingdoms and give an example from each kingdom.
  • Explain how classification changes with new evidence, and evaluate Linnaeus's legacy fairly.

Standards

  • SC.6.L.15.1: Analyze and describe how and why organisms are classified according to shared characteristics with emphasis on the Linnaean system combined with the concept of Domains.
  • SC.6.N.2.2: Explain that scientific knowledge is durable because it is open to change as new evidence or interpretations are encountered.
  • SC.6.N.2.3: Recognize that scientists who make contributions to scientific knowledge come from all kinds of backgrounds and possess varied talents, interests, and goals.
  • SC.6.L.14.1: Describe and identify patterns in the hierarchical organization of organisms from atoms to molecules to cells to tissues to organs to organ systems to organisms (reconnect).
  • NGSS MS-LS4-2 (preview): Apply scientific ideas to construct an explanation for the anatomical similarities and differences among modern organisms.

Materials

  • The interactive lesson
  • 15 to 20 buttons, pasta shapes, bolts or shells
  • Index cards
  • A paper bag, gloves and a heavy book for pressing leaves
  • Printable practice page

Vocabulary

  • classification: Sorting organisms into groups based on shared traits.
  • taxonomy: The science of naming, describing and classifying organisms.
  • species: A group of organisms that can breed with each other and produce offspring that can also reproduce.
  • genus: A group of closely related species (plural: genera).
  • binomial nomenclature: The two-name system for naming species: genus plus species, such as Homo sapiens.
  • domain: The broadest level of classification: Bacteria, Archaea or Eukarya.
  • kingdom: The level below domain, such as Plants, Animals or Fungi.

Key questions to ask

  • What traits do these organisms share?
  • Which level is more specific?
  • Why use a scientific name instead of a common name?
  • What evidence changed the classification?

Common mistakes to watch for

  • Organisms are classified by where they live (whales as fish).
  • Fungi are plants.
  • Scientific names are capitalized like titles (both words capitalized).
  • Human races are biological subspecies.
  • Classification never changes.

Support (if the student is struggling)

  • Provide a partly completed classification tree for the alligator.
  • Sort picture cards of organisms into kingdoms.

Challenge (if the student is ready for more)

  • Look up the full classification of the Florida manatee or the gopher tortoise.
  • Research a species named after a Black or Indigenous scientist or explorer and explain the honor.

Background for the teacher

About 2.1 million species have been described; total estimates vary widely. Linnaeus applied binomial names consistently in Species Plantarum (1753) for plants and the 10th edition of Systema Naturae (1758) for animals; in Systema Naturae he divided Homo sapiens into four continental varieties with prejudiced descriptions. Carl Woese identified Archaea in 1977 using ribosomal RNA, and with colleagues proposed the three-domain system in 1990. U.S. textbooks often use six kingdoms (Bacteria/Eubacteria, Archaea/Archaebacteria, Protista, Fungi, Plantae, Animalia); kingdom schemes vary internationally. Ynés Mexía (1870 to 1938) collected about 145,000 to 150,000 specimens; about 500 new species and roughly 50 species named for her are commonly cited. The Florida panther is Puma concolor (subspecies designation revised in recent taxonomy). Spanish moss (Tillandsia usneoides) is a bromeliad, a flowering plant.

Answer key: interactive lesson

Reconnect

  1. List at least five of the seven characteristics of living things.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Five or more of: made of cells, organized, use energy, grow and develop, respond, reproduce, homeostasis
    Model answer: Made of cells, organized, use energy, grow and develop, respond to stimuli, reproduce and maintain homeostasis.
  2. Week 11: put the levels of organization in order from smallest to largest.
    Answer: cell → tissue → organ → organ system → organism
    Today's levels of classification are different: they sort whole organisms into groups.
  3. Why are viruses usually not classified in any kingdom of living things? Use Week 11.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Not made of cells; cannot use energy or reproduce on their own, so most biologists do not call them living
    Model answer: Viruses are not made of cells and cannot use energy or reproduce on their own, so most biologists do not count them as living organisms.
  4. In the yeast test, yeast released a gas that filled the balloon. Which gas?
    Answer: carbon dioxide
    Carbon dioxide. Yeast is a fungus; today you will see which kingdom it belongs to.

Learn 1: Why and How We Classify

  1. Put the eight levels of classification in order from broadest to most specific.
    Answer: domain → kingdom → phylum → class → order → family → genus → species
    Dear King Philip Came Over For Good Soup.
  2. A lion (Panthera leo) and a tiger (Panthera tigris) are in the same genus. A lion and a housecat (Felis catus) are in the same family but different genera. Which two are most closely related, and how do you know?
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Lion and tiger; They share the most specific level (the same genus), so they share more traits and a more recent common ancestor
    Model answer: The lion and the tiger, because they are in the same genus, Panthera, which is more specific than family. Sharing a more specific group means they are more closely related.
  3. Compare classification to how a grocery store organizes food. Match at least three store levels to classification levels.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: A sensible analogy (store → department → aisle → shelf → item); Explains that each level gets more specific
    Model answer: The whole store is like a domain, the food department like a kingdom, the cereal aisle like a class, the oat cereals like a genus and one exact box of Oat Rings like a species. Each step gets more specific, with fewer items that are more alike.

Learn 2: Binomial Nomenclature

  1. Each scientific name is written wrong. Rewrite them correctly (describe the italics in words if you cannot type them) and explain the rules: (1) gopherus Polyphemus (2) Homo Sapiens (3) felis catus
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Gopherus polyphemus; Homo sapiens; Felis catus; Rules: genus capitalized, species lowercase, whole name in italics or underlined
    Model answer: Gopherus polyphemus, Homo sapiens and Felis catus, each in italics. The genus is always capitalized, the species name is always lowercase, and the whole name is italicized or underlined.
  2. In the name Quercus virginiana, which word is the genus?
    Answer: Quercus
    The capitalized first word is the genus.
  3. Using the panther and the gopher, give two reasons scientific names are better than common names for scientists.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: One organism can have many common names (panther, cougar, puma); One common name can mean different organisms (gopher); Scientific names are the same worldwide in every language
    Model answer: One animal can have many common names, like panther, cougar and puma, while one common name like gopher can mean two different animals. A scientific name like Puma concolor is used by scientists everywhere, in every language, so there is no confusion.

Learn 3: Domains and Kingdoms

  1. Name the kingdom for each organism.
    Answer: Fungi; Plants; Protists
    Yeast is a fungus; Spanish moss is a flowering plant (not a true moss); an amoeba is a single-celled protist.
  2. Explain why fungi are no longer classified as plants. Give two pieces of evidence.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Fungi cannot photosynthesize; they absorb food; Different cell wall material; DNA shows they are more closely related to animals
    Model answer: Fungi cannot make their own food by photosynthesis; they absorb it. Their cell walls are made of a different material, and DNA evidence shows they are more closely related to animals than to plants.
  3. Which domain includes all organisms whose cells have a nucleus?
    Answer: Eukarya
    Eukarya (eu = true, karyon = nucleus).

Learn 4: People Behind the Names

  1. How should we judge Linnaeus? Explain one way his work helped science and one way it caused harm, and say how scientists today have corrected the harm.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Helped: binomial nomenclature and an organized system still used today; Harm: ranked human "varieties" with prejudiced traits, used to justify racism and slavery; Correction: genetics shows humans are one species with more variation within groups than between; race is not a biological category
    Model answer: Linnaeus helped science by creating the two-name system and an organized way to classify life that we still use. But he also ranked humans into "varieties" with prejudiced traits, which was used to justify racism and slavery. Today genetics shows that all humans are one species, with more variation within groups than between them, so scientists reject those ideas.
  2. What does Ynés Mexía's story show about who can contribute to science?
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: People of any background and age can make major contributions; she began in her fifties and discovered about 500 new species
    Model answer: It shows that people of any background and age can make major discoveries. She started studying plants in her fifties and still found about 500 species new to science.
  3. Ynés Mexía collected about 145,000 specimens and found about 500 new species. About how many specimens did she collect for each new species? (Use Week 11 unit rates.)
    Answer: 290 specimens per new species
    145,000 ÷ 500 = 290.

Hands-On

  1. Draw or describe your classification system: the trait used at each level and the groups it created, down to at least four levels.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: At least four levels; A clear trait at each level; Every object ends in its own group
    Model answer: Level 1: metal or plastic buttons. Level 2 (plastic): round or shaped. Level 3 (round): two holes or four holes. Level 4 (four holes): white or colored. Each final group has one kind of button.
  2. List three of your binomial names, explain what each part means, and check that you followed the writing rules.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Three names in binomial form; Genus capitalized, species lowercase, italic or underlined; Meanings explained
    Model answer: Buttonus quadriholus (four-hole button), Buttonus biholus (two-hole button) and Metallicus starrus (a star-shaped metal button). The genus groups similar buttons, and the species describes the exact kind. Genus capitalized, species lowercase, underlined.

Think Deeper

  1. Whales live in the ocean and are shaped like fish, but scientists classify them as mammals, not fish. Explain why, using what you know about how scientists classify organisms.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Classification is based on shared traits and relatedness, not just where something lives or its shape; Whales breathe air with lungs, are warm-blooded, give birth to live young and feed them milk, have hair at some stage (mammal traits); DNA and skeletons show whales are related to land mammals
    Model answer: Scientists classify organisms by shared traits and how closely they are related, not by where they live or their shape. Whales breathe air with lungs, are warm-blooded, give birth to live young and feed them milk, which are mammal traits. Their skeletons and DNA also show they are related to land mammals. Their fish-like shape is just an adaptation to life in water.
  2. Imagine you discover a new species of frog in a Florida spring. Explain what scientists would do to classify it and name it, and write a possible scientific name with a reason for each part.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Compare its traits and DNA with known frogs to place it in a genus and family; Write a species description and publish it; A properly written binomial name with reasons (describing a trait, place or person)
    Model answer: Scientists would compare its body, behavior and DNA with known frogs to find its closest relatives, which would tell them its family and genus. Then they would describe the new species and publish it so other scientists can check it. If it belonged to the genus Lithobates, I might name it Lithobates fontinalis, from the Latin for "of a spring," because it lives in a spring.

Exit Check

  1. Order these levels from broadest to most specific: genus, kingdom, family, species.
    Answer: kingdom → family → genus → species
    Kingdom, family, genus, species.
  2. Write the scientific name for humans correctly and state the two capitalization rules.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Homo sapiens (italic/underlined); Genus capitalized; species lowercase
    Model answer: Homo sapiens, in italics. The genus is capitalized and the species name is lowercase.
  3. Which kingdom includes mushrooms, molds and yeast?
    Answer: Fungi
    Fungi.
  4. Give one example of how classification changed because of new evidence.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Fungi moved out of plants; Archaea separated from Bacteria (Woese); other accurate example
    Model answer: Fungi used to be classified as plants, but evidence like DNA moved them into their own kingdom.

Answer key: printable page

  1. List the eight levels of classification from broadest to most specific.
    Answer: domain, kingdom, phylum, class, order, family, genus, species
  2. Write the scientific name of the American alligator correctly and label its parts.
    Answer: Alligator mississippiensis: genus Alligator, species mississippiensis (italic).
  3. Why do scientists use scientific names?
    Answer: One name per species worldwide; avoids confusion from many or shared common names.
  4. Name the three domains.
    Answer: Bacteria, Archaea, Eukarya
  5. Name the kingdom of a mushroom, an oak tree and an amoeba.
    Answer: Fungi, Plants, Protists
  6. Why are whales classified as mammals, not fish?
    Answer: They breathe air, are warm-blooded, give live birth and make milk; DNA links them to mammals.
