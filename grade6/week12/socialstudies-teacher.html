Unit 3: Ancient Asia · Week 12 · World and American History · Teacher Plan

The Indus Valley

Lesson length: 60 minutes live, plus the after-class interactive lesson

Objectives

  • Locate the Indus River, its major cities and the modern countries of the region.
  • Describe the planned cities of the Indus civilization: grid streets, baked bricks, drains and the Great Bath.
  • Explain how standardized weights, seals and trade connected the Indus Valley to Mesopotamia.
  • Explain why the Indus script has not been deciphered and what that means for historians.
  • Evaluate competing explanations for how the Indus cities were governed and why they declined.

Standards

  • SS.6.W.2.4: Compare the economic, political, social, and religious institutions of ancient river civilizations.
  • SS.6.W.2.3: Identify the characteristics of civilization.
  • SS.6.G.2.1: Explain how major physical characteristics, natural resources, climate, and absolute and relative locations have influenced settlement, interactions, and the economies of ancient civilizations of the world.
  • SS.6.G.2.6: Explain the concept of cultural diffusion, and identify the influences of different ancient cultures on one another.
  • SS.6.W.1.4: Describe the methods of historical inquiry and how history relates to the other social sciences.

Materials

  • The interactive lesson
  • A world map or globe
  • A baking pan, sand, straws or foil, small blocks, water, towels
  • Printable practice page

Vocabulary

  • Harappan civilization: Another name for the Indus Valley civilization, after Harappa, the first city excavated.
  • urban planning: Designing a city's layout, streets and services before or while it is built.
  • citadel: A raised, walled area of a city, often holding important buildings.
  • standardized: Made to the same size or rule everywhere.
  • seal: A small carved stone pressed into clay to mark ownership or trade goods.
  • decipher: To figure out the meaning of a writing system or code.
  • monsoon: Seasonal winds that bring heavy summer rains to South Asia.

Key questions to ask

  • What can objects tell us when we cannot read the writing?
  • What does planning reveal about a society?
  • What evidence supports each explanation?
  • What would change our minds?

Common mistakes to watch for

  • The Indus civilization was part of Mesopotamia or Egypt.
  • Invaders destroyed the Indus cities (little evidence).
  • Ancient people did not have plumbing or planning.
  • The Indus script has been deciphered.

Support (if the student is struggling)

  • Provide a labeled map with modern borders.
  • Use a three-column chart: Kemet, Mesopotamia, Indus.
  • Pre-build the drain model base.

Challenge (if the student is ready for more)

  • Research recent efforts to decipher the Indus script, including computer analysis, and evaluate one claim.
  • Compare Mohenjo-daro's drains with how your town handles stormwater today.

Background for the teacher

The Mature Harappan phase dates to about 2600 to 1900 BCE (broader Indus tradition about 3300 to 1300 BCE). Harappa was first excavated by Daya Ram Sahni (1921) and Mohenjo-daro by R. D. Banerji (1922); John Marshall announced the civilization in 1924. Over 1,000 sites are known; the area exceeds that of Egypt and Mesopotamia combined. Bricks follow roughly 1 : 2 : 4 proportions. The Great Bath measures about 12 by 7 m and 2.4 m deep, sealed with bitumen. Weights follow a binary series for smaller units, then decimal multiples. The script has roughly 400 to 600 signs; average inscription length is about five signs. Meluhha appears in Mesopotamian texts from the Akkadian period; Indus seals and carnelian beads are found in Mesopotamia. Decline after about 1900 BCE is now attributed mainly to climate change (weaker monsoons) and river shifts; the older Aryan invasion explanation (Mortimer Wheeler) is not supported by the archaeological evidence.

Answer key: interactive lesson

Reconnect

  1. Why did writing first develop in Mesopotamia?
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: To keep records of goods (grain, beer, sheep) for temples and cities
    Model answer: To keep records of goods like grain, beer and sheep that temples collected and gave out.
  2. What is the name of the wedge-shaped writing of Mesopotamia?
    Answer: cuneiform
    Cuneiform. Scholars can read it today. This week's script is still a mystery.
  3. Mesopotamia had plenty of mud and grain but lacked stone, timber and metal. How did Mesopotamians get those goods?
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Trade with distant lands, exchanging grain and cloth
    Model answer: They traded their grain and cloth for stone, timber and metal from faraway lands.
  4. In base 60, a scribe writes one 60 and then 2 tens and 3 ones. What number is it?
    Answer: 83
    60 + 23 = 83.

Learn 1: A River Civilization in South Asia

  1. How was the Indus River similar to the Nile and the Tigris and Euphrates? Give two similarities.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Flowed through a dry region; flooded each year; brought water and fertile silt for farming
    Model answer: Like the Nile and the Tigris and Euphrates, the Indus flowed through dry land and flooded every year, bringing water and fertile silt that let farmers grow crops.
  2. In which two modern countries are the main Indus Valley sites found?
    Answer: Pakistan and India
    Harappa and Mohenjo-daro are in Pakistan; Dholavira, Lothal and Rakhigarhi are in India.
  3. The Indus people may have been among the first to grow cotton. Why might that matter for trade and daily life?
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Cotton cloth for clothing in a hot climate; A valuable good to trade
    Model answer: Cotton makes light, cool cloth for a hot climate, and cotton cloth would have been a valuable good to trade with other peoples.

Learn 2: Planned Cities

  1. What do grid streets, standard bricks and city-wide drains suggest about how Indus society was organized? Explain your reasoning.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Someone or some group planned and coordinated building across cities; Shared rules or standards; organized labor; concern for cleanliness and public health
    Model answer: They suggest that someone, or some group, planned the cities and made rules that builders followed everywhere. Building drains for a whole city takes organized workers and shared standards, and it shows people cared about cleanliness and health.
  2. An Indus brick follows the ratio 1 : 2 : 4 (thickness : width : length). If a brick is 7 cm thick, how long is it?
    Answer: 28 cm
    Thickness 1 part = 7 cm, so length 4 parts = 28 cm. (You used ratios in Unit 2.)
  3. The Great Bath was waterproofed with layers of fitted bricks and natural tar. What does this suggest about the builders' knowledge and the importance of the Bath?
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Builders understood waterproofing and engineering; A lot of effort went into it, so it was important (possibly religious or civic use)
    Model answer: The builders understood how to keep water from leaking, which takes real engineering skill. Spending so much effort suggests the Bath was important, perhaps for religious ceremonies.

Learn 3: Seals, Weights and a Mystery Script

  1. Give two reasons the Indus script is so hard to decipher.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Inscriptions are very short (about five signs); No bilingual text (like the Rosetta Stone); unknown language
    Model answer: Most inscriptions have only about five signs, which is too little to find patterns, and no one has found a text that gives the same words in a known language, like the Rosetta Stone did for hieroglyphs.
  2. Continue the pattern of Indus weights.
    Answer: 32; 64
    Each weight is double the last.
  3. What evidence shows that the Indus Valley traded with Mesopotamia? Name two kinds.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Indus seals found in Mesopotamian cities (Ur); Mesopotamian texts mention Meluhha; carnelian beads
    Model answer: Indus seals have been found in Mesopotamian cities like Ur, and Mesopotamian writings mention trade with Meluhha, which scholars identify as the Indus region.

Learn 4: Questions Historians Still Debate

  1. What evidence makes historians unsure who ruled Indus cities? Give your own hypothesis and explain what evidence would test it.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Evidence: no clear palaces, royal tombs or king statues; few weapons; A reasonable hypothesis (councils, shared power, hidden rulers); Evidence that could test it (deciphered texts, palace remains, burial differences)
    Model answer: Archaeologists have not found clear palaces, royal tombs or statues of kings, and there are few weapons. My hypothesis is that groups of merchants ran the cities together. If the script were deciphered and it named councils instead of kings, that would support my idea.
  2. Compare the old "invasion" explanation of the decline with the climate explanation. Which has more support, and why?
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Invasion: no evidence of a great war or destruction; Climate: weaker monsoons and shifting or drying rivers; gradual abandonment fits; The climate explanation has more evidence
    Model answer: The invasion idea has little support, because archaeologists have found no signs of a great war. The climate explanation fits the evidence better: monsoon rains weakened and rivers like the Ghaggar-Hakra dried up or shifted, and the cities were abandoned slowly, not destroyed all at once.
  3. Return to the big question: list three things we know about the Indus people without reading their writing, and one thing we cannot know yet.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Three things known from archaeology (planning, drains, trade, weights, cotton, seals); One unknown (language, names, beliefs, rulers)
    Model answer: We know they planned their cities with grids and drains, traded with Mesopotamia and used standard weights. We do not yet know what language they spoke or what they called themselves.

Hands-On

  1. Describe what happened in Test 1 and Test 2. Which variables did you keep the same?
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Clear comparison of both tests; Controlled variables named (amount of water, slope, pouring speed)
    Model answer: In Test 1, the water spread across the street, washed sand away and pooled around the houses. In Test 2, most of the water ran into the drain and out the end, and the street stayed in shape. I kept the amount of water, the slope and the pouring speed the same.
  2. Why would covered drains have mattered so much in a crowded Indus city during the monsoon? Think about health, streets and homes.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Prevented flooding and damage to streets and homes; Kept waste water away from people, reducing disease; Covering kept drains from clogging and kept streets usable
    Model answer: Covered drains carried rain and waste water away so streets and homes did not flood or wash away. Keeping dirty water away from people helped prevent disease, and covering the drains kept trash out and let people walk on the streets.

Think Deeper

  1. You have now studied three river civilizations: Kemet, Mesopotamia and the Indus Valley. Write a paragraph comparing them on two features (for example, rivers, writing, cities, government or trade). Use specific evidence for each civilization.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Two clear features compared; Specific, accurate evidence for all three civilizations; Explains a similarity and a difference; Organized paragraph
    Model answer: Kemet, Mesopotamia and the Indus Valley all depended on rivers, but they organized their societies differently. All three grew along rivers that flooded and brought fertile silt, though the Nile was predictable while the Tigris and Euphrates were not, and the Indus floods came with the monsoon. Their governments also differed. Kemet was united under a powerful pharaoh who built pyramids, and Mesopotamian city-states had kings like Hammurabi who wrote laws. In the Indus Valley, however, archaeologists have found no royal palaces or tombs, so power may have been shared differently. These differences show that people solved similar problems in different ways.
  2. Harappa and Mohenjo-daro are in Pakistan, while Dholavira and Lothal are in India. Why might it matter that people in both countries see the Indus civilization as part of their heritage?
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Shared history connects people across a modern border; Thoughtful reasoning about heritage, identity, cooperation or preservation
    Model answer: Pakistan and India have often been in conflict, but the Indus civilization is older than both countries and belongs to the history of people on both sides of the border. Seeing it as shared heritage can remind people of their common past and encourage them to work together to protect these sites.

Exit Check

  1. Name one of the two largest and best-known Indus cities.
    Answer: Harappa or Mohenjo-daro
    Harappa and Mohenjo-daro.
  2. Name two features that show Indus cities were carefully planned.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Grid streets; standard bricks; covered drains; wells; the Great Bath
    Model answer: Grid streets and covered drains along the streets.
  3. Why can't historians read the Indus script?
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Short inscriptions; no bilingual text; unknown language
    Model answer: The inscriptions are very short, and no text has been found that gives the same words in a known language.
  4. What do most archaeologists now think caused the Indus cities to decline?
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Climate change: weaker monsoon rains; rivers shifting or drying up
    Model answer: Climate change weakened the monsoon rains and rivers shifted or dried up, so the cities were slowly abandoned.

Answer key: printable page

  1. Where was the Indus Valley civilization, and which modern countries are there today?
    Answer: Along the Indus River in South Asia; today Pakistan and northwest India.
  2. Describe two features of Indus city planning.
    Answer: Grid streets; standard baked bricks; covered drains; wells; the Great Bath.
  3. How do we know the Indus Valley traded with Mesopotamia?
    Answer: Indus seals found in Ur; Mesopotamian texts mention Meluhha; carnelian beads.
  4. Why hasn't the Indus script been deciphered?
    Answer: Very short inscriptions; no bilingual text; unknown language.
  5. What do historians debate about Indus government?
    Answer: No clear palaces, royal tombs or king statues, so they are unsure who ruled.
  6. Explain the leading theory for the decline of the Indus cities.
    Answer: Climate change weakened monsoons and rivers shifted or dried up; no evidence of a great invasion.
