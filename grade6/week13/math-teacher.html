Unit 3: Rates and Percents · Week 13 · Math · Teacher Plan

Percents in Real Life: Discounts, Markups and Tips

Lesson length: 60 minutes live, plus the after-class interactive lesson

Objectives

  • Find a sale price after a percent discount in two ways: by subtracting the discount and by paying the percent that is left.
  • Find an original price from a sale price and a discount percent.
  • Find a selling price from a cost and a markup percent, and find a markup percent from a cost and a price.
  • Calculate tips with mental-math shortcuts built from 10%.
  • Compare deals (percent off, dollars off, buy one get one) and explain which saves more and when.

Standards

  • MA.6.AR.3.4: Apply ratio relationships to solve mathematical and real-world problems involving percentages using the relationship between two quantities.
  • MA.6.AR.3.5: Solve mathematical and real-world problems involving ratios, rates and unit rates, including comparisons, mixtures, ratios of lengths and conversions within the same measurement system.
  • MA.K12.MTR.2.1: Demonstrate understanding by representing problems in multiple ways.
  • MA.K12.MTR.7.1: Apply mathematics to real-world contexts.
  • Financial literacy: discounts, markups, profit and tipping; comparing deals (Jump$tart Spending).

Materials

  • The interactive lesson
  • Five paper strips about 20 cm by 4 cm, a centimeter ruler, scissors, tape and markers
  • A store flyer or website with sale prices (with a parent)
  • A calculator for checking only
  • Printable practice page

Vocabulary

  • discount: An amount taken off a price, often written as a percent off.
  • sale price: The price after the discount is subtracted.
  • original price: The regular price before a discount.
  • cost: What a seller pays to make or buy an item.
  • markup: The amount a seller adds to the cost to set the selling price; markup percent is a percent of the cost.
  • profit: The money left after costs are paid: selling price minus cost.
  • tip (gratuity): Extra money given to a server or worker for good service, usually a percent of the bill.

Lesson flow

6 minReconnect
Run the Week 12 Reconnect questions.
5 minHook
Pose the two shoe stores. Let the student predict before calculating the $40 + $40 case.
12 minDiscounts
Build the tape diagram. Practice both methods; work one original-price problem backward. Discuss the stacked discount.
12 minMarkups
Walk through Imani's hanger. Find a markup percent. Contrast "percent of the cost" with "percent of the price."
9 minTips
Practice the 10%, 5%, 15% and 20% shortcuts mentally before writing.
10 minWhich deal is better?
Compare $10 off with 20% off. Show that discount-then-tax equals tax-then-discount.
6 minAssign
Fold the first percent strip together; assign the other strips, the markup strip, the tip race, Think Deeper and the Exit Check.

Key questions to ask

  • A percent of what?
  • Which method is faster: subtract the discount, or pay what is left?
  • Does the biggest number on a sign always save the most?
  • Why is 30% off and then 20% off not 50% off?

Common mistakes to watch for

  • Adding successive discounts (30% + 20% = 50%).
  • Finding an original price by adding the discount percent to the sale price (for example, $54 + 25% of $54).
  • Figuring markup as a percent of the selling price instead of the cost.
  • Thinking a bigger percent always means bigger savings.

Support (if the student is struggling)

  • Use 10-block tape diagrams for every discount.
  • Start each problem by writing "percent of ___."
  • Practice tips only with 10% and 20% before adding 15%.

Challenge (if the student is ready for more)

  • Find the single discount equal to 25% off followed by 20% off (40%), and explain why.
  • Research the tip credit for tipped workers in Florida and calculate a server's hourly pay with and without tips for one evening.

Background for the teacher

Discount: sale price = original × (1 − rate). Markup: price = cost × (1 + rate); markup percent is based on cost (retailers sometimes quote margin, a percent of price, instead; this lesson uses markup on cost only). Successive discounts multiply: 0.7 × 0.8 = 0.56, a 44% total discount. Discount and tax commute because both are multiplications: 80 × 0.75 × 1.075 = 80 × 1.075 × 0.75 = 64.5. In Florida, sales tax applies to the price after a store discount; manufacturers' coupons are handled differently, which this lesson does not address. U.S. tipping convention at sit-down restaurants is about 15% to 20% of the pre-tax bill. Florida allows employers a tip credit, so tipped employees may be paid a lower direct hourly wage than the state minimum, with tips making up the difference.

Answer key: interactive lesson

Reconnect

  1. What is 30% of $70?
    Answer: $21
    10% = $7, so 30% = 3 × $7 = $21.
  2. 18 is 45% of what number?
    Answer: 40
    45% → 18, so 5% → 2, so 100% → 40.
  3. Complete the conversions.
    Answer: 8; 60
    0.08 × 100 = 8%; 3/5 = 60/100 = 60%.
  4. Find the sales tax on a $36 purchase at 7.5%.
    Answer: $2.70
    $36 × 0.075 = $2.70.

Learn 1: Discounts and Sale Prices

  1. A $45 bike helmet is 20% off. What is the sale price?
    Answer: $36
    You pay 80%: 0.8 × $45 = $36.
  2. A $24 book is 35% off. What do you pay? Use the pay-what-is-left method.
    Answer: $15.60
    You pay 65%: 0.65 × $24 = $15.60.
  3. A pair of binoculars is on sale for $54, which is 25% off. What was the original price?
    Answer: $72
    $54 is 75% of the original. 75% → $54, 25% → $18, 100% → $72.
  4. A $100 rain jacket is 30% off. At the register you get an extra 20% off the sale price. What do you pay?
    Answer: $56
    70% of $100 = $70. Then 80% of $70 = $56.
  5. Sofia says 30% off and then an extra 20% off is the same as 50% off. Use the rain jacket to explain whether she is right.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Not right: 50% off would be $50, but the price is $56 (44% off); The extra 20% is a percent of the smaller sale price, not of the original price
    Model answer: She is not right. 50% off $100 would be $50, but you actually pay $56, which is 44% off. The extra 20% is taken from the $70 sale price, not from the original $100, so it saves only $14, not $20.

Learn 2: Markups: How Sellers Set Prices

  1. A bike shop pays $250 for a bike and uses a 40% markup. What is the selling price?
    Answer: $350
    40% of $250 = $100; $250 + $100 = $350 (or 1.4 × $250).
  2. A shop buys a puzzle for $15 and sells it for $24. What is the markup percent?
    Answer: 60 %
    Markup $9; $9 ÷ $15 = 0.6 = 60%.
  3. Imani's supplies for a set of coasters cost $6. She sells the set for $15. What is her profit?
    Answer: $9
    $15 − $6 = $9.
  4. A shopper says, "The store buys this lamp for $20 and sells it for $35. That's a rip-off!" Explain why stores need a markup, using at least two costs from the lesson.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: The store must pay for more than the item: rent, workers, shipping; Some profit is needed to stay in business
    Model answer: The $15 markup is not all profit. The store has to pay rent for the building, wages for workers and shipping to get the lamp there. If it sold the lamp for $20, it would lose money and close.

Learn 3: Tips: Paying for Good Service

  1. Find a 20% tip on a $45 bill.
    Answer: $9
    10% = $4.50; double it: $9.
  2. Find a 15% tip on a $38 bill.
    Answer: $5.70
    10% = $3.80; 5% = $1.90; total $5.70.
  3. A family's bill is $62.40 before tax. They leave a 20% tip. How much is the tip?
    Answer: $12.48
    10% = $6.24; double it: $12.48.
  4. Explain how you would estimate an 18% tip on a $51 bill in your head. Give your estimate.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: A sensible strategy (round $51 to $50; 20% of $50 = $10, then a little less; or 10% + 5% + a bit); An estimate near $9.18 (about $9)
    Model answer: I would round $51 to $50. 20% of $50 is $10, and 18% is a little less, so about $9. The exact tip is $9.18.

Learn 4: Money: Which Deal Is Better?

  1. A $10-off coupon and a 20%-off coupon save the same amount at one price. What is that price?
    Answer: $50
    $10 is 20% of the price. 20% → $10, so 100% → $50.
  2. A $90 tent is 30% off. Sales tax is 7.5% of the sale price. What is the total cost? Round to the nearest cent.
    Answer: $67.73
    Sale price: 0.7 × $90 = $63. Tax: $63 × 0.075 = $4.725, about $4.73. Total: $67.73.
  3. For the $80 shoes, Yusuf adds the 7.5% tax first ($86) and then takes 25% off. Does he get the same total, $64.50? Calculate, then explain why.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Calculates 75% of $86 = $64.50, the same total; Explains that both orders multiply $80 by 0.75 and by 1.075, and the order of multiplication does not change the product
    Model answer: Yes: 75% of $86 is $64.50, the same total. Both ways multiply $80 by 0.75 and by 1.075, and you can multiply numbers in any order and get the same product.

Hands-On

  1. List each sale item: its regular price, percent off, the value of one block, how many blocks you folded under, and the sale price. Did each match the ad? Show your work for two items.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Four items with regular price, percent off and sale price; Correct sale prices; Compares each with the ad; Work shown for two items
    Model answer: Sneakers $60, 25% off: each block $6; I folded under 2½ blocks ($15), so I pay $45. Water bottle $24, 30% off: block $2.40; folded 3 ($7.20); pay $16.80. Board game $35, 20% off: block $3.50; folded 2 ($7); pay $28. Hoodie $40, 40% off: block $4; folded 4 ($16); pay $24. All four matched the ad.
  2. What were your selling prices at a 50% and a 75% markup on the $12 cost? How did the strip show that a markup is a percent of the cost?
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Correct prices: $18 and $21; Explains that the added blocks are the same size as the cost blocks, so the markup is a percent of the cost
    Model answer: Each block is $1.20. A 50% markup added 5 blocks ($6), so the price was $18. A 75% markup added 7½ blocks ($9), so the price was $21. The blocks I taped on were the same size as the cost blocks, which shows the markup is a percent of the cost, not of the selling price.

Think Deeper

  1. Return to the hook. You need running shoes costing $50 and $30. Store A has 25% off everything. Store B has buy one, get the second 50% off (taken off the cheaper pair). Which store is cheaper, and by how much? Then explain when Store B's deal would be just as good as Store A's.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Store A: 75% of $80 = $60; Store B: $50 + $15 = $65; Store A is cheaper by $5; Store B matches only when both pairs cost the same (or explains that the discount shrinks when the cheaper pair costs less)
    Model answer: Store A: 75% of $80 = $60. Store B: $50 + half of $30 = $50 + $15 = $65. Store A is $5 cheaper. Store B's deal is only as good when both pairs cost the same, because then the 50% off one pair equals 25% off the total. When the second pair is cheaper, Store B's discount is smaller.
  2. Imani sells 20 plant hangers a month at $14 each (supplies $8 each). If she raises her markup to 100%, she expects to sell only 14 a month. Which markup earns her more profit each month? Show your work and say what else she might consider.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: 75% markup: $6 profit × 20 = $120; 100% markup: price $16, $8 profit × 14 = $112; The 75% markup earns more; A thoughtful extra consideration (time spent crocheting, customers, materials, demand changes)
    Model answer: At a 75% markup, her profit is $6 each, and $6 × 20 = $120. At a 100% markup, the price is $16, her profit is $8 each, and $8 × 14 = $112. The 75% markup earns $8 more. But at 100% she crochets 6 fewer hangers, so she saves time and yarn; she might decide that free time is worth $8.

Exit Check

  1. A $56 game is 25% off. What is the sale price?
    Answer: $42
    You pay 75%: 0.75 × $56 = $42.
  2. Find a 20% tip on a $35 bill.
    Answer: $7
    10% = $3.50; double it: $7.
  3. An item costs a store $20. The store uses a 60% markup. What is the selling price?
    Answer: $32
    60% of $20 = $12; $20 + $12 = $32.
  4. After 25% off, a backpack costs $27. What was the original price?
    Answer: $36
    $27 is 75% of the original: 75% → $27, 25% → $9, 100% → $36.

Answer key: printable page

  1. An $80 scooter is 15% off. Find the sale price in two ways.
    Answer: Discount $12, $80 − $12 = $68; or 85% of $80 = $68.
  2. A sale price of $42 is 30% off. What was the original price?
    Answer: $42 is 70%; 10% = $6; original $60.
  3. A shop pays $30 for a lamp and uses a 50% markup. Find the selling price and the profit.
    Answer: Price $45; profit $15.
  4. A craft fair seller buys supplies for $5 and sells the item for $8. What is the markup percent?
    Answer: $3 ÷ $5 = 60%.
  5. Find 15% and 20% tips on a $64 bill.
    Answer: 15%: $9.60; 20%: $12.80.
  6. Which saves more on a $35 sweater: $8 off or 20% off? Explain.
    Answer: 20% = $7, so $8 off saves more.
