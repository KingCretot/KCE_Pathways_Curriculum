Unit 3: The Power of Poetry · Week 13 · ELA · Teacher Plan

Harlem Renaissance Poets

Lesson length: 60 minutes live, plus the after-class interactive lesson

Objectives

  • Explain what the Harlem Renaissance was and how the Great Migration made it possible.
  • Analyze how Langston Hughes uses repetition, history and a collective speaker in free verse.
  • Analyze how Claude McKay uses the sonnet form, its rhyme scheme and its turn to express a worker's experience.
  • Analyze how Jessie Redmon Fauset uses an epigraph, a symbol and parallel stanzas to connect past and present.
  • Use the roots migr, nat, voc and liber, and write a poem modeled on Hughes's structure.

Standards

  • ELA.6.R.1.4: Describe the impact of various poetic forms on meaning and style.
  • ELA.6.R.1.2: Analyze the development of stated or implied theme(s) throughout a literary text.
  • ELA.6.R.3.1: Explain how figurative language contributes to tone and meaning in text(s).
  • ELA.6.V.1.2: Apply knowledge of Greek and Latin roots and affixes to determine meanings of words and phrases in grade-level content.
  • ELA.6.C.2.1: Present information orally, in a logical sequence, using nonverbal cues, appropriate volume, clear pronunciation, and appropriate pacing.
  • ELA.K12.EE.1.1: Cite evidence to explain and justify reasoning.

Materials

  • The interactive lesson
  • Large paper, markers, paper and pencil
  • Optional: a jazz or blues recording
  • Printable practice page

Vocabulary

  • Harlem Renaissance: A flowering of Black art, music and writing centered in Harlem, New York, in the 1920s and early 1930s.
  • Great Migration: The movement of about 6 million Black Southerners to cities in the North and West between about 1910 and 1970.
  • speaker: The voice that talks in a poem; it is not always the poet.
  • collective voice: A speaker who speaks for a whole group of people, not just one person.
  • free verse: Poetry without a regular rhyme scheme or meter.
  • volta: The turn in a sonnet, where the thought or feeling shifts.
  • epigraph: A quotation placed at the beginning of a poem or book to set its theme.
  • symbol: Something concrete, like stars or a river, that stands for a bigger idea.

Key questions to ask

  • Who is speaking, and for whom?
  • Why might a poet choose free verse? A sonnet?
  • How does the form match the feeling?
  • How do these poems connect past and present?

Common mistakes to watch for

  • The speaker is always the poet.
  • Free verse has no structure (it uses repetition and parallelism instead).
  • The Harlem Renaissance was only about Harlem (it influenced the whole country and the world).
  • Old forms like the sonnet are only for old-fashioned subjects.

Support (if the student is struggling)

  • Read each poem aloud first; the student follows along.
  • Provide a two-column chart: what the poem says / what it means.
  • Give sentence frames: "The speaker is ___ because ___."

Challenge (if the student is ready for more)

  • Read Hughes's autobiography passage about writing the poem (The Big Sea, 1940) at a library, and compare his account with the poem.
  • Find another poem from The Book of American Negro Poetry (1922, free online) and present it.

Background for the teacher

Hughes (1901 to 1967) wrote "The Negro Speaks of Rivers" in 1920 at age 18 while crossing the Mississippi near St. Louis by train; he describes this in The Big Sea (1940). It appeared in The Crisis in June 1921; the text here is from The Weary Blues (Knopf, 1926), where it carries the dedication to W. E. B. Du Bois. The Lincoln line refers to the traditional account of Lincoln's flatboat trips to New Orleans (1828 and 1831). McKay (1889 to 1948) was born in Jamaica, came to the United States in 1912 to study, and worked as a railroad dining-car waiter among other jobs; "The Tired Worker" appears in Harlem Shadows (1922) and in The Book of American Negro Poetry (1922), the source of this text. Fauset (1882 to 1961) was literary editor of The Crisis from 1919 to 1926; "Oriflamme" appeared in The Crisis in January 1920. Sojourner Truth (about 1797 to 1883) was born enslaved in Ulster County, New York. The Great Migration figure of about 6 million (1910 to 1970) follows the U.S. National Archives and the Smithsonian. All three poem texts were published before 1929 and are in the public domain in the United States.

Reconnect

  1. A Shakespearean sonnet ends with two rhyming lines. What are two rhyming lines in a row called?
    Answer: a couplet
    Sonnet 18 ends with the gg couplet.
  2. Complete the facts about haiku.
    Answer: 17; 5-7-5
    Bashō's "furu ike ya / kawazu tobikomu / mizu no oto" counts 5-7-5.
  3. What is a refrain? Give an example from any Week 12 poem.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: A line or phrase repeated at regular places; A correct example ("We bear her along like a pearl on a string")
    Model answer: A refrain is a line repeated at regular places in a poem, like "We bear her along like a pearl on a string" in "Palanquin Bearers."
  4. Which root inside the word symphony means sound?
    Answer: phon
    sym (together) + phon (sound).

Learn 1: Harlem in the 1920s: A Rebirth

  1. What does the word renaissance mean?
    Answer: rebirth
    Re (again) + nat/naiss (born): born again.
  2. Why do you think so many artists and writers gathered in one neighborhood at the same time? Give two reasons.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Connects to the Great Migration bringing many people to Harlem; A second reason: magazines and publishers nearby, artists inspiring each other, audiences for music and art, freedom to create
    Model answer: The Great Migration brought thousands of Black people to Harlem, so there was a large community and audience. Being close together, artists could inspire and support each other, and magazines like The Crisis were there to publish their work.
  3. Complete the facts.
    Answer: Crisis; Fauset
    The Crisis published Hughes's first famous poem in 1921.
  4. Use the roots.
    Answer: migrant; liberate
    migr = move; liber = free.

Learn 2: Langston Hughes: A Voice as Old as Rivers

  1. Connect the poem to Social Studies. Which river in the poem flows through each civilization you studied?
    Answer: Euphrates; Nile
    The Euphrates (with the Tigris) watered Mesopotamia; the Nile was the life of Kemet.
  2. Who is the "I" in this poem? Use two details from the poem to prove your answer.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: A collective voice: Black people (or African peoples) across history; Two details showing the speaker spans thousands of years and many places (Euphrates when dawns were young, the pyramids, Lincoln)
    Model answer: The "I" is a collective voice for Black people throughout history. The speaker bathed in the Euphrates "when dawns were young" and raised the pyramids by the Nile thousands of years ago, but also heard the Mississippi in Lincoln's time. Only a whole people, not one person, could live through all of that.
  3. What does "My soul has grown deep like the rivers" mean? Why do you think Hughes repeats it?
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Meaning: the people's spirit has gained wisdom, strength or endurance over a long history, like a river cut deep over time; Repetition emphasizes this main idea or theme and gives the poem a song-like or spiritual feeling
    Model answer: It means the speaker's spirit has become wise and strong over thousands of years of history, the way a river carves a deep channel over time. Hughes repeats it because it is the poem's main idea, and the repetition makes it sound like a song or a prayer.
  4. This poem has no regular rhyme scheme or meter. What is this kind of poetry called?
    Answer: free verse
    Free verse is held together by other patterns, like repetition and parallel structure.

Learn 3: Claude McKay: A Worker's Sonnet

  1. What is the rhyme scheme of lines 1 to 4? Type the four letters with no spaces.
    Answer: abab
    afternoon/moon (a), soft/aloft (b).
  2. Type the one-word conjunction that begins line 11, where the sonnet turns.
    Answer: But
    "But what steals out the gray clouds red like wine?" Dawn is coming.
  3. "The wretched day was theirs, the night is mine." Who might "they" be? What does this line reveal about the worker's life?
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: They: the bosses, employers or the people the worker labors for; The worker's days belong to others; only the night (rest) is his own, so he has little freedom or control
    Model answer: "They" are probably the bosses or employers who own his workday. The line shows that his days do not belong to him; the only time that is truly his is the night, when he can rest, and even that is short.
  4. Type the two words that rhyme in the final couplet.
    Answer: pity; city
    The gg couplet: pity / city.
  5. Why might McKay have chosen the sonnet, an old form used by Shakespeare, to write about a modern Black worker? Give one possible reason and explain it.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: A reasonable purpose (to show a worker's feelings deserve the same respect as any subject; to prove mastery of a respected form; the strict form feels like the worker's trapped routine); Explanation linked to the poem
    Model answer: By using the same form Shakespeare used for love and beauty, McKay shows that a tired worker's feelings deserve just as much respect. The strict 14 lines and set rhymes also feel like the worker's routine, which repeats every day with no escape.

Learn 4: Jessie Redmon Fauset: Still Looking at the Stars

  1. Compare the last lines of the two stanzas: "Still looking at the stars." and "Still visioning the stars!" What changes, and what does the change show?
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Notices the change from looking to visioning and from a period to an exclamation point; Explains the shift from sorrowful, passive watching to active hope, determination or imagining the future
    Model answer: "Looking" becomes "visioning," and the period becomes an exclamation point. The mother quietly looked at the stars in her grief, but her descendants actively imagine a free future and fight for it, so the poem moves from sorrow to determination.
  2. Whose words appear in the epigraph at the start of the poem?
    Answer: Sojourner Truth
    Truth's memory of her mother looking at the stars sets the poem's theme.
  3. What do the stars symbolize in "Oriflamme"? Explain how the epigraph helps you understand the symbol.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Stars: hope, freedom, connection to loved ones or a future that cannot be taken away; Uses the epigraph: the mother and her children looked at the same stars, so the stars connect separated people
    Model answer: The stars stand for hope and connection. In the epigraph, Truth's mother says her children, sold far away, look up at the same stars she does. So the stars connect people who were separated and stand for a hope that slavery could not take.
  4. Find the rhymes.
    Answer: scars; bars
    scars/stars and bars/stars: pain and prison bars both answered by hope.

Hands-On

  1. Write your finished poem.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: 10 to 14 lines; An opening line and a refrain that repeats; Four parallel lines beginning with I + a past-tense verb; Specific places and vivid images
    Model answer: I've known oceans: I've known oceans wider than my grandmother's stories and older than the islands. My heart has grown wide like the oceans. I waded in the Caribbean when my mother was small. I crossed the Atlantic in a crowded airplane at night. I fished in the Peace River with my father at dawn. I watched the Gulf turn orange over the Tampa sky. I've known oceans: Salt-bright, restless oceans. My heart has grown wide like the oceans.
  2. Explain your refrain. What does it say about you or your family, and why did you choose those four places?
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Explains the meaning of the refrain; Explains why the places matter
    Model answer: My refrain, "My heart has grown wide like the oceans," means my family's story stretches across many places, and that made me open to the world. I chose the places where my family came from and where we live now, from the oldest to the newest.

Think Deeper

  1. Both Hughes and Fauset connect the present to the past. Compare how each poet does it. Use at least one quotation from each poem.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Accurate explanation for Hughes (a collective speaker who has lived through all of history; rivers from ancient times to America); Accurate explanation for Fauset (the epigraph and the first stanza look back; the second stanza applies the mother's hope to the present); At least one accurate quotation from each; A clear comparison (similarity and difference)
    Model answer: Hughes connects past and present through a single collective speaker who has lived through all of history, from "I bathed in the Euphrates when dawns were young" to hearing the Mississippi in Lincoln's time. Fauset connects them through two generations: the first stanza shows Sojourner Truth's mother "still looking at the stars," and the second shows her descendants "still visioning the stars!" Both poets show that Black history is long and that its strength continues. The difference is that Hughes's past stretches across thousands of years and many lands, while Fauset focuses on slavery in America and the fight for freedom that followed.
  2. McKay's worker says, "the night is mine." Hughes's speaker says, "My soul has grown deep." What does each speaker claim to own that no one can take away? Why would that matter to writers of the Harlem Renaissance?
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: McKay: rest, private time, the night (even if brief); Hughes: a deep soul, history and wisdom; A thoughtful link to the Harlem Renaissance (claiming dignity, identity and pride when others controlled work, laws or opportunities)
    Model answer: McKay's worker owns only the night, the few hours of rest when no boss controls him. Hughes's speaker owns something bigger: a soul made deep by thousands of years of history. This mattered to Harlem Renaissance writers because Black Americans faced unfair laws and jobs that others controlled. Their poems claimed the things no one could take, such as rest, history, dignity and pride.

Exit Check

  1. Which magazine first published "The Negro Speaks of Rivers" in 1921?
    Answer: The Crisis
    The NAACP's magazine, edited by W. E. B. Du Bois.
  2. What is the name for a 14-line poem made of three quatrains and a couplet, like "The Tired Worker"?
    Answer: a sonnet (Shakespearean)
    abab cdcd efef gg.
  3. What is an epigraph, and why did Fauset begin "Oriflamme" with one?
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: A quotation at the start of a poem that sets its theme; Fauset's epigraph from Sojourner Truth introduces the mother and the stars that the poem builds on
    Model answer: An epigraph is a quotation at the start of a poem. Fauset used Sojourner Truth's memory of her mother looking at the stars to introduce the image and theme her poem builds on.
  4. What does the root migr mean, as in migration?
    Answer: move
    migr = move: migrate, migration, immigrant.

Answer key: printable page

  1. What was the Harlem Renaissance, and how did the Great Migration help make it possible?
    Answer: A 1920s flowering of Black art and writing in Harlem; the Great Migration brought many Black Southerners to northern cities like New York.
  2. Name the four rivers in "The Negro Speaks of Rivers." Why does Hughes choose these rivers?
    Answer: Euphrates, Congo, Nile, Mississippi; they trace Black history from the first civilizations and Africa to America.
  3. Mark the rhyme scheme of "The Tired Worker" and name the line where it turns.
    Answer: abab cdcd efef gg; the turn is at line 11 ("But").
  4. Explain the symbol of the stars in "Oriflamme."
    Answer: Hope and connection that cannot be taken away; separated families looked at the same stars.
  5. Define and give an example word for each root: migr, nat, voc, liber.
    Answer: migr = move (migrate); nat = born (native); voc = voice or call (vocal); liber = free (liberty).
  6. Which poem's form best fits its feeling? Explain.
    Answer: Model: McKay's strict sonnet fits the worker's unchanging daily routine, and the turn at line 11 matches the shock of dawn arriving.
