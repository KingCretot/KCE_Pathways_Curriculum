Unit 3: The Diversity of Life · Week 13 · Life Science · Teacher Plan

Dichotomous Keys

Lesson length: 60 minutes live, plus the after-class interactive lesson

Objectives

  • Explain what a dichotomous key is and how it uses paired, opposite statements.
  • Use a dichotomous key to identify Florida leaves and animals.
  • Describe leaf traits used in keys: simple or compound, lobed, toothed or smooth edges, vein patterns and needles.
  • Write and test a dichotomous key for a real collection, and revise it using evidence.
  • Explain how a key (for identifying) is different from classification (for showing relationships).

Standards

  • SC.6.L.15.1: Analyze and describe how and why organisms are classified according to shared characteristics with emphasis on the Linnaean system combined with the concept of Domains.
  • SC.6.N.1.4: Discuss, compare, and negotiate methods used, results obtained, and explanations among groups of students conducting the same investigation.
  • SC.6.N.1.5: Recognize that science involves creativity, not just in designing experiments, but also in creating explanations that fit evidence.
  • NGSS Science and Engineering Practices 2 and 6: developing and using models; constructing explanations and designing solutions.

Materials

  • The interactive lesson
  • The student's 6 to 8 pressed leaves from Week 12
  • Paper, pencil, a ruler in centimeters, sticky notes
  • Optional: a magnifying glass and a Florida tree field guide
  • Printable practice page

Vocabulary

  • dichotomous key: A tool that identifies an organism through a series of paired, opposite choices.
  • couplet: In a key, a pair of opposite statements; you choose the one that fits.
  • trait: A feature you can observe or measure, such as leaf shape or number of legs.
  • simple leaf: A leaf with one blade on its stalk.
  • compound leaf: A leaf made of several smaller leaflets on one stalk.
  • margin: The edge of a leaf: smooth (entire) or toothed.
  • invasive species: A non-native species that spreads and harms native ecosystems.

Key questions to ask

  • Is each couplet a true pair of opposites?
  • Could someone who has never seen these leaves follow this key?
  • Why avoid color?
  • How is identifying different from classifying?

Common mistakes to watch for

  • A key shows how organisms are related (it only identifies).
  • Each leaflet of a compound leaf is a separate leaf.
  • Color is a reliable trait.
  • Phone apps are always right.

Support (if the student is struggling)

  • For a homeschool student, the parent tester stands in for a classmate when comparing methods and results.
  • Start with 4 leaves instead of 8.
  • Give couplet frames: "___a. Leaf is ___ / ___b. Leaf is not ___."
  • Use only one trait type (shape) for the first key.

Challenge (if the student is ready for more)

  • Write a key for 8 Florida animals from Week 12 using only observable traits.
  • Compare a free UF/IFAS invasive plant guide with your key: what traits do the experts use?

Background for the teacher

Leaf traits: slash pine (Pinus elliottii) needles in bundles of 2 and 3; cabbage palm (Sabal palmetto) leaves are costapalmate (a fan with a curved midrib); Brazilian pepper (Schinus terebinthifolia) has pinnately compound leaves with usually 3 to 13 leaflets, often toothed; it is on Florida's state Noxious Weed List and is prohibited from sale and planting, and as a relative of poison ivy in the cashew family its sap can irritate skin. Live oak (Quercus virginiana): simple, oval, leathery, usually smooth-edged, evergreen. Sweetgum (Liquidambar styraciflua): star-shaped, 5 to 7 pointed lobes, finely toothed. Red maple (Acer rubrum): 3 to 5 shallow lobes, toothed margins, often a red petiole. Yaupon holly (Ilex vomitoria) is a native red-berried shrub. A key with n end points has n − 1 couplets. Florida's invasive plant information: UF/IFAS Center for Aquatic and Invasive Plants and the Florida Invasive Species Council.

Answer key: interactive lesson

Reconnect

  1. Complete the levels of classification, from broadest to most specific.
    Answer: phylum; genus
    Dear King Philip Came Over For Good Soup.
  2. In the scientific name Puma concolor, which word is the genus?
    Answer: Puma
    The genus comes first and is capitalized; the species name follows.
  3. Why do scientists use scientific names instead of common names like "panther" or "cougar"?
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Common names vary by place and language; one scientific name is used worldwide, so there is no confusion
    Model answer: The same animal has many common names (panther, cougar, mountain lion), but it has only one scientific name, Puma concolor, which scientists everywhere understand.
  4. Mushrooms and the yeast from Week 11 belong to which kingdom?
    Answer: Fungi
    Fungi absorb their food.

Learn 1: How a Dichotomous Key Works

  1. At each step of a dichotomous key, how many choices do you have?
    Answer: 2 choices
    Dicho = in two: every couplet has exactly two statements.
  2. Use the key. You find a flat, leathery leaf about 5 cm long. It is shaped like an egg, has no bulges, has no teeth along its edge, and grows alone on its stalk. What tree is it?
    Answer: live oak
    1b → 2b → 3b → 4b: live oak.
  3. Use the key. A leaf has several leaflets on one stalk. What plant is it?
    Answer: Brazilian pepper
    1b → 2b → 3a: Brazilian pepper, an invasive species.
  4. Why do you think keys always give exactly two choices at each step, instead of five or ten?
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Two opposite choices are easy to compare and hard to confuse; One clear decision at a time makes the key reliable and repeatable for any user
    Model answer: Two opposite choices make each decision simple: the leaf either is a needle or it isn't. With five or ten choices, people could get confused or pick different answers, so the key would be less reliable.

Learn 2: Reading a Leaf Like a Scientist

  1. Use the leaf vocabulary.
    Answer: compound; entire
    Compound = many leaflets; entire = a smooth margin.
  2. A cabbage palm's leaf has veins that run side by side. What is this vein pattern called?
    Answer: parallel veins
    Palms and grasses have parallel veins; most broad leaves have net veins.
  3. A student writes the couplet "1a. Leaf is green / 1b. Leaf is red." Give two reasons this is a poor couplet for a red maple, using what you learned.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Color changes with the season, age or light (red maple leaves are green in summer, red in fall); Not a measurable or permanent trait, so different users at different times would choose differently
    Model answer: Red maple leaves are green most of the year and turn red in fall, so the same tree would key out two different ways depending on the month. Color also depends on the light and the leaf's age, so two people might disagree. A shape trait like lobes is better.

Learn 3: Writing a Key That Works

  1. What is wrong with this couplet? "1a. Leaf is lobed. / 1b. Leaf is longer than 8 cm." Rewrite it so it works.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: The statements are not opposites: a leaf could be both lobed and longer than 8 cm, or neither; A correct rewrite: "1a. Leaf is lobed / 1b. Leaf is not lobed" (or a length pair)
    Model answer: The two statements are not opposites. A sweetgum leaf could be lobed and longer than 8 cm, so it fits both, and a small oval leaf fits neither. A better couplet: "1a. Leaf is lobed. / 1b. Leaf is not lobed."
  2. How many couplets does a dichotomous key need to identify 8 different leaves?
    Answer: 7 couplets
    Each couplet splits one group into two, so you always need one fewer couplet than organisms: 8 − 1 = 7.
  3. Explain why "Leaf is pretty / Leaf is not pretty" fails as a couplet, even though the two statements are opposites.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: "Pretty" is an opinion, not an observable or measurable trait; Different people would choose differently, so the key would not give repeatable results
    Model answer: "Pretty" is an opinion, not something you can observe or measure. Two people would choose differently, so the key would not give the same answer every time.

Learn 4: Keys in Real Science

  1. Why is it important for Florida families and landowners to be able to identify Brazilian pepper correctly? Give two reasons.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: It spreads and crowds out native plants and wildlife habitat; Correct ID avoids destroying native look-alikes (yaupon holly) or letting it spread; its sap can irritate skin
    Model answer: Brazilian pepper spreads quickly and crowds out the native plants that wildlife need, so it should be removed. But if people guess wrong, they might destroy a harmless native plant like yaupon holly or leave the invader to spread. Knowing what it is also warns them to wear gloves, because its sap can irritate skin.
  2. Fill in: a dichotomous key is a tool for ___ an organism, not for showing how organisms are related.
    Answer: identifying
    Keys identify; classification shows relationships.
  3. A plant-ID app is correct about 85 out of every 100 times. If you use it on 40 plants, about how many will it identify wrongly?
    Answer: 6 plants
    It is wrong 15% of the time: 0.15 × 40 = 6.
  4. A key puts bats and birds on the same branch because both have wings. Is that a mistake? Explain the difference between a key and classification.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Not a mistake for a key: it only needs easy-to-see traits to identify; Classification groups by shared ancestry and many traits, so bats are mammals (fur, milk), not birds
    Model answer: It is not a mistake for a key, because a key only needs a trait that is easy to see to separate organisms. Classification is different: it groups organisms by shared ancestry and many traits, so bats go with mammals because they have fur and feed their young milk.

Hands-On

  1. Write your finished dichotomous key as a numbered list of couplets.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: One fewer couplet than leaves; Each couplet is a pair of opposites; Observable or measurable traits only (no color or opinions); Every leaf reached by exactly one path
    Model answer: 1a. Leaf is a needle ........ go to 2 1b. Leaf is flat ........ go to 3 2a. Needles in bundles of 2 or 3 ........ slash pine 2b. Needles single ........ leaf A 3a. Leaf is lobed ........ go to 4 3b. Leaf is not lobed ........ go to 5 4a. 5 to 7 long, pointed lobes ........ sweetgum 4b. 3 shallow, toothed lobes ........ red maple 5a. Edge is smooth ........ live oak 5b. Edge is toothed ........ leaf B
  2. Describe the test. Which leaf did your tester choose, did they reach the right answer, and what did you revise?
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Describes the test and the result; Identifies a confusing couplet; Explains a specific revision based on the evidence
    Model answer: My mom chose leaf 4. She got stuck at couplet 5, "edge is smooth / edge is toothed," because the leaf had tiny teeth only near the tip. I changed it to "teeth along most of the edge / no teeth or teeth only at the tip," and on the second try she reached the right answer.

Think Deeper

  1. A key's couplet reads "Leaf longer than 10 cm / Leaf shorter than 10 cm." A student measures a leaf that is exactly 10 cm and gets stuck. How would you fix the couplet? What does this teach about writing scientific tools?
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Fix: include the boundary in one choice ("10 cm or longer / shorter than 10 cm") or use a different trait; Notes that living things vary, so traits near the boundary are risky; Lesson: tools must be clear, cover every case, be tested and revised so anyone gets the same result
    Model answer: I would rewrite it as "Leaf 10 cm or longer / Leaf shorter than 10 cm," so every leaf fits exactly one choice. Even better, I might choose a different trait, because leaves on the same tree vary in size and one near 10 cm could go either way. This shows that scientific tools must cover every possible case and should be tested by other people, then revised, so everyone gets the same result.
  2. You find a plant in your yard that might be Brazilian pepper. Would you trust a phone app or a dichotomous key more before removing it? Explain, and describe how you could use both.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: A clear position with reasons; Strengths and weaknesses of apps (fast, but can be wrong with look-alikes or bad photos) and keys (slower, but checks traits step by step); A sensible plan using both (app first, then confirm with a key, an expert or UF/IFAS extension)
    Model answer: I would trust a key more, because it makes me check each trait myself, while an app can be fooled by a blurry photo or a look-alike such as yaupon holly. The app is still useful because it is fast. My plan: take a clear photo for the app, then confirm with a Florida key, checking for leaflets and toothed edges. If they disagree, I would ask the county extension office before removing the plant.

Exit Check

  1. Use the key from the lesson. A leaf is a needle. What tree is it?
    Answer: slash pine
    1a: needles in bundles → slash pine.
  2. Use the key. A flat leaf about 12 cm across grows alone on its stalk. It has five deep, pointed sections that spread out like the fingers of a hand. What tree is it?
    Answer: sweetgum
    1b → 2b → 3b → 4a → 5a.
  3. Give one rule for writing a good couplet and explain why it matters.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: A correct rule (opposites; observable or measurable traits; no color or opinions); Why: so anyone gets the same answer
    Model answer: The two statements must be opposites, so every organism fits exactly one choice and anyone using the key gets the same answer.
  4. A leaf has 9 small leaflets lined up along one stalk, with one bud where the stalk meets the twig. Is it one compound leaf or 9 simple leaves? Type compound or simple.
    Answer: compound (one leaf)
    One bud at the base of the whole stalk means it is one compound leaf; single leaflets have no buds.

Answer key: printable page

  1. What is a dichotomous key, and what is a couplet?
    Answer: A tool that identifies organisms through paired opposite choices; a couplet is one pair of opposite statements.
  2. Trace the path for a red maple leaf through the key (for example, 1b → ...).
    Answer: 1b → 2b → 3b → 4a → 5b.
  3. Explain the difference between a simple and a compound leaf.
    Answer: Simple: one blade on the stalk. Compound: several leaflets on one stalk.
  4. Rewrite this couplet so it works: "1a. Leaf has teeth / 1b. Leaf is long."
    Answer: 1a. Leaf edge has teeth / 1b. Leaf edge is smooth (no teeth).
  5. How many couplets does a key for 12 organisms need? Explain.
    Answer: 11, because each couplet splits one group into two.
  6. Why should a key avoid color as a trait?
    Answer: Color changes with season, age and light, so the same organism could key out differently.
