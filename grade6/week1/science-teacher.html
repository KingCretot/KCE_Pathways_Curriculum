Unit 1: Thinking Like a Scientist · Week 1 · Life Science · Teacher Plan

Observations, Inferences and Questions

Lesson length: 60 minutes live, plus the after-class interactive lesson

Objectives

  • Tell the difference between an observation and an inference.
  • Make qualitative and quantitative observations using your senses and tools.
  • Make inferences that are supported by evidence, and recognize that more than one inference can fit the same evidence.
  • Turn a question you wonder about into a testable scientific question.
  • Describe how scientist Charles Henry Turner used careful observation to discover how insects learn.

Standards

  • SC.6.N.1.1: Define a problem from the sixth grade curriculum, use appropriate reference materials to support scientific understanding, plan and carry out scientific investigation of various types, such as systematic observations or experiments, identify variables, collect and organize data, interpret data in charts, tables, and graphics, analyze information, make predictions, and defend conclusions.
  • SC.6.N.1.4: Discuss, compare and negotiate methods used, results obtained and explanations among groups of students conducting the same investigation.
  • SC.6.N.2.3: Recognize that scientists who make contributions to scientific knowledge come from all kinds of backgrounds and possess varied talents, interests and goals.
  • SC.7.N.1.6 (preview): Explain that empirical evidence is the cumulative body of observations of a natural phenomenon on which scientific explanations are based.
  • NGSS Science and Engineering Practices 1 and 7: asking questions; engaging in argument from evidence.

Materials

  • The interactive lesson
  • A shoebox or bag and one hidden object (prepared by a family member before class if possible)
  • Ruler and kitchen scale (optional)
  • Printable practice page

Vocabulary

  • observation: Information gathered directly with your senses or with tools. Example: the leaf is 6 centimeters long.
  • inference: An explanation or conclusion based on observations and what you already know. Example: an animal ate the leaf.
  • qualitative observation: An observation that describes qualities, such as color, texture, smell or shape.
  • quantitative observation: An observation that uses numbers or measurements.
  • evidence: Observations and data that support an idea.
  • testable question: A question that can be answered by doing an investigation and collecting data.
  • prior knowledge: What you already know before you begin, which helps you make inferences.
  • hypothesis: A testable prediction that explains what you expect to happen and why.

Lesson flow

5 minReconnect
Run the Reconnect questions. Note whether the student already knows metric units; they matter in Week 3.
8 minHook: the garden
Show the garden picture. Ask the student to say everything they notice for one minute without explaining anything. Write the list. Then ask for explanations and write them in a second column. Label the columns observations and inferences.
12 minObservations and inferences
Sort statements together. Push on why 'a raccoon ate it' is an inference. Introduce the word probably, and ask what new evidence would change the inference.
10 minTestable questions
Convert two not-testable questions together. Have the student name what is changed and what is measured in each.
10 minScientist spotlight
Read about Charles Henry Turner together. Discuss: how did he do serious science without a university lab? How did evidence change what other scientists believed?
12 minMystery container live
If a container is ready, do the activity live: the student makes observations out loud while you record them, then infers before opening. Otherwise model with an object you hide on your side of the camera.
3 minAssign
Assign the Think Deeper questions, the Exit Check and the printable page.

Key questions to ask

  • Did you see that, or are you thinking it?
  • What else could explain the same evidence?
  • What would make you change your mind?
  • How could you turn that into a number?
  • What would you change, and what would you measure?

Common mistakes to watch for

  • Believing an inference is a guess. It must be based on evidence and prior knowledge.
  • Believing there is only one correct inference for a set of observations.
  • Thinking qualitative observations are less scientific; both types matter.
  • Writing a question that names what is measured but not what is changed.

Support (if the student is struggling)

  • Use a two-column chart labeled I SEE and I THINK for every statement.
  • Give sentence frames: I observe... This makes me infer... because I know...
  • Reduce the mystery container to a choice among four objects the student sees beforehand.

Challenge (if the student is ready for more)

  • Have the student research one more of Turner's discoveries, such as how ants find their way home, and describe the experiment.
  • Ask the student to design a trail-camera investigation for the garden mystery with a clear testable question.

Background for the teacher

Charles Henry Turner (1867 to 1923) earned his PhD in zoology from the University of Chicago in 1907 and taught at Sumner High School in St. Louis. His 1910 experiments gave early evidence that honeybees learn color and pattern (Karl von Frisch later proved bee color vision in 1914), that insects can hear, and that ants learn routes. A circling behavior of ants returning to the nest is known as Turner's circling. He published dozens of papers despite being shut out of university positions by racism.

Raccoon front tracks are about 5 to 8 centimeters long with five long, finger-like toes. Dog tracks show four toes with claw marks and a triangular pad.

Answer key: interactive lesson

Reconnect

  1. What tool measures temperature?
    Answer: thermometer
    A thermometer measures temperature in degrees Celsius or Fahrenheit.
  2. Scientists use the metric system. What is the basic metric unit of length?
    Answer: meter
    The meter is the basic unit of length. Centimeters and kilometers are based on it.
  3. What is the basic metric unit of mass?
    Answer: gram (scientists treat the kilogram as the official base unit)
    Mass is measured in grams and kilograms.
  4. You want to measure the length of a leaf. Name the tool and the metric unit you would use. Answer like this: tool, unit.
    Answer: a ruler, centimeters (or millimeters)
    A metric ruler measures small lengths in centimeters or millimeters.
  5. In an experiment, what do we call the part you change on purpose?
    Answer: independent variable
    The independent variable is what you change. You will master variables in Week 2.
  6. What tool makes small objects look larger so you can see details?
    Answer: hand lens (magnifying glass) or microscope
    A hand lens magnifies a little; a microscope magnifies much more. Microscopes return in Unit 2.

Learn 1: Observations: What You Actually Notice

  1. "The tracks are about 7 centimeters long." Is this a qualitative or quantitative observation?
    Answer: quantitative
    It uses a measurement with a number, so it is quantitative.
  2. "The mud smells earthy." Qualitative or quantitative?
    Answer: qualitative
    It describes a quality (smell) with no number.
  3. Change this qualitative observation into a quantitative one: The tomato plant is tall.
    Answer: For example: The tomato plant is 110 centimeters tall.
    Adding a measurement with a unit makes it quantitative.
  4. Look at the picture. How many tracks are in the mud?
    Answer: 5
    Counting is a quantitative observation: 5 tracks.

Learn 2: Inferences: What It Might Mean

  1. "A raccoon ate the tomato." Is this an observation or an inference ?
    Answer: inference
    Nobody saw a raccoon. It is an explanation built from the tracks and the bite.
  2. Look at the garden picture. Write one observation a scientist could measure and one inference that the observation supports.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Observation includes a number or measurement; Inference is an explanation, not a fact; Inference is supported by the observation
    Model answer: Observation: the tracks are about 7 centimeters long. Inference: a small animal about the size of a raccoon made them.
  3. Which observation is the best evidence that the visitor was not a dog?
    Answer: The five long, finger-like toes (dog tracks have short, rounded toes).
    The shape of the toes matches a raccoon, not a dog.
  4. What additional evidence could Chidi collect tonight to test his inference?
    Answer: Set up a trail camera, or spread flour near the plant and check for fresh tracks.
    New observations, such as a camera recording, can support or reject the inference.

Learn 3: Asking Testable Questions

  1. Is this question testable? Which smells best: lemon, mint or lavender? Type yes or no .
    Answer: no
    "Best" is an opinion. Nothing can be measured to settle it.
  2. Is this question testable? Does the temperature of water change how fast sugar dissolves? Type yes or no.
    Answer: yes
    You can change the water temperature and measure the time it takes the sugar to dissolve.
  3. In the sugar question, identify the parts.
    Answer: water temperature / time for the sugar to dissolve
    The water temperature is changed; the dissolving time is measured.
  4. Rewrite as a testable question: Is sunlight good for plants?
    Answer: For example: Does the number of hours of sunlight change how tall bean plants grow in two weeks?
    A testable version names what you change (sunlight) and what you measure (growth).

Learn 4: Scientist Spotlight: Charles Henry Turner

  1. What did many scientists believe about insects before Turner's experiments?
    Answer: That insects act only on instinct and cannot learn.
    Turner's evidence challenged the idea that insects only act on instinct.
  2. Write Turner's honeybee investigation as a testable question.
    Answer: For example: Can honeybees learn to find food by the color of the card it is on?
    He changed the card color and observed where the bees went.
  3. In what year did Turner earn his PhD from the University of Chicago?
    Answer: 1907
    1907. He was one of the first African Americans to earn a doctorate from that university.

Hands-On

  1. List your 6 or more observations, each labeled QL or QN.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Completes every step of the activity; Includes specific details, numbers or observations from the activity; Explains thinking in complete sentences
  2. Write your 2 inferences with evidence, your extra test, and what the object turned out to be. Which observation helped most, and which one misled you?
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Completes every step of the activity; Includes specific details, numbers or observations from the activity; Explains thinking in complete sentences

Think Deeper

  1. The footprint puzzle. Two sets of animal tracks, one large and one small, come toward each other across the wet sand from opposite directions. Where they meet, the sand is trampled and scattered. After that spot, only the large tracks continue. Write two different inferences about what happened, the evidence for each, and one observation that would help you decide between them.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Two different, reasonable inferences; Each inference is tied to specific observations; Separates observation from inference clearly; Names a new observation that could help decide between them
    Model answer: Inference 1: the large animal caught and carried off the small animal, because the small tracks stop where the sand is trampled. Inference 2: the small animal climbed onto the large animal or flew away, which would also end its tracks. Looking for drag marks, feathers or fur at the trampled spot would help decide.
  2. Write one question you truly wonder about in your home, yard or neighborhood. Then rewrite it as a testable question, naming what you would change and what you would measure.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Original question from the student's own world; Rewritten question is testable; Names what is changed and what is measured

Exit Check

  1. "The ice cube melted in 12 minutes." Observation or inference?
    Answer: observation
    It was measured directly.
  2. "The room must be warm because the ice melted fast." Observation or inference?
    Answer: inference
    It explains the observation using prior knowledge.
  3. "The leaf has 7 holes in it." Qualitative or quantitative?
    Answer: quantitative
    It uses a count.
  4. Turn this inference back into the observations that could support it: The dog is hungry. Write two observations.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Two observations someone could see or measure; Neither observation is itself an inference
    Model answer: The dog is standing by its empty bowl. The dog has not eaten since 7 a.m., and it is whining.
  5. Is this testable? Does the height a ball is dropped from change how high it bounces? Type yes or no.
    Answer: yes
    Change the drop height and measure the bounce height.
  6. Why do scientists use the word probably when they state an inference?
    Answer: Because an inference could be wrong and might change with new evidence.
    Inferences are explanations, not direct facts, so they stay open to new evidence.
  7. Turner's experiments gave evidence that honeybees can learn to recognize what two things?
    Answer: colors and patterns
    His colored-card experiments gave early evidence that bees learn colors and patterns. The German scientist Karl von Frisch later proved bee color vision in 1914.

Answer key: printable page

  1. Mark each O or I. ___ The puddle is 40 cm wide. ___ It rained last night. ___ The bread has green spots. ___ The bread is old. ___ The dog is barking. ___ The dog wants to go outside.
    Answer: O, I, O, I, O, I
  2. Write two qualitative and two quantitative observations about an object on your table right now.
    Answer: Answers vary; qualitative describe qualities (color, texture, smell); quantitative use numbers with units.
  3. A plant on the windowsill is drooping and its soil is dry and cracked. Write one inference and the evidence for it.
    Answer: For example: the plant needs water, because the soil is dry and cracked and drooping leaves are a sign of water loss.
  4. Write another inference that could explain the same drooping plant.
    Answer: For example: the plant is getting too much heat from the window, or it has a disease or pests.
  5. Rewrite as a testable question: Are cats smart?
    Answer: For example: Can a cat learn to find a treat under a red cup faster after five practice days?
  6. In your testable question, what would you change and what would you measure?
    Answer: Answers should name one changed variable and one measured result.
  7. Explain how Charles Henry Turner's work changed what scientists believed about insects.
    Answer: His experiments showed insects can see color, hear and learn, challenging the belief that insects act only on instinct.
