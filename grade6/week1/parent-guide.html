Grade 6 · Week 1 · Parent Guide

Week 1: Getting Started in Grade 6

Welcome to Grade 6 Pathways. This week your child learns the core tools of every subject: checking answers in math, reading like a writer, thinking like a scientist and working like a historian. You do not need to teach the lessons. Your job is to ask good questions and connect the learning to everyday life.

How scoring works: each lesson scores itself. Your child gets two tries per question: full points on the first try, half points on the second, then the answer and explanation appear. A score of 85 or higher means the lesson is mastered. Written answers go to Jonathan, who scores them with a rubric.

Time this week: About 60 minutes of live lessons per subject, plus 30 to 45 minutes of after-class work per subject (the interactive lesson, a hands-on activity and short written responses).

Gather this week: a grocery receipt or store flyer; sticky notes; 5 index cards and poster paper; markers and two highlighters; a shoebox with a lid and one hidden household object; an old photo, letter and object from your family.

Heads up: Your child will interview an older relative or family friend for 10 minutes for Social Studies. Schedule it early in the week.

ELA: Story Elements and How Rubrics Work

Your child reads an original story called The Cast Net, learns the five parts of a plot, practices showing feelings through details, learns four Greek and Latin word roots, and learns to use a rubric. They begin choosing a true story from their own life for a personal narrative due in Week 5.

Ask at dinner: "What true story from your life could you tell? What was the problem, and what was the turning point?"

Try it together: Tell a family story at dinner, such as a trip, a funny mistake or the day someone was born, and help your child spot the setting, problem and climax.

Signs it's clicking: They can explain the difference between an internal and an external conflict, and they use words like climax and resolution. Signs they need help: They describe a story only as a list of events, or cannot pick a personal story to write about. Help them brainstorm.

Math: Whole Numbers, Long Division and Budgets

Your child reviews multiplication and long division, learns to estimate before calculating, decides what a remainder means in a real situation, and builds a weekly budget for a young person's banana bread business.

Ask at dinner: "How do you know your answer makes sense?" Asking this every day builds the habit of checking.

Try it together: Share a real grocery receipt. Have your child estimate the total before looking, then sort items into needs and wants. This is part of the hands-on lab.

Signs it's clicking: They estimate first without being reminded, and they can explain why 250 loaves need 21 boxes of 12, not 20. Signs they need help: They forget the zero in answers like 1,836 ÷ 6 = 306, or they struggle with multiplication facts. A few minutes of fact practice each day helps a lot.

Life Science: Observations, Inferences and Questions

Your child learns the difference between what they observe and what they infer, makes measured observations, writes testable questions, and learns about Charles Henry Turner, who proved that insects can learn.

Ask at dinner: "Did you see that, or are you explaining what you think it means?"

Try it together: Help with the Mystery Container: secretly put one household object in a closed shoebox and let your child investigate it without opening it.

Signs it's clicking: They catch themselves stating an inference as a fact and correct it, and they turn wonderings into questions they could test. Signs they need help: They think an inference is just a random guess. Remind them it must be backed by evidence.

World and American History: How Historians Know the Past

Your child learns primary and secondary sources, how archaeology and oral tradition (such as West African griots) preserve history, how to read BCE and CE dates, and how a single word like discovered can reveal bias.

Ask at dinner: "Whose point of view is this from? Whose story might be missing?"

Try it together: Let your child interview you or another relative for 10 minutes about your childhood. Share an old photo, letter or object. Their interview becomes a primary source.

Signs it's clicking: They can explain why 3000 BCE is older than 2000 BCE and why historians compare more than one source. Signs they need help: They mix up BCE order or century names (the 1900s are the 20th century). A number line with 1 BCE and 1 CE side by side helps (there is no year 0).
