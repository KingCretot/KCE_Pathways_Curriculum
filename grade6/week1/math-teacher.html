Unit 1: Number Power · Week 1 · Math · Teacher Plan

Whole-Number Operations and Long Division

Lesson length: 60 minutes live on Zoom, plus 35 to 45 minutes for the after-class interactive lesson and lab

Objectives

  • Estimate to check whether an answer is reasonable before and after you calculate.
  • Multiply multi-digit whole numbers using an area model and the standard algorithm.
  • Divide multi-digit numbers by two-digit divisors with long division.
  • Decide what a remainder means in a real situation: round up, drop it, or use it.
  • Build a weekly budget using income, expenses and profit.

Standards

  • MA.6.NSO.2.1: Multiply and divide positive multi-digit numbers, including using a standard algorithm with procedural fluency (whole numbers this week; decimals in Weeks 2 to 4).
  • Review of MA.5.NSO.2.1 and MA.5.NSO.2.2: multiply and divide multi-digit whole numbers using a standard algorithm.
  • MA.K12.MTR.6.1: Assess the reasonableness of solutions.
  • MA.K12.MTR.7.1: Apply mathematics to real-world contexts.
  • Financial literacy: income, expenses, profit and budgeting (Jump$tart National Standards, Spending and Saving).

Materials

  • The interactive lesson on a laptop or desktop
  • Whiteboard or paper for working problems live on Zoom
  • A grocery receipt or store flyer for the hands-on lab
  • Sticky notes
  • Printable practice page

Vocabulary

  • dividend: The number being divided. In 84 ÷ 7 = 12, the dividend is 84.
  • divisor: The number you divide by. In 84 ÷ 7 = 12, the divisor is 7.
  • quotient: The answer to a division problem. In 84 ÷ 7 = 12, the quotient is 12.
  • remainder: The amount left over when a number cannot be divided evenly.
  • estimate: A close, reasonable value found with rounded or compatible numbers.
  • compatible numbers: Numbers that are easy to compute with mentally, such as 6,000 ÷ 30.
  • income: Money coming in, such as pay or sales.
  • expense: Money going out to pay for something.
  • profit: Income minus expenses. What a business keeps.
  • budget: A plan for how money will come in and go out over a period of time.

Lesson flow

5 minWelcome and reconnect
Run the Reconnect questions together. Have the student explain one answer out loud. Note any gaps in multiplication facts or place value; they predict trouble with long division.
8 minHook: Amara's business
Read the situation together. Ask: what numbers would a business owner need to know every week? List the student's ideas, then point to income, expenses and profit.
10 minEstimate first
Model compatible numbers with 6,215 ÷ 31. Then have the student estimate 7,950 ÷ 39 before you show the answer. Stress that estimating is a checking tool professionals use.
10 minMultiply
Draw the area model for 46 × 23 on the whiteboard. Connect each box to a line of the standard algorithm. The student completes 38 × 24 and 207 × 48 while explaining each step.
15 minLong division and remainders
Work 1,476 ÷ 12 together using Divide, Multiply, Subtract, Bring down. Then work 1,836 ÷ 6 and pause at the zero. Use the festival boxes to discuss what a remainder means in three different ways.
8 minBudget
Build Amara's budget table with the student. Have them say the profit formula in their own words before calculating.
4 minWrap up and assign
Preview the hands-on Receipt Budget Lab, the Think Deeper questions and the Exit Check for after class. Assign the printable page.

Key questions to ask

  • How do you know your answer is reasonable?
  • Why did you choose those compatible numbers?
  • What does the 0 in 306 mean?
  • In this situation, should the remainder be dropped, rounded up, or used? How do you know?
  • Which of Amara's expenses could she cut, and which can she not? Why?

Common mistakes to watch for

  • Skipping the zero in the quotient (1,836 ÷ 6 written as 36). Have the student say 'one digit up for every digit down.'
  • Rounding the divisor and dividend to the nearest ten instead of to compatible numbers, which makes estimating harder.
  • Always dropping the remainder, even when everything must be included (boxes, vans).
  • Adding instead of subtracting when finding profit, or subtracting income from expenses.

Support (if the student is struggling)

  • Give a multiplication facts chart for 1 to 12 during long division so facts do not block the method.
  • Use graph paper to keep digits in place-value columns.
  • Start with one-digit divisors before moving to two-digit divisors.
  • For two-digit divisors, list the first few multiples (12, 24, 36, 48...) beside the problem.

Challenge (if the student is ready for more)

  • Have the student find Amara's profit per loaf (265 ÷ 64, about $4.14) and discuss whether a price change from $7 to $8 is worth it if sales drop to 55 loaves.
  • Ask the student to create a long-division problem whose quotient has two zeros, then trade with you to solve.

Answer key: interactive lesson

Reconnect

  1. What is 9 × 12?
    Answer: 108
    9 × 12 = 9 × 10 + 9 × 2 = 90 + 18 = 108.
  2. In the number 2,468,315, what digit is in the ten-thousands place?
    Answer: 6
    From the right: ones 5, tens 1, hundreds 3, thousands 8, ten-thousands 6.
  3. Round 47,682 to the nearest thousand.
    Answer: 48,000
    The hundreds digit is 6, which is 5 or more, so 47 thousands rounds up to 48 thousands.
  4. What is 36 × 25?
    Answer: 900
    36 × 25 = 9 × 4 × 25 = 9 × 100 = 900. Spotting 4 × 25 = 100 is a useful shortcut.
  5. Name the parts of a division problem.
    Answer: dividend / divisor / quotient
    Dividend ÷ divisor = quotient.
  6. What is 1,000 ÷ 8?
    Answer: 125
    8 × 125 = 1,000, so 1,000 ÷ 8 = 125.
  7. The answer to a multiplication problem has a special name. What is it?
    Answer: product
    Factor × factor = product.
  8. Estimate 412 × 19 by rounding each number to its greatest place. What is your estimate?
    Answer: 8,000
    412 rounds to 400 and 19 rounds to 20. 400 × 20 = 8,000.

Learn 1: Estimate First, Then Check

  1. Estimate 7,950 ÷ 39 using compatible numbers. What is your estimate?
    Answer: 200
    7,950 is close to 8,000 and 39 is close to 40. 8,000 ÷ 40 = 200.
  2. Amara's calculator shows 4,212 ÷ 9 = 4,680. Without dividing, is that reasonable? Type yes or no .
    Answer: no
    Dividing 4,212 by 9 must give a number much smaller than 4,212, but 4,680 is larger than 4,212. An estimate of 4,500 ÷ 9 = 500 shows the answer should be about 468, not 4,680. A digit was probably entered wrong.
  3. Round 3,849 to the nearest hundred.
    Answer: 3,800
    The tens digit is 4, which is less than 5, so 38 hundreds stays 38 hundreds.

Learn 2: Multiplying Multi-Digit Numbers

  1. Fill in the area model for 38 × 24.
    Answer: 600 / 120 / 160 / 32
    Multiply each place-value part: tens by tens, tens by ones, ones by tens, ones by ones.
  2. Now add the parts. What is 38 × 24?
    Answer: 912
    600 + 120 + 160 + 32 = 912. Check: 40 × 25 = 1,000, so 912 is reasonable.
  3. Use the standard algorithm: 207 × 48 = ?
    Answer: 9,936
    207 × 8 = 1,656 and 207 × 40 = 8,280. 1,656 + 8,280 = 9,936.
  4. Each loaf uses 325 grams of flour. How many grams does Amara need for 48 loaves?
    Answer: 15,600
    325 × 48 = 15,600 grams, which is 15.6 kilograms. Estimate: 300 × 50 = 15,000.

Learn 3: Long Division with Two-Digit Divisors

  1. Put the four repeating steps of long division in order.
    Answer: Divide → Multiply → Subtract → Bring down
    Divide, Multiply, Subtract, Bring down. Then repeat.
  2. 1,836 ÷ 6 = ?
    Answer: 306
    18 ÷ 6 = 3, 3 ÷ 6 = 0 (write the 0), 36 ÷ 6 = 6. The quotient is 306.
  3. 2,592 ÷ 24 = ?
    Answer: 108
    25 ÷ 24 = 1 R1. Bring down 9: 19 ÷ 24 = 0. Bring down 2: 192 ÷ 24 = 8. The quotient is 108.
  4. 7,315 ÷ 35 = ?
    Answer: 209
    73 ÷ 35 = 2 R3, 31 ÷ 35 = 0, 315 ÷ 35 = 9. Check: 35 × 209 = 7,315.
  5. Amara has 500 loaves and boxes that hold 12. How many boxes does she need so every loaf is packed?
    Answer: 42
    500 ÷ 12 = 41 R8. Forty-one boxes leave 8 loaves unpacked, so she needs 42 boxes.
  6. Loaf pans cost $12 each. Amara has $95. How many pans can she buy?
    Answer: 7 pans
    95 ÷ 12 = 7 R11. She cannot buy part of a pan, so she can buy 7 and keeps $11.

Learn 4: Building a Weekly Budget

  1. What was Amara's income for the week?
    Answer: $448
    64 × $7 = $448.
  2. What were her total expenses?
    Answer: $183
    38 + 24 + 18 + 46 + 35 + 22 = $183.
  3. What was her profit?
    Answer: $265
    $448 − $183 = $265.
  4. Amara is thinking about raising her price from $7 to $8. She expects sales to drop from 64 loaves to 55 loaves, and her expenses stay at $183. What would her profit be at $8?
    Answer: $257
    55 × $8 = $440 income. $440 − $183 = $257 profit, which is $8 less than the $265 she earns at $7. Raising the price would cost her money, so she should keep the $7 price.
  5. Amara wants a $900 stand mixer and saves $120 of profit each week. How many weeks must she save?
    Answer: 8 weeks
    900 ÷ 120 = 7.5. After 7 weeks she has $840, which is not enough, so she needs 8 weeks.

Think Deeper

  1. Jalen worked out 4,836 ÷ 12 and got 43 . Find the correct quotient.
    Answer: 403
    48 ÷ 12 = 4, then 3 ÷ 12 = 0 (a zero must go in the quotient), then 36 ÷ 12 = 3. The answer is 403.
  2. Explain Jalen's mistake to him in two or three sentences. How could an estimate have warned him?
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Names the missing zero in the quotient when 12 does not go into 3; Explains that every digit brought down needs a digit in the quotient; Uses an estimate (4,800 ÷ 12 = 400) to show 43 is far too small
    Model answer: Jalen forgot to write a 0 in the quotient when 12 did not go into 3, so his digits slid over and 403 became 43. An estimate of 4,800 ÷ 12 = 400 shows the answer must be about 400, so 43 is far too small.
  3. Describe three real situations from your own life: one where you round a quotient up , one where you drop the remainder, and one where the remainder is the answer.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Round-up example where everything must be included (seats, boxes, trips); Drop-the-remainder example where only complete groups count (full bags, items you can afford); Remainder-as-answer example (leftovers, the last group); Each example shows the division clearly
    Model answer: Round up: 30 people need rides and each car holds 4, so 30 ÷ 4 = 7 R2 means 8 cars. Drop the remainder: I have $25 and books cost $6, so 25 ÷ 6 = 4 R1 means I can buy 4 books. Remainder is the answer: 50 cookies shared among 8 friends gives 6 each with 2 left over for me.

Exit Check

  1. 3,124 × 7 = ?
    Answer: 21,868
    3,124 × 7 = 21,868.
  2. 84 × 56 = ?
    Answer: 4,704
    84 × 50 = 4,200 and 84 × 6 = 504. 4,200 + 504 = 4,704.
  3. 6,048 ÷ 8 = ?
    Answer: 756
    8 × 756 = 6,048.
  4. 9,630 ÷ 45 = ?
    Answer: 214
    96 ÷ 45 = 2 R6, 63 ÷ 45 = 1 R18, 180 ÷ 45 = 4. Check: 45 × 214 = 9,630.
  5. A homeschool co-op is taking 180 students on a field trip. Each van holds 14 students. How many vans are needed?
    Answer: 13 vans
    180 ÷ 14 = 12 R12. Twelve vans leave 12 students behind, so 13 vans are needed.
  6. Marcus earns $15 an hour and works 18 hours. He spends $85 on a bike part and $64 on a game. How much money does he have left?
    Answer: $121
    15 × 18 = $270 income. 85 + 64 = $149 spent. 270 − 149 = $121 left.
  7. Without calculating exactly, is 5,118 ÷ 6 closest to 85, 850 or 8,500? Type the number.
    Answer: 850
    6 × 850 = 5,100, so the quotient is about 850 (exactly 853).
  8. Create your own long-division problem with a two-digit divisor whose quotient has a 0 in the middle (like 306). Write the problem, solve it, and check it with multiplication.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Two-digit divisor; Quotient has a zero in the middle; Correct solution; Checked by multiplying
    Model answer: 2,448 ÷ 12 = 204. Check: 12 × 204 = 2,448.
  9. Budget vocabulary.
    Answer: income / expenses / profit
    Income − expenses = profit.
