Unit 1: Stories That Shape Us (Narrative) · Week 1 · ELA · Teacher Plan

Reading Like a Writer: Story Elements and How Rubrics Work

Lesson length: 60 minutes live, plus the after-class interactive lesson

Objectives

  • Identify setting, characters, conflict and the five parts of plot in a short story.
  • Explain how a writer uses sensory details and similes to show a character's feelings.
  • Use the Greek and Latin roots graph, scrib/script, bio and auto to figure out new words.
  • Read a rubric, score a sample paragraph with it, and use it to plan your own writing.
  • Write the opening paragraph of a personal narrative.

Standards

  • ELA.6.R.1.1: Analyze how the interaction between characters contributes to the development of a plot.
  • Review of ELA.5.R.1.1: Explain how setting, events, conflict and characterization contribute to the plot in a literary text.
  • ELA.6.V.1.2: Apply knowledge of Greek and Latin roots and affixes to determine meanings of words and phrases in grade-level content.
  • ELA.6.C.1.2: Write personal or fictional narratives using narrative techniques, precise words and phrases, and figurative language.
  • ELA.6.V.1.3: Apply knowledge of context clues, figurative language, word relationships, reference materials and/or background knowledge to determine and clarify the meaning of words and phrases.
  • ELA.K12.EE.2.1: Read and comprehend grade-level complex texts proficiently.

Materials

  • The interactive lesson
  • The story The Cast Net (in the lesson; print it if the student likes to annotate on paper)
  • Highlighters in two colors
  • Index cards or sticky notes and poster paper for the hands-on activity
  • Printable practice page

Vocabulary

  • setting: When and where a story takes place.
  • conflict: The problem or struggle a character faces. External conflict is against someone or something outside; internal conflict is inside the character's own mind.
  • exposition: The beginning of a plot, where the setting, characters and situation are introduced.
  • rising action: The events that build tension as the character tries to solve the problem.
  • climax: The turning point, when the tension is highest and the conflict is faced.
  • falling action: The events right after the climax.
  • resolution: How the story ends and the conflict is settled.
  • sensory details: Words that describe what a character sees, hears, smells, tastes or touches.
  • rubric: A scoring guide that lists the traits of the work and describes each level of quality.
  • graph / scrib, script: Greek and Latin roots that mean write, as in autograph, biography, describe and manuscript.

Lesson flow

5 minReconnect
Run the Reconnect questions. Listen for whether the student uses terms such as protagonist, adjective and theme confidently.
5 minHook
Ask the student to tell a short family story out loud. Point out that it already has a setting, a character and a problem.
15 minRead The Cast Net
Student reads silently, then you read paragraphs 3 to 8 aloud. Second read: highlight details about Mateo's feelings in one color and sensory details in another.
10 minPlot mountain
Sketch the mountain together and place the story's events on it. Discuss why paragraph 8, not 7, is the climax.
10 minShow, don't tell, and roots
Model one telling-to-showing rewrite, then have the student do one out loud. Introduce the four roots with the word-building table; challenge the student to build one new word from two roots.
12 minHow rubrics work
Walk through each trait row. Score the sample paragraph together, trait by trait, and have the student justify each score with words from the rubric.
3 minAssign
Explain the story mountain activity and the opening paragraph; both feed the Week 5 personal narrative.

Key questions to ask

  • What clues tell you how Mateo feels before the writer ever names a feeling?
  • Why do you think the writer included the boys who laugh?
  • Is the bigger conflict the net or Mateo's doubt? Defend your answer.
  • What does Abuelo mean when he says the net will teach you if you pay attention?
  • How would you score this paragraph, and what words in the rubric back up your score?

Common mistakes to watch for

  • Confusing the climax with the most exciting-sounding sentence instead of the turning point of the conflict. Some students will argue for paragraph 7, when Mateo figures out his mistake; accept the argument if they support it, and discuss why the net opening is the moment the conflict is resolved.
  • Thinking a conflict must be a fight; conflicts can be against nature, a skill or oneself.
  • Treating a rubric as a grade after the fact rather than a planning tool.
  • Writing showing sentences that still name the feeling (I was nervous and my hands shook).

Support (if the student is struggling)

  • Read the story aloud together first, then reread for details.
  • Provide sentence starters: The setting is... The problem is... I know this because the text says...
  • Offer a short list of feeling words and matching body-language details for the showing practice.

Challenge (if the student is ready for more)

  • Ask the student to rewrite paragraph 3 from the point of view of one of the boys on the pier (preview of Week 2).
  • Have the student find a fifth root in a book they are reading and teach it to you.

Background for the teacher

The story is set at Charlotte Harbor in Southwest Florida. Cast-net fishing for small baitfish such as pinfish is common on Florida piers. Veracruz is a coastal state in Mexico with a long fishing tradition. A useful connection: scribes in Kemet (ancient Egypt) appear in Social Studies in Unit 2, and the root scrib links the two subjects.

How the lesson runs

During the live lesson, the student shares their screen and answers the Reconnect questions and the Learn checks live while you teach. The Hands-On activity, Think Deeper and Exit Check are completed after class.

Scoring

The interactive lesson scores itself: full points on the first try, half points on the second try, then the answer and explanation appear. Mastery is 85. Written responses arrive in your results email with the score; score them with the rubric below before the student moves on.

Answer key: interactive lesson

Reconnect

  1. What do we call the main person or animal a story is about?
    Answer: main character
    The main character, also called the protagonist, is the one the story follows.
  2. Name the part of speech of each underlined word in: The tired dog slept quietly .
    Answer: adjective / verb / adverb
    Tired describes the noun dog (adjective). Slept is the action (verb). Quietly tells how he slept (adverb).
  3. Read: The cliff was so precipitous that climbers needed ropes just to stand near the edge. Using context clues, what does precipitous mean?
    Answer: very steep
    Climbers needing ropes to stand near the edge tells you the cliff is extremely steep.
  4. Is this a complete sentence or a fragment? Running down the hall after the bell rang. Type sentence or fragment .
    Answer: fragment
    It has no subject and no complete verb (running needs a helper such as was), so it is a fragment. Fix it: Maya ran down the hall after the bell rang.
  5. A story told by a character using I and me is told from what point of view?
    Answer: first person
    First person uses I, me and we. Third person uses he, she and they.
  6. What is the lesson or message about life that a story teaches called?
    Answer: theme
    A theme is the message about life a story shows. You will study theme closely in Week 3.

Learn 1: Read: The Cast Net

  1. Where and when does the story take place? (setting)
    Answer: A pier at Charlotte Harbor, early in the morning
    Paragraph 1: the pier at Charlotte Harbor at six in the morning.
  2. Name the main character's problem in a few words. (conflict)
    Answer: He cannot throw the cast net so it opens.
    Mateo's net keeps landing in a tangled lump instead of opening.
  3. Mateo also has an internal conflict, a struggle inside his own mind. What is he starting to believe about himself in paragraph 5?
    Answer: That he is just not a fisherman (he wants to give up).
    "Maybe I'm just not a fisherman" shows doubt inside his own mind.
  4. In which paragraph number does the climax happen, when everything turns around?
    Answer: 8
    In paragraph 8 the net finally spins open. Paragraph 7 is the last step of the rising action, when he figures out what to change.

Learn 2: The Plot Mountain

  1. Put these events from the story in plot order.
    Answer: Abuelo shows Mateo how to hold the net → The older boys snicker at a bad throw → Abuelo tells about his summer in Veracruz → The net spins open in a perfect circle → Mateo grins when Abuelo says "Again"
    Exposition, rising action, rising action, climax, resolution.
  2. The last word Abuelo says is the same word he said in paragraph 4: Again. Why does the word feel different at the end? Explain in two sentences.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: At first, again meant try once more after failing; At the end, Mateo has succeeded, so again is a challenge he welcomes; Uses evidence such as Mateo grinning

Learn 3: How Writers Show Feelings

  1. Paragraph 1 describes the net with a sense other than sight. What does the net smell like?
    Answer: salt and old rope
    "It smelled like salt and old rope" is a smell detail that puts you on the pier.
  2. Find the simile in paragraph 3 that describes the bad throw. Type the thing the net is compared to.
    Answer: a wet towel
    "The net flopped into the water like a wet towel" compares the net to a towel using like.
  3. Rewrite this telling sentence as a showing sentence: Keisha was excited. Do not use the word excited.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Shows the feeling through actions, body language or speech; Does not name the feeling; Specific details the reader can picture

Learn 4: Word Roots: Writing and Life

  1. Using the roots, what is an autobiography ?
    Answer: The story of a person's life written by that same person.
    auto (self) + bio (life) + graph (write) = a life story written by oneself.
  2. Use a root word to complete each sentence.
    Answer: autograph / biography / biology
    Autograph (self + write), biography (life + write), biology (life + study).
  3. A scribe worked in ancient Kemet (Egypt). Based on the root, what was a scribe's job?
    Answer: Writing and keeping records
    Scrib means write. Scribes wrote and kept records, and you will meet them in Social Studies.

Learn 5: How Rubrics Work

  1. Score the sample paragraph for Evidence . How many exact quotations from the story does it use?
    Answer: 2
    It quotes "Maybe I'm just not a fisherman" and "Again." Each one is explained.
  2. Using the rubric, what Evidence score (1 to 4) does the sample earn?
    Answer: 4
    Two or more exact details, each explained, is a 4.
  3. Which rubric trait checks spelling, capital letters and punctuation?
    Answer: conventions
    Conventions are the rules of written English.
  4. Why is it smart to read a rubric before you write instead of after?
    Answer: So you know what excellent work looks like and can plan to include it.
    A rubric is a map of the goal. Reading it first lets you plan to hit every trait.

Hands-On

  1. Type the five events from your story mountain in order, labeled with the plot part.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Completes every step of the activity; Includes specific details, numbers or observations from the activity; Explains thinking in complete sentences
  2. Who listened to your story, and which part did they want to hear more about? How will you add detail there?
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Completes every step of the activity; Includes specific details, numbers or observations from the activity; Explains thinking in complete sentences

Think Deeper

  1. Write the opening paragraph (exposition) of your personal narrative. Put the reader in the setting with at least two sensory details, introduce yourself as a character, and hint at the problem coming.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Setting is clear: where and when; At least two sensory details beyond sight; First-person point of view (I, me); Hints at the conflict without giving away the climax; Complete sentences with correct capitals and punctuation
    Model answer: The waiting room at the hospital smelled like hand sanitizer and burnt coffee, and the plastic chair stuck to the backs of my legs. I was eight, holding a juice box I was too nervous to drink, and every time the big doors swung open I jumped. I did not know yet that by the end of that night, I would be somebody's big sister.
  2. Score your own paragraph with the rubric. Give yourself a 1 to 4 for Ideas and for Conventions, and explain one thing you would change to move up a level.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Gives a score for each trait; Explains the score with a reason; Names one specific improvement

Exit Check

  1. Plot parts.
    Answer: climax / exposition / resolution
    Climax, exposition, resolution.
  2. A character arguing with a friend has what kind of conflict: internal or external ?
    Answer: external
    The struggle is with another person, outside the character's mind.
  3. A character deciding whether to tell the truth has what kind of conflict?
    Answer: internal
    The struggle happens inside the character's own mind.
  4. What does the root bio mean?
    Answer: life
    Bio means life: biology, biography, biome.
  5. Using roots, what does inscription most likely mean?
    Answer: Words written or carved onto something
    In (on) + script (write): words written or carved on something, like a monument.
  6. Rewrite as a showing sentence: The room was messy. Do not use the word messy.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Specific details the reader can picture; Does not use the word messy
  7. On a rubric, what does the top level (4) describe?
    Answer: Work that exceeds the goal (the strongest work)
    Level 4 describes work that goes beyond the goal.

Answer key: printable page

  1. Describe the setting of The Cast Net in one sentence. Include where and when.
    Answer: The story takes place on a pier at Charlotte Harbor early in the morning.
  2. What is Mateo's external conflict? What is his internal conflict?
    Answer: External: he cannot make the net open. Internal: he doubts himself and thinks he is not a fisherman.
  3. Copy one sentence that shows Mateo is embarrassed without using the word embarrassed. Explain how it shows the feeling.
    Answer: "Mateo's ears burned" or "stared at the boards so he would not have to look at anyone": both are physical reactions to being laughed at.
  4. Label each event: E (exposition), RA (rising action), C (climax), FA (falling action) or R (resolution). ___ The net comes up full of pinfish. ___ Abuelo explains how to hold the net. ___ The net spins open. ___ The boys snicker.
    Answer: FA, E, C, RA
  5. Write the meaning of each root and one word that uses it: graph ____ bio ____ auto ____ script ____
    Answer: graph = write (paragraph); bio = life (biology); auto = self (automatic); script = write (manuscript)
  6. Change this telling sentence into a showing sentence: The test was hard.
    Answer: Answers vary; must use specific details or actions, e.g., I erased the same answer three times and my brain felt like static.
  7. What lesson about learning does Mateo discover? Support your answer with one quotation.
    Answer: Patience and paying attention matter more than talent; for example, Abuelo says the net would teach him if he paid attention, and Mateo succeeds after he slows down.
