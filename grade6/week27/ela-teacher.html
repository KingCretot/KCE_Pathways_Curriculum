Unit 6: Research and Presentation · Week 27 · ELA · Teacher Plan

Judging Sources and Spotting Misinformation

Lesson length: 60 minutes live, plus the after-class interactive lesson

Objectives

  • Evaluate a source's reliability using its author, purpose, evidence, date and publisher.
  • Use lateral reading and the SIFT moves to check a source or claim.
  • Distinguish misinformation from disinformation and identify common persuasion tricks.
  • Check images and AI-generated content before trusting them.
  • Choose and justify reliable sources for your research question.

Standards

  • ELA.6.C.4.1: Conduct research to answer a question, drawing on multiple reliable and valid sources, and refocusing the inquiry when appropriate.
  • ELA.K12.EE.1.1: Cite evidence to explain and justify reasoning.

Materials

  • The interactive lesson
  • A computer or tablet with internet access (parent supervision)
  • A timer
  • The student's research question and sources
  • Printable practice page

Vocabulary

  • reliable: Trustworthy because it is accurate, well supported and from a knowledgeable source.
  • misinformation: False or misleading information shared without the intent to deceive.
  • disinformation: False information spread on purpose to deceive.
  • lateral reading: Checking a source by leaving it and searching what other sources say about it.
  • bias: A one-sided point of view that can affect how information is presented.
  • primary source: An original source created at the time, such as data, a speech or an eyewitness account.

Key questions to ask

  • Who made this, and why?
  • What do other sources say about it?
  • Where did this claim, quote or image first appear?
  • How does this post want me to feel?

Common mistakes to watch for

  • Lots of shares means it's true.
  • A professional-looking site must be reliable.
  • .org sites are always trustworthy.
  • If an AI tool says it, it must have a source.

Support (if the student is struggling)

  • Provide the five-question checklist as a bookmark.
  • Model one lateral search aloud.
  • Pair the student with a parent for the online challenge.

Challenge (if the student is ready for more)

  • Find a real correction published by a news outlet and explain what it shows about reliability.
  • Compare how two news outlets with different viewpoints cover the same Florida story.

Background for the teacher

Practice claim answers: (1) false, since manatees were reclassified from endangered to threatened in 2017 and remain federally threatened; (2) partly true: Texas records the most total lightning because of its size, while Florida has the highest lightning density; a given year can vary; (3) false, per NASA; (4) mostly true, since properly sealed honey keeps for very long periods. The springs post is invented for the lesson. The Stanford History Education Group study (Wineburg and McGrew, 2017) compared fact-checkers, historians and students. SIFT was developed by Mike Caulfield.

Sources

  • Caulfield, Mike, and Sam Wineburg, Verified: How to Think Straight, Get Duped Less, and Make Better Decisions about What to Believe Online (2023).
  • Wineburg, Sam, and Sarah McGrew, "Lateral Reading: Reading Less and Learning More When Evaluating Digital Information," Stanford History Education Group working paper (2017).
  • News Literacy Project, Checkology resources.

How the lesson runs

During the live lesson, the student shares their screen and answers the Reconnect questions and the Learn checks live while you teach. The Hands-On activity, Think Deeper and Exit Check are completed after class.

Scoring

The interactive lesson scores itself: full points on the first try, half points on the second try, then the answer and explanation appear. Mastery is 85. Written responses arrive in your results email with the score; score them with the rubric below before the student moves on.

Answer key: interactive lesson

Reconnect

  1. Write your Unit 6 research question from last week.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: A focused, open research question
    Model answer: Why do algae blooms keep spreading from Lake Okeechobee, and what could stop them?
  2. What do we call smaller questions that help answer your main research question?
    Answer: sub-questions
    Plan three to five.
  3. Name one source you already found for your question. What makes you think it is trustworthy, or what makes you unsure?
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Names a source; Gives a reason for trust or doubt
    Model answer: I found a page from the South Florida Water Management District. I trust it because it's a government agency that measures the lake, but I'm not sure how it handles blame for pollution.

Learn 1: What Makes a Source Reliable

  1. Rank these sources for the question "How many manatees live in Florida?" from most to least reliable, and explain your top and bottom choices: (a) a fan video titled "I swam with 1,000 manatees!", (b) the Florida Fish and Wildlife Conservation Commission's manatee survey, (c) a 2009 encyclopedia, (d) a news article quoting the survey.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: A sensible ranking; Explains the top choice with reliability criteria; Explains the bottom choice
    Model answer: Most to least: (b), (d), (c), (a). The FWC survey is the original data from scientists who count manatees. The news article is good but secondhand. The encyclopedia is reliable but out of date. The fan video is entertainment with an exaggerated title and no method.
  2. A website about the health benefits of orange juice is run by an orange juice company. Does that make it false? How should a researcher use it?
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Recognizes the bias or purpose without assuming it's false; Explains checking its claims against independent sources
    Model answer: It isn't automatically false, but the company wants to sell juice, so it might leave out downsides like sugar. A researcher should check its claims against independent sources, like a university nutrition department or government health site.

Learn 2: Reading Laterally: How Fact-Checkers Work

  1. Put the SIFT moves in order.
    Answer: Stop → Investigate the source → Find better coverage → Trace claims to the original
    Stop first, then check outward.
  2. Use the SIFT moves to describe exactly what you would do to check the springs post in the hook. Name a real kind of source you'd use for the "Find" step.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Applies all four moves to the post; Names a reliable kind of source for checking springs
    Model answer: Stop: the caps and "share before it's deleted" are warning signs, so I won't share. Investigate: search the account name to see who runs it. Find: check what the Florida Department of Environmental Protection and university scientists say about spring flow. Trace: look for the "one study" and see what it actually said, and do a reverse image search on the photo to see where it really came from.

Learn 3: Misinformation Tricks

  1. Name three misinformation tricks used in the springs post, quoting the words that show each one.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Three different tricks named; A quotation from the post for each
    Model answer: Emotional hook: "SHOCKING!!!" Fake authority: "Scientists CONFIRM" and "One study proves it" with no names. Urgency: "SHARE before this gets deleted!!!"
  2. Your grandmother shares the springs post because she's worried about the springs. The account that created it makes money from clicks. Which person is spreading misinformation and which is spreading disinformation? Explain.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Correctly labels each person; Explains the difference
    Model answer: Grandma is spreading misinformation, because she believes it and means well. The account is spreading disinformation if it knows the claim is exaggerated and posts it to get clicks and money. The difference is whether they intend to deceive.

Learn 4: Images and AI-Generated Content

  1. An AI chatbot tells you, "A 2021 University of Florida study found that springs lose 2 inches of water a year," with a link. What should you do before using this in your research, and why?
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Says what to do before using the claim; Explains why that step matters; Mentions checking the details match
    Model answer: I should click the link and find the actual study to make sure it exists, that the University of Florida really published it, and that it says exactly that. AI tools sometimes invent studies and numbers that sound real, so I can't cite something I haven't checked.

Hands-On

  1. Report your verdict and best source for each of the four practice claims. Which claim surprised you, and which SIFT move helped most?
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Four verdicts with sources; Reliable sources chosen; Reflects on a surprise and the most useful move
    Model answer: 1. False: the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service reclassified manatees from endangered to threatened in 2017, so they are still listed as threatened. 2. Partly true: Texas gets the most lightning in total because it is so large, but Florida gets the most lightning per square mile (Vaisala Xweather lightning report; National Weather Service). 3. False: NASA says you can't see the Great Wall from the Moon. 4. Mostly true: archaeologists found honey that had not spoiled in ancient Egyptian tombs, though it can spoil if water gets in. The manatee claim surprised me; "Find better coverage" helped most.
  2. Share your source scorecard for two research sources, with the score for each criterion. Did you replace a source? Explain why or why not.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Two sources scored on all five criteria; Totals calculated; A justified decision about keeping or replacing
    Model answer: Source 1, South Florida Water Management District: who 3, why 2, evidence 3, when 3, where 3 = 14. Source 2, a blog called Florida Fishing Fun: who 1, why 1, evidence 1, when 2, where 1 = 6. I replaced the blog with a Florida Sea Grant fact sheet from the University of Florida, which scored 14.

Think Deeper

  1. Research shows false news often spreads faster online than true news. Explain at least two reasons why, and describe what you personally will do differently before sharing something.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Two reasonable explanations; A specific personal plan
    Model answer: False stories are often more shocking and emotional than the truth, so people share them before thinking, and they're designed to spread, with urgency like "share before it's deleted." Also, people tend to share things that match what they already believe. Before I share anything, I'll stop, search the source and see whether at least two reliable sources agree.
  2. Historians in Units 4 and 5 had to judge sources too: Herodotus, al-Bakri, Ibn Battuta, Leo Africanus. Choose one and explain how the questions about who, why, evidence, when and where would apply to it.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Accurate details about the chosen source; Applies at least four of the five questions
    Model answer: Leo Africanus: who, a well-traveled scholar from North Africa; why, to describe Africa for European readers; evidence, his own visit to Timbuktu about 1510, so firsthand but from memory; when, written years after the visit; where, published in Italian and later translated into English in 1600, so some words may have changed. Like a modern website, it's useful but needs checking against other sources.

Exit Check

  1. What is the name for checking a source by leaving it and searching what others say about it?
    Answer: lateral reading
    Fact-checkers do it first.
  2. What is the difference between misinformation and disinformation?
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Explains the difference between the two terms
    Model answer: Misinformation is false information shared by mistake; disinformation is false information spread on purpose to deceive.
  3. Name two red flags that a post might be misinformation.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Two valid red flags
    Model answer: All-caps emotional words and pressure to share right away.

Answer key: printable page

  1. List the five questions for judging a source.
    Answer: Who made it? Why? What evidence? When? Where is it published?
  2. What do the letters in SIFT stand for?
    Answer: Stop, Investigate the source, Find better coverage, Trace claims to the original.
  3. Name three misinformation tricks.
    Answer: Emotional hooks, urgency, fake authority, exaggeration, out-of-context images, us-versus-them.
  4. Why should you never cite a source you haven't opened yourself?
    Answer: It might not exist, might say something different, or might be invented by AI.
  5. Rate one of your research sources on the five questions and decide whether to keep it.
    Answer: Model: South Florida Water Management District: who 3, why 2, evidence 3, when 3, where 3 = 14 of 15, so I keep it.
