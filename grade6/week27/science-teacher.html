Unit 6: Heredity and Adaptation · Week 27 · Life Science · Teacher Plan

Punnett Squares

Lesson length: 60 minutes live, plus the after-class interactive lesson

Objectives

  • Explain how Mendel's pea plant experiments revealed dominant and recessive traits.
  • Use genotype and phenotype, homozygous and heterozygous correctly.
  • Complete Punnett squares for single-gene crosses and state outcomes as ratios, fractions and percents.
  • Explain the difference between a predicted probability and actual results.
  • Recognize that many traits involve more than one gene or incomplete dominance.

Standards

  • SC.7.L.16.2: Determine the probabilities for genotype and phenotype combinations using Punnett Squares and pedigrees.
  • SC.7.L.16.1: Understand and explain that every organism requires a set of instructions that specifies its traits, that this hereditary information (DNA) contains genes located in the chromosomes of each cell, and that heredity is the passage of these instructions from one generation to another.

Materials

  • The interactive lesson
  • Two coins, masking tape and a marker
  • Printable practice page

Vocabulary

  • genotype: The alleles an organism has for a gene, written with letters such as PP, Pp or pp.
  • phenotype: The trait that actually shows, such as purple or white flowers.
  • homozygous: Having two identical alleles for a gene (PP or pp).
  • heterozygous: Having two different alleles for a gene (Pp); also called a carrier for a recessive trait.
  • Punnett square: A grid used to predict the possible genotypes of offspring from two parents.
  • probability: The chance that an event will happen, written as a fraction, ratio or percent.

Key questions to ask

  • What alleles can each parent pass on?
  • What does each box stand for?
  • Is this a genotype or a phenotype?
  • Why didn't the results match the prediction exactly?

Common mistakes to watch for

  • A Punnett square tells exactly how many offspring will have a trait.
  • Dominant means more common.
  • Heterozygous organisms show a mix of both traits (only in incomplete dominance).
  • Every trait is controlled by one gene.

Support (if the student is struggling)

  • Provide blank Punnett square templates.
  • Color-code each parent's alleles.
  • Use the coins before drawing squares.

Challenge (if the student is ready for more)

  • Predict the results of a cross involving two genes (a dihybrid cross) for pea seed shape and color.
  • Research the inheritance of sickle cell trait and why carriers are protected against malaria.

Background for the teacher

Mendel published in 1866 (Proceedings of the Natural History Society of Brünn); his work was rediscovered in 1900 by Hugo de Vries, Carl Correns and Erich von Tschermak. Flower color counts: 705 purple and 224 white in the F2 generation. Reginald Punnett introduced the square around 1905. Black (B) and chocolate (b) in Labradors also depend on a second gene (E/e) for yellow; the example assumes the dogs are not yellow. Florida places Punnett squares in Grade 7 (SC.7.L.16.2); KCE teaches them here per the Grade 6 scope.

Sources

  • Mendel, Gregor, Experiments on Plant Hybridization (1866), English translation by William Bateson (1901).
  • National Human Genome Research Institute, Punnett Square.

Answer key: interactive lesson

Reconnect

  1. What do we call the different forms of a gene?
    Answer: alleles
    Such as purple or white.
  2. How many alleles for each gene does an offspring receive from each parent?
    Answer: 1
    One from each parent, two in all.
  3. An allele whose trait shows when at least one copy is present is called ___.
    Answer: dominant
    A recessive trait needs two copies.
  4. In last week's bead babies, about what fraction of babies showed the recessive trait? Did every family get exactly the same result?
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Reports a fraction near one-fourth; Notes that results varied by chance
    Model answer: About one-fourth of my babies were dd. My results weren't exactly one-fourth, and other families got slightly different numbers because each draw was random.

Learn 1: Genotype and Phenotype

  1. For pea flower color (P = purple, p = white), give the phenotype of each genotype.
    Answer: purple; purple; white
    Only two recessive alleles show white.
  2. Two purple pea plants look exactly alike, but one is PP and one is Pp. How could a scientist find out which is which without a DNA test?
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Cross each with a white (pp) plant or breed them; Explains that only the Pp plant can produce white offspring
    Model answer: Cross each purple plant with a white (pp) plant. The PP plant can only pass on P, so all its offspring will be purple. The Pp plant can pass on p, so about half its offspring will be white.

Learn 2: Making a Punnett Square

  1. Cross Pp × pp. How many of the four boxes have the genotype pp?
    Answer: 2
    The pp parent always gives p; the Pp parent gives P or p, so two boxes are Pp and two are pp.
  2. In the Pp × pp cross, what percent of offspring are expected to have white flowers?
    Answer: 50 percent
    2 of 4 boxes are pp.
  3. In a PP × pp cross, what percent of offspring are expected to be purple?
    Answer: 100 percent
    Every box is Pp.

Learn 3: Probability Is Not a Promise

  1. Mendel counted 705 purple and 224 white flowers. To the nearest tenth, how many purple flowers were there for each white one?
    Answer: 3.1
    705 ÷ 224 ≈ 3.15, close to 3 : 1.
  2. Two dogs, both carriers (Bb) of a recessive coat color, have 4 puppies. The Punnett square predicts 1 in 4 with the recessive color. Their litter has 2 puppies with the recessive color. Did the Punnett square make a mistake? Explain.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Says the Punnett square was not wrong; Explains probability for each puppy and small numbers
    Model answer: No. The square gives each puppy a 1 in 4 chance, but each puppy is a separate chance, like a coin flip. In a small litter of 4, getting 2 is possible. With hundreds of puppies, the results would get close to one-fourth.

Learn 4: Beyond Simple Dominance

  1. Red snapdragons crossed with white snapdragons produce pink flowers. What is this pattern called?
    Answer: incomplete dominance
    Neither allele fully hides the other.
  2. Why do humans show a wide range of heights instead of just "tall" and "short" like Mendel's pea plants?
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Height depends on many genes; Environment (nutrition, health) also affects it
    Model answer: Human height depends on hundreds of genes, each adding a little, plus nutrition and health while growing. All those combinations make a smooth range instead of two types.

Hands-On

  1. Show your results for 20, 50 and 100 offspring (purple and white counts and percent white). How did the percent white compare with 25 percent as the number grew?
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Results at all three sizes; Percent white calculated; A conclusion about larger samples
    Model answer: 20 flips: 17 purple, 3 white (15 percent white). 50 flips: 36 purple, 14 white (28 percent). 100 combined: 74 purple, 26 white (26 percent). The more offspring, the closer I got to 25 percent, just like Mendel counting thousands of plants.
  2. How is the coin model like real inheritance, and how is it different?
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: One way it matches (each parent gives one allele by chance); One difference (real traits can involve many genes, real cells use meiosis, coins can't model environment)
    Model answer: Like real inheritance, each parent gives one of two alleles by chance, just as each coin lands on one side. But real traits often involve many genes and the environment, and the coin can't show incomplete dominance or the actual process of making sex cells.

Think Deeper

  1. A dog breeder has a black Labrador (Bb) and a chocolate Labrador (bb). Black (B) is dominant. Draw the Punnett square in words, give the genotype and phenotype ratios, and explain what the breeder should tell a family hoping for a chocolate puppy.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Correct Punnett square; Correct ratios (1 Bb : 1 bb; 1 black : 1 chocolate); Explains probability honestly to the family
    Model answer: Across the top: B and b. Down the side: b and b. Boxes: Bb, Bb, bb, bb. Genotypes: 2 Bb : 2 bb (1 : 1). Phenotypes: 1 black : 1 chocolate. The breeder should say each puppy has a 50 percent chance of being chocolate, so a litter will probably include some chocolate puppies, but it isn't guaranteed.
  2. Mendel's work was ignored for 34 years. Suggest two reasons why, and explain what his story teaches about how science works.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Two plausible reasons; A lesson about science (evidence, communication, replication)
    Model answer: He published in a little-read journal, and many scientists of his time didn't use math to study living things, so his counts and ratios seemed strange. His story shows that good ideas need evidence that others can repeat and communicate; when scientists repeated his results in 1900, the idea finally took hold.

Exit Check

  1. The trait that actually shows, such as white flowers, is the ___.
    Answer: phenotype
    The letters are the genotype.
  2. In a Bb × Bb cross, what percent of offspring are expected to show the recessive trait?
    Answer: 25 percent
    1 of 4 boxes is bb.
  3. A plant's genotype for height is TT, Tt or tt. How many of these three genotypes are heterozygous?
    Answer: 1
    Only Tt has two different alleles.
  4. Why doesn't a Punnett square tell you exactly how many offspring will have a trait?
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Explains that it shows probability for each offspring
    Model answer: It shows the chance for each offspring, and each one is a separate chance, so actual results vary, especially in small numbers.

Answer key: printable page

  1. Define genotype, phenotype, homozygous and heterozygous.
    Answer: Genotype: allele letters; phenotype: the trait shown; homozygous: two identical alleles; heterozygous: two different alleles.
  2. Complete a Punnett square for Tt × Tt (T = tall). Give the genotype and phenotype ratios.
    Answer: TT, Tt, Tt, tt; 1 : 2 : 1; 3 tall : 1 short.
  3. Complete a Punnett square for Tt × tt. What percent are expected to be short?
    Answer: Tt, Tt, tt, tt; 50 percent short.
  4. Why did Mendel study thousands of plants?
    Answer: Large numbers get closer to the predicted ratio; small numbers vary by chance.
  5. Give one example of a trait that doesn't follow simple dominance.
    Answer: Pink snapdragons (incomplete dominance), roan cattle or AB blood (codominance), human height (many genes).
