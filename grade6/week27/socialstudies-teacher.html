Unit 6: The Americas and the Meeting of Worlds · Week 27 · World and American History · Teacher Plan

The Mexica (Aztec), the Inca and Cahokia

Lesson length: 60 minutes live, plus the after-class interactive lesson

Objectives

  • Explain how the Mexica built Tenochtitlan and governed through tribute.
  • Explain how the Inca organized a huge empire using roads, the mit'a labor system and quipus.
  • Describe Cahokia and the Mississippian culture of North America.
  • Analyze a primary source describing Tenochtitlan, considering its author's point of view.
  • Compare how the three societies adapted to their environments and organized power.

Standards

  • SS.6.G.2.1: Explain how major physical characteristics, natural resources, climate, and absolute and relative locations have influenced settlement, interactions, and the economies of ancient civilizations of the world.
  • SS.6.E.3.4: Describe the relationship among civilizations that engage in trade, including the benefits and drawbacks of voluntary trade.
  • SS.6.W.1.3: Interpret primary and secondary sources.
  • SS.6.W.1.6: Describe how history transmits culture and heritage and provides models of human character.

Materials

  • The interactive lesson
  • Yarn or string: one 2-foot main cord and 6 to 8 one-foot cords in two or three colors
  • Printable practice page

Vocabulary

  • Mexica: The people, often called Aztecs, who founded Tenochtitlan and led the Triple Alliance in central Mexico.
  • chinampa: A raised farm field built in shallow lake water from mud and plants.
  • tribute: Payments of goods or labor that conquered peoples must give to a ruling power.
  • Inca: The rulers of Tawantinsuyu, an empire along the Andes Mountains of South America.
  • quipu: An Inca recording device of knotted cords used to track numbers and possibly other information.
  • Mississippian culture: Farming societies along the Mississippi and its rivers that built large earthen mounds, about 800 to 1600 CE.

Key questions to ask

  • How did geography shape this society?
  • How did rulers hold such large territories?
  • Who wrote this source, and when?
  • What evidence shows these were connected, complex societies?

Common mistakes to watch for

  • The Americas had no cities before Europeans arrived.
  • Aztec is what the Mexica called themselves.
  • The Inca had no way to keep records.
  • A small number of Spanish soldiers conquered empires alone (they had many Indigenous allies, and disease devastated the population).

Support (if the student is struggling)

  • Use a three-column chart: Mexica, Inca, Cahokia.
  • Practice reading the quipu diagram together.
  • Pre-teach chinampa, tribute and quipu.

Challenge (if the student is ready for more)

  • Research the Codex Mendoza's tribute pages and what they show about Mexica tribute.
  • Find out how researchers are trying to decode non-number information in quipus.

Background for the teacher

Dates and figures: Tenochtitlan founded 1325 (traditional date); Triple Alliance 1428; population estimates range from about 150,000 to 300,000; fall of Tenochtitlan August 13, 1521. Pachacuti's expansion began about 1438; estimates of the Inca population range from about 6 to 14 million; road network estimates about 40,000 km (25,000 mi); the Qhapaq Ñan became a UNESCO World Heritage Site in 2014; Atahualpa was captured in 1532 and executed in 1533. Cahokia's population estimates range from about 10,000 to 20,000 at the core (some estimates higher); Monks Mound is about 30 m tall; UNESCO 1982. Nahuatl speakers about 1.7 million (INEGI); Quechua speakers estimates vary widely (about 8 to 10 million). The Bernal Díaz quotation is from the 1844 Lockhart translation (Project Gutenberg eBook #32474), checked against the text. The Americas before 1492 are outside Florida's Grade 6 NGSSS world history range; the standards above cover the skills taught.

Answer key: interactive lesson

Reconnect

  1. What are the "three sisters" crops? Name all three.
    Answer: maize, beans and squash
    They fed the Maya, the Mexica and Cahokia.
  2. In Maya numbers, what is 2 bars and 4 dots?
    Answer: 14
    10 + 4 = 14.
  3. What were the Maya rulers of each city-state called (in English)?
    Answer: holy lords (k'uhul ajaw)
    Each city-state had its own.
  4. The Maya never united into one empire. Predict one advantage an empire might have over separate city-states, and one challenge it would face.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: One advantage; One challenge
    Model answer: An empire could gather resources and soldiers from a huge area, but it would be hard to control distant peoples who resented paying it.

Learn 1: The Mexica and Tenochtitlan

  1. What were the raised farm fields built in Lake Texcoco called?
    Answer: chinampas
    They allowed several harvests a year.
  2. The Mexica let conquered peoples keep their rulers but demanded tribute. Give one advantage and one danger of this system for the Mexica.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: One advantage; One danger
    Model answer: An advantage was that the Mexica didn't need to govern every town directly, yet they received huge wealth. The danger was that heavy tribute made conquered peoples resent them, so many, like the Tlaxcalans and others, were willing to join the Spanish against Tenochtitlan.
  3. Reread Bernal Díaz's description. What does it tell us about Tenochtitlan, and what should a historian remember about who wrote it and when?
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: What the source shows about the city; A consideration about the author's point of view or timing
    Model answer: It shows Tenochtitlan was enormous and grand, with tall stone temples rising from the lake, so impressive the Spanish thought it was a dream. A historian should remember Díaz was a conquistador writing decades later, so his memory may have changed, and he wrote partly to defend the conquest.

Learn 2: The Inca: An Empire on the Mountains

  1. Read the blue quipu cord: 1 knot in the hundreds place, none in the tens and 3 in the ones place. What number is it?
    Answer: 103
    100 + 0 + 3 = 103.
  2. Explain how the Inca road system helped them rule such a large, mountainous empire. Give two ways.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Two specific ways the roads helped govern
    Model answer: The roads let armies move quickly to put down rebellions, and chasqui runners carried messages and quipu records so rulers in Cusco knew what was happening across the empire. They also moved food from storehouses to places with bad harvests.
  3. Compare the Inca mit'a labor system with paying taxes in money. Why might the Inca have used labor instead of coins?
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Explains what mit'a was; A reasonable reason for labor instead of money
    Model answer: In mit'a, families paid the state with work, like building roads, instead of money. The Inca didn't use coins or markets the way Europeans did, and they needed huge amounts of labor for roads, terraces and storehouses, so work was the most useful payment.

Learn 3: Cahokia: A City of Mounds

  1. What is the name of the largest earthen mound in the Americas, at Cahokia?
    Answer: Monks Mound
    About 100 feet tall.
  2. Archaeologists found Great Lakes copper, Gulf of Mexico shells and Appalachian mica at Cahokia. What does this evidence show?
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Concludes there were long-distance trade networks; Connects to Cahokia's importance or wealth
    Model answer: It shows Cahokia was the center of a trade network reaching across much of North America, so it was a wealthy, important city that people traveled long distances to trade with.

Learn 4: Three Societies, Three Environments

  1. Choose two of the three societies and compare how each changed its environment to grow food.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Two societies with accurate farming methods; A comparison
    Model answer: The Mexica built chinampas, piling lake mud into raised fields in shallow water, while the Inca carved terraces into steep mountainsides. Both turned land that seemed useless for farming into fields that fed huge populations.

Hands-On

  1. What data did you record, what did each color stand for, and could your family member read it correctly? What would make a quipu hard to read without the person who made it?
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Describes the data and color code; Reports whether it was read correctly; Explains a challenge of reading a quipu without its maker
    Model answer: I recorded pages read (red) and minutes of piano practice (blue) for five days. My dad read the numbers correctly once I told him the color code, but he couldn't have known what the colors meant without me. Inca quipu-keepers trained for years, so today many quipus are hard to read because the meanings of colors and patterns were lost.

Think Deeper

  1. Which achievement do you think was most impressive: Tenochtitlan's chinampas and causeways, the Inca road system, or Monks Mound? Make a claim and support it with at least two facts.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: A clear claim; At least two accurate facts; Explains why it is most impressive
    Model answer: The Inca road system was most impressive. The Inca built about 25,000 miles of roads across the Andes, with rope bridges over canyons, and relay runners carried messages up to 150 miles a day. Building that across the world's longest mountain range without iron tools or wheeled carts shows incredible engineering and organization.
  2. Compare the Inca roads with Persia's Royal Road (Week 16) or Rome's roads (Week 18). How did each empire use roads to hold power?
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Accurate details about both road systems; At least one similarity; At least one difference
    Model answer: Like Persia's Royal Road with its relay riders, the Inca had chasqui runners carrying messages quickly, and both empires used roads to move armies and govern distant lands. But Persian riders used horses on mostly flatter ground, while Inca runners carried messages on foot over steep mountain steps and rope bridges.

Exit Check

  1. On what lake did the Mexica build Tenochtitlan?
    Answer: Lake Texcoco
    Founded in 1325.
  2. What knotted-cord device did the Inca use to keep records?
    Answer: the quipu (khipu)
    Base-10 knots.
  3. Near which present-day U.S. city are the Cahokia mounds?
    Answer: St. Louis (Collinsville, Illinois)
    Beside the Mississippi River.
  4. In one or two sentences, explain what tribute was in the Mexica Empire.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Defines tribute as goods or labor paid by conquered peoples to the Mexica
    Model answer: Tribute was the goods, like cloth, cacao and feathers, that conquered peoples had to send to the Mexica as payment for being ruled.

Answer key: printable page

  1. How did the Mexica build a city in a lake and feed its people?
    Answer: Causeways and an aqueduct; chinampas, raised fields of lake mud with several harvests a year.
  2. What was tribute?
    Answer: Goods or labor that conquered peoples paid to the Mexica.
  3. Name three ways the Inca held their empire together.
    Answer: Roads and chasqui runners, the mit'a labor system, quipu records, storehouses.
  4. Read this quipu cord: 3 knots (hundreds), 0 (tens), 7 (ones).
    Answer: 307.
  5. Describe Cahokia and Monks Mound.
    Answer: A Mississippian city near St. Louis, peak about 1050 to 1200; Monks Mound is the largest earthen mound in the Americas.
  6. What should a historian keep in mind when reading Bernal Díaz?
    Answer: He was a conquistador writing decades later, partly to defend the conquest.
