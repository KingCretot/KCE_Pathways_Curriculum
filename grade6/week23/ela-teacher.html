Unit 5: Novel Study · Week 23 · ELA · Teacher Plan

Symbols and Theme

Lesson length: 60 minutes live, plus the after-class interactive lesson

Objectives

  • Explain what a symbol is and analyze how the club, the fire and the trail work as symbols.
  • Distinguish a topic from a theme and write a theme statement as a complete sentence.
  • Trace how a theme develops across chapters using text evidence.
  • Compare characters (François and Perrault versus Hal, Charles and Mercedes) to reveal theme.
  • Use the root anim and the suffix -ity to determine word meanings.

Standards

  • ELA.6.R.1.2: Analyze the development of stated or implied theme(s) throughout a literary text.
  • ELA.6.R.1.1: Analyze how the interaction between characters contributes to the development of a plot in a literary text.
  • ELA.6.V.1.2: Apply knowledge of Greek and Latin roots and affixes to determine meanings of words and phrases in grade-level content.
  • ELA.K12.EE.1.1: Cite evidence to explain and justify reasoning.

Materials

  • The interactive lesson
  • The Call of the Wild, Chapters IV and V
  • Household objects for symbols, poster board, markers, sticky notes
  • Printable practice page

Vocabulary

  • symbol: An object, person or event that stands for a bigger idea.
  • theme: The central message or insight about life that a story reveals.
  • topic: The subject of a story in a word or two, such as survival or loyalty.
  • motif: An image, idea or object that repeats through a story.
  • ancestor: A relative from long ago from whom a person or animal is descended.
  • instinct: Behavior an animal is born knowing, without being taught.
  • foil: A character whose differences highlight another character's qualities.

Key questions to ask

  • What could this object stand for?
  • Is that a topic or a theme?
  • Where else in the book does this idea appear?
  • How does comparing these characters reveal a message?

Common mistakes to watch for

  • A theme is one word.
  • Every object in a story is a symbol.
  • A story has only one theme.
  • The theme must be stated directly by the author.

Support (if the student is struggling)

  • Provide a theme-statement frame: "In [topic], people (or animals) learn that ..."
  • Read Chapters IV and V aloud together or use an audiobook.
  • Use a symbol chart with columns for object, where it appears and meaning.

Challenge (if the student is ready for more)

  • Track the word "call" through the novel and predict what the title means.
  • Compare London's view of instinct with what scientists know about dog behavior today.

Background for the teacher

Content note: Chapter IV describes the veteran dog Dave's illness and death on the trail with a new mail-team driver; Chapter V describes the starvation and beating of the dogs and the deaths of Hal, Charles and Mercedes when the sled breaks through the river ice. The Chapter IV vision passage includes a period label for the cook (not quoted in the lesson); explain to students that the label is outdated and hurtful. Darwin's On the Origin of Species was published in 1859; London read Darwin and Herbert Spencer, and the novel reflects ideas about heredity common in 1903 that modern science has revised.

Sources

  • Jack London, The Call of the Wild (1903), Project Gutenberg.
  • Labor, Earle, Jack London: An American Life (2013).

How the lesson runs

During the live lesson, the student shares their screen and answers the Reconnect questions and the Learn checks live while you teach. The Hands-On activity, Think Deeper and Exit Check are completed after class.

Scoring

The interactive lesson scores itself: full points on the first try, half points on the second try, then the answer and explanation appear. Mastery is 85. Written responses arrive in your results email with the score; score them with the rubric below before the student moves on.

Answer key: interactive lesson

Reconnect

  1. Who was Buck's antagonist in Chapter III?
    Answer: Spitz
    In Chapter IV, Buck takes his place as leader.
  2. Buck against hunger and snow is character versus ___.
    Answer: nature
    Week 22.
  3. London describes Buck's change with two words: "development (or ___)."
    Answer: retrogression
    Moving backward.
  4. In one sentence, describe how Buck has changed from Chapter I to the end of Chapter III.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Names who Buck was in Chapter I; Names what he has become by the end of Chapter III; One clear sentence
    Model answer: Buck has gone from a proud, comfortable estate dog to a tough, cunning survivor who fought Spitz for leadership.

Learn 1: Reading Symbols

  1. A symbol or image that keeps returning through a novel is called a ___.
    Answer: motif
    The club returns in almost every chapter.
  2. In Chapter I the club made Buck obey. In Chapter IV he "was become wise in the way of clubs." How has the club's meaning changed for Buck, and what does that show about him?
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Explains what the club meant to Buck in Chapter I; Explains how Buck responds to the club in Chapter IV, using the quotation; States what the change shows about his character
    Model answer: In Chapter I the club was terrifying and forced Buck to obey. By Chapter IV he understands it so well that he circles just out of reach and refuses to give in until he gets leadership. It shows he has grown cunning and confident; force no longer controls him.

Learn 2: The Fire and the Hairy Man

  1. What does the fire symbolize in this passage? Quote a phrase that supports your answer.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Names a bigger idea the fire stands for, supported by the passage; An accurate quotation from the excerpt
    Model answer: The fire symbolizes Buck's connection to his ancestors and the ancient wild past. London says Buck's instincts are "the memories of his ancestors become habits" that have "become alive again," and the flames seem to be "of another fire" from long ago.
  2. Behaviors an animal is born knowing, without being taught, are called ___.
    Answer: instincts
    London calls them memories of ancestors.

Learn 3: Theme Across a Whole Novel

  1. The topic is survival. Explain how London develops it in Chapter I and how he develops it differently in Chapter V.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: What survival requires in Chapter I, with a detail; What survival requires in Chapter V, with a detail; Explains the difference
    Model answer: In Chapter I, survival means learning to obey the man with the club, because fighting him is hopeless. In Chapter V, survival means knowledge: Buck refuses to pull onto the rotten ice because he senses danger, while Hal, Charles and Mercedes die because they never learned the North.
  2. Rewrite this weak theme statement so it is a strong one: "Buck learns to be tough."
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: A complete sentence about life in general, not naming Buck; Insightful, not a cliché
    Model answer: Hard environments can force us to discover strengths we never knew we had.
  3. Use the suffix -ity.
    Answer: adaptability; cruelty
    -ity means the state or quality of.

Learn 4: Characters as Foils: Chapter V

  1. Compare François and Perrault with Hal, Charles and Mercedes. What theme does the contrast reveal? State it as a complete sentence.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Contrasts the two groups' knowledge and treatment of the dogs with details; States a theme as a complete sentence about life that names no characters
    Model answer: François and Perrault knew the trail, kept order and took care of the dogs' food and feet, while Hal, Charles and Mercedes "did not know how to do anything" and refused to learn. The contrast reveals a theme: knowledge and respect for nature are what keep people alive in a harsh environment, and arrogance can be deadly.
  2. Which man warns about the rotten ice and cuts Buck free?
    Answer: John Thornton
    He becomes the center of Chapter VI.

Hands-On

  1. Describe your three museum objects, what each symbolizes and the quotation you used. Then write your theme statement and list your four pieces of evidence (one from each of four chapters).
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Three symbols with accurate meanings and quotations; A strong theme statement; Four pieces of evidence from four chapters
    Model answer: A stick for the club (force; "a man with a club was a lawgiver"), a red scarf for the man in the red sweater (cruelty and power; "He was beaten (he knew that); but he was not broken"), and a fire picture for Buck's visions (ancestry; "the memories of his ancestors become habits"). Theme: "To survive in a harsh world, you must adapt, even if it means leaving parts of your old self behind." Evidence: Chapter I, Buck learns to obey the club; Chapter II, he steals food to survive; Chapter III, his "primordial beast" grows; Chapter V, Hal and Charles die because they never adapt.

Think Deeper

  1. Write a paragraph explaining one theme of The Call of the Wild so far. State the theme as a complete sentence, support it with at least two quotations from two different chapters, and explain how each quotation develops the theme.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: A clear theme statement; Two accurate quotations from two chapters; Explanation of how each develops the theme; Organized like a T-E-E-C paragraph
    Model answer: One theme of The Call of the Wild is that ignorance and arrogance can be more dangerous than any wilderness. In Chapter II, Buck survives because he learns quickly: his theft of food shows "his adaptability, his capacity to adjust himself to changing conditions, the lack of which would have meant swift and terrible death." Buck respects the harsh rules of the North and learns from them. In Chapter V, Hal, Charles and Mercedes do the opposite: London writes, "They did not know how to do anything, and as the days went by it became apparent that they could not learn." They overload the sled, mistreat the dogs and ignore John Thornton's warning about the ice, and it costs them their lives. Together, these chapters show that in a harsh environment, the willingness to learn decides who survives.
  2. If you were writing a story about your own life this year, what object could be a symbol, and what would it stand for? Explain why it fits.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: A specific object; A clear bigger idea it stands for; An explanation connecting the object to experiences
    Model answer: My library card could be a symbol of independence. This year I started going to the library by myself and choosing my own books for my homeschool reading. Every time I use the card, it reminds me that I'm becoming someone who can plan my own learning, so it would stand for growing up and freedom.

Exit Check

  1. Turn the topic "loyalty" into a theme statement the novel could support, and name one moment that supports it.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: A complete theme sentence that names no characters; One supporting moment from the novel
    Model answer: Loyalty is earned through kindness, not force. Buck obeys the man with the club but loves Thornton, who saved and cared for him.
  2. Use the root anim (life, spirit) to complete the word: something alive and moving is ___ (anim + ate).
    Answer: animate
    Anim means life; animate means alive.
  3. In Chapter IV, what bigger idea does the fire in Buck's visions stand for?
    Answer: ancestry and the ancient past
    "the memories of his ancestors become habits."
  4. A character whose differences highlight another character's qualities is a ___.
    Answer: foil
    Hal and Charles are foils to François and Perrault.
  5. Choose one symbol from the novel and explain what it stands for.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: A symbol from the novel; An accurate bigger meaning
    Model answer: The club stands for force and power, the law that rules the North.

Answer key: printable page

  1. Explain what the club, the fire and the trail symbolize.
    Answer: Club: force and power; fire: ancestry and the ancient past; trail: work and purpose.
  2. Choose one theme of the novel and show how it develops from an early chapter to a late chapter.
    Answer: Model: To survive, you must adapt even if you leave parts of yourself behind. Chapter I: Buck learns to obey the club; Chapter V: he refuses to pull onto rotten ice, while those who never adapted die.
  3. How are Hal, Charles and Mercedes foils to François and Perrault?
    Answer: The newcomers are ignorant and careless; the experienced drivers knew the trail and cared for the dogs.
  4. Write a theme statement about knowledge or ignorance, supported by one quotation.
    Answer: Model: Ignorance can be more dangerous than the wilderness; "They did not know how to do anything ... they could not learn."
  5. What does London mean when he says Buck's instincts are "the memories of his ancestors become habits"?
    Answer: Buck's wild behaviors come from his ancestors and are waking up again in him.
  6. Add -ity to adaptable, cruel and able. What does -ity mean?
    Answer: Adaptability, cruelty, ability; -ity means the state or quality of.
