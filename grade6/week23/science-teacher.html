Unit 5: Ecosystems · Week 23 · Life Science · Teacher Plan

Cycles of Matter: Water and Carbon

Lesson length: 60 minutes live, plus the after-class interactive lesson

Objectives

  • Explain the difference between energy flow and matter cycling in ecosystems.
  • Describe the water cycle, including evaporation, transpiration, condensation, precipitation, runoff and groundwater.
  • Explain how Florida's springs and the Floridan aquifer fit into the water cycle.
  • Describe the carbon cycle, including photosynthesis, respiration, decomposition and burning fossil fuels.
  • Model a cycle and explain how human activity can change it.

Standards

  • SC.8.L.18.4: Cite evidence that living systems follow the Laws of Conservation of Mass and Energy.
  • SC.8.L.18.3: Construct a scientific model of the carbon cycle to show how matter and energy are continuously transferred within and between organisms and their physical environment.
  • SC.6.E.7.2: Investigate and apply how the cycling of water between the atmosphere and hydrosphere has an effect on weather patterns and climate.
  • SC.6.N.3.4: Identify the role of models in the context of the sixth grade science benchmarks.
  • NGSS MS-LS2-3: Develop a model to describe the cycling of matter and flow of energy among living and nonliving parts of an ecosystem.

Materials

  • The interactive lesson
  • A zipper bag, water, food coloring, marker, tape
  • Red cabbage juice indicator (adult-made) or bromothymol blue, 2 clear cups, a straw
  • Optional: an elodea sprig
  • Printable practice page

Vocabulary

  • evaporation: The change of liquid water into water vapor, powered by the sun's energy.
  • transpiration: The release of water vapor from plant leaves.
  • condensation: The change of water vapor into liquid droplets, forming clouds and dew.
  • precipitation: Water falling from clouds as rain, snow, sleet or hail.
  • aquifer: An underground layer of rock or sand that holds water.
  • cellular respiration: The process in which living things use oxygen to break down sugar for energy, releasing carbon dioxide and water.
  • fossil fuel: Coal, oil or natural gas, formed over millions of years from the remains of ancient living things.

Key questions to ask

  • Is this energy or matter, and is it flowing or cycling?
  • Where does this water or carbon go next?
  • What powers this cycle?
  • How do people change this cycle?

Common mistakes to watch for

  • Plants don't do respiration.
  • Clouds are water vapor (they are liquid droplets or ice).
  • Matter is used up or destroyed in ecosystems.
  • Groundwater is underground lakes or rivers everywhere (mostly it fills spaces in rock).

Support (if the student is struggling)

  • Act out the water cycle as a single water drop.
  • Color-code the carbon cycle arrows (in versus out of the air).
  • Provide a cycle vocabulary card.

Challenge (if the student is ready for more)

  • Research how much water your county pumps from the aquifer and where it goes.
  • Find the latest Mauna Loa carbon dioxide reading from NOAA and graph the trend for five years.

Background for the teacher

The Floridan aquifer system underlies all of Florida and parts of Georgia, Alabama and South Carolina and supplies drinking water to roughly 10 million people (USGS). Florida has more than 1,000 documented springs. The Mauna Loa record (Keeling Curve) began in 1958 at about 315 ppm; annual averages passed 420 ppm in 2023 and rose to about 424 to 427 ppm in 2024 (NOAA). Matter cycling standards appear in Florida's Grade 8 (SC.8.L.18.x); KCE teaches them here per the Grade 6 scope. Red cabbage indicator turns pinker in acid; carbonated water from breath gives a mild change that is best seen against an unbreathed control cup.

Answer key: interactive lesson

Reconnect

  1. About what percent of energy passes from one level of a food chain to the next?
    Answer: about 10 percent
    The rest is used or lost as heat.
  2. Which organisms break down dead matter and return nutrients to the soil?
    Answer: decomposers
    They are key to the carbon cycle today.
  3. Mercury builds up in panthers because it is stored in the body, not used up. Is mercury energy or matter?
    Answer: matter
    Matter is never used up; it moves and accumulates.
  4. Last week you learned energy is lost as heat at each level. Where does that heat go, and can the ecosystem use it again?
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Says where the heat goes; Says whether the food chain can reuse it and what that means for ecosystems
    Model answer: The heat spreads into the air and eventually out into space. The food chain can't reuse it, so ecosystems need new energy from the sun all the time.

Learn 1: Energy Flows, Matter Cycles

  1. Energy or matter?
    Answer: energy; matter
    Energy flows; matter cycles.
  2. A carbon atom in a sawgrass leaf could later be part of a marsh rabbit, a panther and the air. Describe one possible journey for that atom, using at least three living or nonliving stops.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: At least three stops in a logical order; Correct processes linking them
    Model answer: The carbon atom in a sawgrass leaf is eaten by a marsh rabbit and becomes part of its muscle. A panther eats the rabbit. When the panther breathes out, the atom leaves as carbon dioxide in the air, where another sawgrass plant takes it in during photosynthesis.

Learn 2: The Water Cycle

  1. Put these steps in a logical order, starting with water in the Gulf.
    Answer: evaporation → condensation → precipitation → runoff into a river
    Then the river carries water back to the sea.
  2. Water vapor released from plant leaves is called ___.
    Answer: transpiration
    Plants are part of the water cycle.
  3. Explain why Florida often has thunderstorms on summer afternoons rather than mornings, using water-cycle words.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Explains what the morning sun does, using a water-cycle word; Explains what happens to the rising air by afternoon, using at least two more water-cycle words
    Model answer: In the morning, the sun heats the land and the water around Florida, so lots of water evaporates. The warm, moist air rises, cools and condenses into big storm clouds by afternoon, and then the water falls as rain.

Learn 3: Springs and the Floridan Aquifer

  1. What is the name of the huge underground layer of water-filled limestone beneath much of Florida?
    Answer: the Floridan aquifer
    It feeds Florida's springs.
  2. Explain how fertilizer used on lawns and farms miles from a spring can affect the spring's ecosystem. Use the water cycle in your answer.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Traces the fertilizer's path to the spring using water-cycle steps; Explains at least one effect on living things in the spring
    Model answer: When it rains, water soaks into the ground and carries fertilizer with it into the aquifer. The groundwater flows underground to the spring, bringing extra nutrients that feed algae. The algae cloud the water, block sunlight from eelgrass and harm the animals that depend on the spring.

Learn 4: The Carbon Cycle

  1. Which carbon-cycle process removes carbon dioxide from the air?
    Answer: photosynthesis
    Respiration releases carbon dioxide.
  2. Carbon dioxide in the air was about 315 parts per million in 1958 and about 425 in 2024. By how many parts per million did it rise?
    Answer: 110 ppm
    425 − 315 = 110.
  3. Fossil fuels formed from living things millions of years ago. Explain why burning them adds carbon to today's air even though that carbon was once part of the cycle.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Explains where the carbon was for millions of years; Explains why releasing it now raises carbon dioxide in the air
    Model answer: The carbon in fossil fuels was locked underground for millions of years, out of the active cycle. Burning coal, oil and gas releases that stored carbon as carbon dioxide all at once, much faster than plants and oceans can take it back in, so the amount in the air goes up.

Hands-On

  1. Describe what you saw in the water cycle bag over 2 days. Label which parts of the water cycle happened, and name one way the bag is not like the real water cycle.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Observations over time; Evaporation, condensation and precipitation identified; A limit
    Model answer: After a few hours, fog appeared on the inside of the bag; by evening there were droplets at the top that slid down. The sun's heat caused evaporation, the cooler top caused condensation, and the dripping was like precipitation. Unlike the real cycle, the bag is sealed, with no plants, rivers, oceans or groundwater.
  2. What color change did you see after blowing bubbles? What does it show about the carbon cycle? If you tried the plant, what happened?
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Describes the color change compared with the control cup; Explains what in your breath caused it and which process produced it; Reports and explains the plant result, or notes honestly that you did not try it
    Model answer: The purple cabbage juice turned pinkish after I blew bubbles, because my breath contains carbon dioxide from cellular respiration, which made the water slightly acidic. When I put the elodea in sunlight for an afternoon, the color slowly shifted back toward purple, because the plant used the carbon dioxide for photosynthesis.

Think Deeper

  1. A new shopping center covers a Florida field with pavement and buildings. Predict how this changes the water cycle in that area, including what happens to infiltration, runoff and the aquifer.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Predicts what happens to infiltration and runoff; Predicts the effect on the aquifer and springs; Considers what removing the plants changes
    Model answer: Pavement stops rain from soaking into the ground, so infiltration drops. More water runs off the parking lot into storm drains, carrying oil and litter into rivers and lakes and causing flooding. With less water soaking in, less recharges the Floridan aquifer, which over time can lower spring flow. Removing the field's plants also means less transpiration.
  2. Some people propose planting more trees to reduce carbon dioxide in the air. Use the carbon cycle to explain why that could help, and give one limit of this solution.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Explains how trees remove and store carbon; Gives one limit, explained
    Model answer: Trees take in carbon dioxide during photosynthesis and store the carbon in their wood for decades or even centuries, so more trees can pull carbon out of the air. But it takes many years for trees to grow, and when trees die and decompose or burn, they release that carbon again. Trees alone can't absorb carbon as fast as burning fossil fuels releases it.

Exit Check

  1. Water vapor cooling into droplets to form clouds is called ___.
    Answer: condensation
    Then precipitation falls.
  2. Which process returns carbon dioxide to the air every time you breathe out?
    Answer: cellular respiration
    All living things do it.
  3. Where does the water in Florida's springs come from?
    Answer: the Floridan aquifer (groundwater from rain)
    Rain soaks in and flows out at springs.
  4. How is the way matter moves through an ecosystem different from the way energy moves?
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Describes how matter moves; Contrasts it with how energy moves
    Model answer: Matter is recycled over and over, but energy flows in one direction and is lost as heat, so the sun must keep supplying it.

Answer key: printable page

  1. Explain the difference between how energy and matter move through an ecosystem.
    Answer: Energy flows one way and is lost as heat; matter is recycled again and again.
  2. Describe the water cycle using evaporation, transpiration, condensation, precipitation and runoff.
    Answer: Sun evaporates water and plants transpire; vapor condenses into clouds; precipitation falls; water runs off or soaks in.
  3. How do Florida's springs connect to the water cycle?
    Answer: Rain infiltrates into the Floridan aquifer, flows through limestone and comes back up at springs.
  4. Name four processes in the carbon cycle and whether each adds CO2 to the air or removes it.
    Answer: Photosynthesis removes; respiration, decomposition and burning fossil fuels add.
  5. Why has the amount of carbon dioxide in the air risen since 1958?
    Answer: Mainly because people burn fossil fuels, releasing carbon stored underground for millions of years.
  6. Rain can take years to travel through the aquifer. Explain why a drought two years ago could still lower a spring's flow today.
    Answer: Spring water fell as rain years earlier; less rain then means less water reaching the spring now.
