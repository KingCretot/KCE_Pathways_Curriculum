Grade 6 · Week 23 · Parent Guide

Week 23: One-Step Equations, Symbols and Theme, Cycles of Matter and Songhai

Math solves one-step equations by keeping them balanced and uses them to find missing prices on receipts. ELA reads Chapters IV and V of The Call of the Wild and learns how symbols and contrasting characters build a theme. Science follows water through Florida's springs and aquifer and carbon through air, living things and fossil fuels. Social Studies tells the story of the Songhai Empire and Timbuktu, a city where books sold for more than any other goods.

How scoring works: each lesson scores itself. Your child gets two tries per question: full points on the first try, half points on the second, then the answer and explanation appear. A score of 85 or higher means the lesson is mastered. Written answers go to Jonathan, who scores them with a rubric.

Time this week: About 60 minutes of live lessons per subject, plus a hands-on activity, Think Deeper questions and an Exit Check for each. ELA also needs time to read Chapters IV and V (about an hour). The science water cycle bag needs 2 days at a sunny window, so start it early in the week.

Gather this week: A clothes hanger with notches, 2 identical paper or plastic cups and string (or 2 bags), about 30 identical small objects such as pennies, paper clips or dried beans, an envelope or small opaque bag, a family member, paper and pencil, and optionally a few real store receipts (Math); household objects or craft materials to stand for symbols (a stick, a red cloth, a printed or drawn picture of a fire, a string or rope), a large sheet of paper or poster board, markers or colored pencils, sticky notes or paper leaves, The Call of the Wild (Chapters IV and V), and paper and pencil (ELA); a clear zipper sandwich bag, water and optional blue food coloring, a permanent marker, tape, a sunny window, 2 clear cups, red cabbage juice indicator (an adult chops red cabbage, covers it with hot water, waits 10 minutes and strains it) or bromothymol blue from a pet or pool store, a drinking straw, optionally a sprig of an aquarium plant such as elodea from a pet store, and paper and pencil (Science); 2 sheets of plain paper, a brown paper grocery bag, optionally a cup of cold brewed tea and a cotton ball or paper towel, a pencil and a pen or fine marker, a ruler, scissors, yarn or string and a large blunt needle (or a stapler), 20 index cards, a shoebox or small box, and a timer (Social Studies).

Heads up: Novel content: Chapter V is the hardest chapter in the book. Hal, Charles and Mercedes overload and starve the dogs, Hal beats Buck, several dogs die, and the chapter ends with the three travelers and the team falling through the river ice to their deaths, while John Thornton saves Buck. Please preview it and decide how to read it with your child (reading aloud and summarizing the hardest parts works well). Science safety: an adult makes the cabbage juice with hot water; your child blows out through the straw only, never sucks in; no one drinks any of the liquids; wash hands afterward. Use a picture of a fire for ELA, never a real candle or flame. Keep pennies, beans, scissors and needles away from young children and pets.

ELA: Symbols and Theme

Your child explains how the club, the fire and the trail work as symbols, tells a topic from a theme and writes a theme as a full sentence, traces how a theme grows across chapters with evidence, and compares the skilled drivers François and Perrault with the unprepared Hal, Charles and Mercedes.

Ask at dinner: "What do you think London is saying about survival, and which moment shows it?"

Try it together: Ask your child to explain the symbol poster to you object by object.

Signs it's clicking: They write a theme as a sentence, such as "Knowledge of a place matters more than wealth when surviving it," instead of one word like "survival." Signs they need help: They give a topic ("nature") instead of a theme. Ask, "What does the story say about nature?"

Math: Solving One-Step Equations

Your child tells expressions from equations, tests whether a value is a solution, solves one-step equations with whole numbers, decimals and fractions using inverse operations, checks every answer by substitution, and writes equations to find a missing price, original price, quantity or unit price.

Ask at dinner: "What is being done to the variable, and what undoes it?"

Try it together: Hide a price on a real grocery receipt and let your child write and solve an equation to find it.

Signs it's clicking: They check an answer by putting it back into the original equation without being asked. Signs they need help: They do the same operation instead of the inverse (adding 12 to both sides of x + 12 = 30). Have them balance it on the hanger scale.

Life Science: Cycles of Matter: Water and Carbon

Your child explains that energy flows one way but matter cycles, describes the water cycle, explains how rain feeds the Floridan aquifer and Florida's springs and how fertilizer can reach them, and traces carbon through photosynthesis, respiration, decomposition and burning fossil fuels, including the rise in carbon dioxide measured at Mauna Loa since 1958.

Ask at dinner: "Where might the water in your glass have been before it reached our tap?"

Try it together: Look at the water cycle bag together each morning and evening and name what you see.

Signs it's clicking: They explain why Florida storms often come in the afternoon. Signs they need help: They say clouds are water vapor. Remind them vapor is invisible; clouds are tiny liquid droplets.

World and American History: Songhai, Timbuktu and Centers of Learning

Your child learns how Sunni Ali and Askia Muhammad built and organized the Songhai Empire, reads a 1600 English translation of Leo Africanus describing Timbuktu's scholars and book trade, explores what students studied there, explains how Morocco's guns defeated Songhai in 1591, and learns about the scholar Ahmad Baba and the librarians who saved Timbuktu's manuscripts in 2012 and 2013.

Ask at dinner: "If our house could save only eight books, which would you choose and why?"

Try it together: Do the 5-minute rescue challenge together and compare your choices.

Signs it's clicking: They use evidence to prove that West Africa had a written history. Signs they need help: They picture Timbuktu as a single university building. Explain that learning happened in many mosques and scholars' homes.
