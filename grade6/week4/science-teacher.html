Unit 1: Thinking Like a Scientist · Week 4 · Life Science · Teacher Plan

Graphs, Models and Scientific Tools

Lesson length: 60 minutes live, plus the after-class interactive lesson

Objectives

  • Choose the right graph (line, bar, circle or scatter) for a question and a set of data.
  • Build and read a line graph with a title, labeled axes, units and an even scale.
  • Spot a misleading graph and explain how it tricks the reader.
  • Explain what scientific models are, why scientists use them, and their limits.
  • Read a hurricane forecast cone correctly as a model of uncertainty.

Standards

  • SC.6.N.1.1: Define a problem from the sixth grade curriculum, use appropriate reference materials to support scientific understanding, plan and carry out scientific investigation of various types, such as systematic observations or experiments, identify variables, collect and organize data, interpret data in charts, tables, and graphics, analyze information, make predictions, and defend conclusions.
  • SC.6.N.3.4: Identify the role of models in the context of the sixth grade science benchmarks.
  • SC.6.N.1.5: Recognize that science involves creativity, not just in designing experiments, but also in creating explanations that fit evidence.
  • MA.6.DP.1 connection: displaying and interpreting numerical data.
  • NGSS Science and Engineering Practices 2 and 4: developing and using models; analyzing and interpreting data.

Materials

  • The interactive lesson
  • A thermometer
  • Graph paper and two colored pencils
  • A globe or map to discuss models
  • A current or past NHC forecast graphic on screen (optional)
  • Printable practice page

Vocabulary

  • graph: A picture of data that shows patterns, comparisons or relationships.
  • axis (plural axes): The reference lines of a graph. The x-axis runs across; the y-axis runs up and down.
  • scale: The evenly spaced numbers along an axis.
  • trend: The general direction data moves, such as increasing, decreasing or staying the same.
  • model: A representation of an object, process or system that helps scientists study or explain it. Models can be physical, drawings, math or computer programs.
  • limitation: A way a model is different from the real thing, so it cannot show or predict everything.
  • uncertainty: How much a measurement or prediction might be off. Honest science reports it.
  • misleading: Giving the wrong idea, even when the numbers themselves are true.

Key questions to ask

  • What question is this graph trying to answer?
  • Where does the y-axis start? Is the scale even?
  • What does this model show well, and what does it leave out?
  • What would a person misunderstand if they misread this model?

Common mistakes to watch for

  • Putting the dependent variable on the x-axis.
  • Uneven intervals on an axis.
  • Using a line graph for separate categories.
  • Thinking the forecast cone shows the size of the storm, or that areas outside the cone are safe.
  • Believing a model is a perfect copy of reality.

Support (if the student is struggling)

  • Provide a pre-drawn graph frame with axes labeled.
  • Use the TAILS checklist as a card.
  • Limit the investigation to four readings.

Challenge (if the student is ready for more)

  • Calculate the average difference between sun and shade across all five Okafor readings (5 °C).
  • Research how the size of the NHC cone has shrunk over the years as forecasts improved, and explain why.

Background for the teacher

The NHC cone represents the probable track of the center of a tropical cyclone. Its size is set from historical official forecast errors over the previous five years, so the entire track of the center remains within the cone about 60 to 70 percent of the time. The cone does not depict storm size, and hazards frequently occur outside it. The rainfall numbers in the misleading-graph example are illustrative, not official climate data. Dr. J. Marshall Shepherd directs the Atmospheric Sciences Program at the University of Georgia and was the 2013 president of the American Meteorological Society.

Answer key: interactive lesson

Reconnect

  1. Convert 2.5 kilometers to meters.
    Answer: 2500 m
    Down three steps: 2.5 × 1,000 = 2,500 m.
  2. Convert 480 milliliters to liters.
    Answer: 0.48 L
    Up three steps: 480 ÷ 1,000 = 0.48 L.
  3. When you read a graduated cylinder, which part of the curved surface do you read?
    Answer: the bottom of the meniscus
    Read at eye level at the bottom of the meniscus.
  4. Three measurements of 51.2 g, 51.3 g and 51.2 g are close together but the true mass is 60 g. Are they accurate, precise, both or neither?
    Answer: precise but not accurate
    Close together (precise) but far from the true value (not accurate).
  5. Trials of 14 cm, 17 cm and 14 cm were recorded. What is the average?
    Answer: 15 cm
    (14 + 17 + 14) ÷ 3 = 45 ÷ 3 = 15 cm.
  6. What belongs in the heading of every column in a data table, besides the name of the variable? Why does it matter?
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Names units; Explains that numbers without units can be misread (Mars Climate Orbiter)
    Model answer: The units, like cm or °C, because a number without a unit can be misread. That is how the Mars Climate Orbiter was lost.

Learn 1: Reading a Line Graph

  1. At 2 PM, how many degrees warmer is the sun than the shade?
    Answer: 8 °C
    37 − 29 = 8 °C.
  2. At what time is the difference between sun and shade the smallest?
    Answer: 8 AM
    At 8 AM the difference is only 1 °C, because the sun has not had time to heat the open ground.
  3. Describe the trend in the sun temperature from 2 PM to 4 PM.
    Answer: It decreases (drops from 37 °C to 33 °C).
    The line slopes down: the temperature is decreasing as the sun gets lower.
  4. Which variable is on the x-axis, and is it the independent or dependent variable?
    Answer: time, the independent variable
    Time goes on the x-axis. The temperature depends on the time, not the other way around.
  5. Predict the sun and shade temperatures at 6 PM. Use the trend on the graph to explain your prediction.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Predicts both temperatures lower than at 4 PM; Predicts the gap narrowing; Uses the trend to justify
    Model answer: I predict about 30 °C in the sun and 27 °C in the shade. Both lines are going down after 2 PM, and the gap is getting smaller as the sun gets lower, so by 6 PM they should be closer together.

Learn 2: Choosing the Right Graph

  1. A student measures her bean plant's height every day for three weeks. Which graph fits best?
    Answer: a line graph
    Height changing over time calls for a line graph.
  2. A survey asks 40 families which pet they own, and you want to show what fraction of families chose each pet. Which graph fits best?
    Answer: a circle graph
    Parts of one whole group fit a circle graph.
  3. You test how far four different balls bounce. Which graph fits best?
    Answer: a bar graph
    Comparing separate categories (types of balls) fits a bar graph.
  4. A coach records each runner's hours of sleep and their race time. What graph should she use, and what would she look for in it?
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Scatter plot; Looks for a relationship or pattern between the two variables; Notes that a pattern does not prove one causes the other
    Model answer: A scatter plot, with hours of sleep on one axis and race time on the other. She would look for a pattern, such as runners who sleep more having faster times. Even if there is a pattern, it does not prove sleep caused the faster times.

Learn 3: How Graphs Can Mislead

  1. How many more inches of rain does Cedar Key get than Palmdale?
    Answer: 4 inches
    46 − 42 = 4 inches.
  2. In Graph A, the Palmdale bar rises 2 units above the axis and the Cedar Key bar rises 6. How many times taller does the Cedar Key bar look?
    Answer: 3 times
    6 ÷ 2 = 3, even though 46 is only about 1.1 times 42.
  3. Why might someone choose to make a graph like Graph A? Give a real-world example of who might do this.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Explains it exaggerates differences; Gives a plausible real example (an ad, a campaign, a company report); Suggests how a reader should respond
    Model answer: Someone might want a small difference to look huge. A company could use it in an ad to make its battery look like it lasts much longer than a competitor's. A reader should check where the y-axis starts before believing the picture.

Learn 4: Models and Their Limits

  1. Why does the forecast cone get wider further into the future?
    Answer: Forecasts are less certain the further ahead they go.
    Small errors grow over time, so the possible path widens.
  2. Your town is 40 miles outside the cone. Can the hurricane still affect you? Type yes or no.
    Answer: yes
    Yes. The cone shows the likely path of the center only, not the storm's size. Wind, rain and storm surge often reach far outside it.
  3. A globe is a model of Earth. Describe one thing the globe shows well and one limitation it has.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Names a real strength (shape, relative positions, true sizes of continents); Names a real limitation (no true mountain heights, no weather, no detail, cannot show change)
    Model answer: A globe shows Earth's round shape and the true sizes and positions of the continents better than a flat map. One limitation is that it cannot show how tall mountains really are, and it does not show anything changing, like weather or cities.

Hands-On

  1. Type your data table with times and both temperatures, including units.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: At least four readings for each location; Times and °C units shown; Data looks reasonable
    Model answer: Time | Sun (°C) | Shade (°C) 9 AM | 27 | 25 10 AM | 30 | 26 11 AM | 33 | 27 12 PM | 35 | 28
  2. Describe your graph: When was the difference greatest? How did your results compare with the Okafor family's? What would you change if you did it again?
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Identifies the time of greatest difference; Compares with the Okafor data; Names one way to improve the investigation (more trials, same height, same type of ground)
    Model answer: The biggest difference was 7 °C at 12 PM. Like the Okafor data, the gap grew toward midday, but my readings were a little lower. Next time I would take readings until 4 PM and keep both thermometers over grass.
  3. Write two testable questions you could investigate yourself for your Week 5 project. For one of them, name the independent and dependent variables.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Two testable questions; Correct independent variable; Correct dependent variable
    Model answer: 1. Does black or white paper get hotter in the sun? 2. Does the amount of water change how fast a bean seed sprouts? For question 1, the independent variable is paper color and the dependent variable is temperature in °C.

Think Deeper

  1. A website shows a graph with a y-axis from 98 to 100 and claims a new juice "skyrockets" energy. The bar for the juice drinkers is at 99.6 and the bar for water drinkers is at 99.1. Write three questions a scientist would ask before believing the claim.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Asks about the axis or scale; Asks about the sample size or how the test was done (fair test, variables); Asks what was measured and in what units, or who paid for the study
    Model answer: 1. Why does the y-axis start at 98 instead of 0, and what is the real difference? 2. How many people were tested, and was it a fair test with only the drink changed? 3. What exactly was measured, in what units, and who paid for the study?
  2. Design a model you could build at home to show why the shady spot stays cooler. Describe the materials, what each part represents, and one limitation of your model.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Describes realistic materials; Explains what each part represents (lamp = sun, cardboard = tree); Names a genuine limitation
    Model answer: I would use a desk lamp as the sun, two cups of water as the ground, and a piece of cardboard held above one cup as the tree's shade. After 20 minutes I would measure both cups. A limitation is that a real tree also cools the air by releasing water from its leaves, which my cardboard cannot do.

Exit Check

  1. A family wants to show what fraction of its monthly budget goes to food, rent, gas and savings. Which graph fits best?
    Answer: a circle graph
    Parts of one whole (the budget) fit a circle graph.
  2. What does the T in TAILS stand for?
    Answer: title
    Every graph needs a title.
  3. Using the Okafor data, how much did the shade temperature rise from 8 AM to 2 PM?
    Answer: 6 °C
    29 − 23 = 6 °C.
  4. Using the Okafor data, how much did the sun temperature rise from 8 AM to 2 PM?
    Answer: 13 °C
    37 − 24 = 13 °C, more than twice the rise in the shade.
  5. A forecast cone does not show the size of a storm. What does it show?
    Answer: the probable path of the storm's center
    The cone shows where the center of the storm will probably travel.
  6. What is the first thing you should check on any graph to see if it is misleading, and why?
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Names the y-axis or scale; Explains that a scale not starting at zero can exaggerate differences
    Model answer: Check where the y-axis starts and whether the scale is even, because on a bar graph, starting above zero can make small differences look huge.

Answer key: printable page

  1. Name the best graph for each: (a) a puppy's mass each week for 10 weeks; (b) the favorite sport of 30 students as fractions of the class; (c) the number of birds seen at four feeders; (d) hours of practice and number of free throws made.
    Answer: (a) line; (b) circle; (c) bar; (d) scatter
  2. On graph paper, graph this data as a line graph: Time (min) 0, 5, 10, 15, 20; Water temperature (°C) 80, 66, 55, 47, 42. Then describe the trend.
    Answer: Line graph with time on x-axis, temperature on y-axis; the water cools quickly at first, then more slowly.
  3. What do the letters in TAILS stand for?
    Answer: Title, Axes labeled, Intervals even, Labels with units, Scale that fits
  4. A bar graph's y-axis starts at 90. Bar A shows 92 and Bar B shows 96. Explain why this graph could be misleading.
    Answer: Starting at 90 makes B look three times taller than A, even though the real difference is only 4 out of about 94.
  5. Explain what a hurricane forecast cone shows and what it does not show.
    Answer: It shows the probable path of the storm's center, getting wider with uncertainty. It does not show the storm's size or where wind, rain and flooding will reach.
  6. Name a model you use in everyday life, what it represents, and one limitation.
    Answer: For example: a map app represents roads and places but does not show every pothole or live traffic perfectly.
