Unit 1: Number Power · Week 4 · Math · Teacher Plan

Dividing Decimals

Lesson length: 60 minutes live on Zoom, plus 35 to 45 minutes for the after-class interactive lesson and pantry activity

Objectives

  • Divide a decimal by a whole number using long division.
  • Divide by a decimal by first making the divisor a whole number.
  • Estimate quotients to check where the decimal point belongs.
  • Calculate and compare unit prices to find the better buy.
  • Decide when buying in bulk is and is not a smart choice.

Standards

  • MA.6.NSO.2.1: Multiply and divide positive multi-digit numbers with decimals to the thousandths, including using a standard algorithm with procedural fluency.
  • MA.6.AR.3.5 (preview): Solve mathematical and real-world problems involving ratios, rates and unit rates, including comparisons.
  • MA.K12.MTR.6.1: Assess the reasonableness of solutions.
  • MA.K12.MTR.7.1: Apply mathematics to real-world contexts.
  • Financial literacy: comparison shopping, unit pricing and smart buying (Jump$tart Spending and Saving).

Materials

  • The interactive lesson
  • Four grocery packages or flyer pages with prices and package sizes
  • Printable practice page

Vocabulary

  • dividend: The number being divided.
  • divisor: The number you divide by.
  • quotient: The answer to a division problem.
  • unit price: The cost of one unit, such as one pound, one ounce or one item. Unit price = total price ÷ number of units.
  • bulk: A large quantity bought at once, often for a lower unit price.
  • better buy: The option with the lower unit price, if both items meet your needs.
  • repeating decimal: A decimal in which a digit or group of digits repeats forever, such as 0.333...

Lesson flow

6 minReconnect
Run the Week 3 Reconnect questions.
5 minHook
Show the two rice bags. Ask the student to guess the better buy before calculating.
10 minDecimal ÷ whole number
Model 7.65 ÷ 5 with long division. Stress placing the decimal point straight up. Work 6.48 ÷ 24 to show the leading zero.
14 minDividing by a decimal
Use the cookies-and-friends analogy for why multiplying both numbers by 10 keeps the quotient the same. Work 1.5 ÷ 0.03 with the money estimate.
12 minUnit prices
Calculate all three comparisons. Have the student explain each better buy in a sentence.
8 minWhen bulk is not a bargain
Discuss the bananas problem. Push the student to weigh spoilage, storage and budget.
5 minAssign
Assign the Pantry Unit Price Detective, Think Deeper and the Exit Check.

Key questions to ask

  • Where does the decimal point go in the quotient, and why?
  • Why can we multiply both numbers by 10 without changing the answer?
  • Will the quotient be bigger or smaller than the dividend? How do you know?
  • Is the lower unit price always the better choice for your family?

Common mistakes to watch for

  • Moving the decimal point in the divisor but not in the dividend.
  • Forgetting the leading zero (writing 6.48 ÷ 24 as 2.7).
  • Believing division always makes numbers smaller.
  • Assuming the bigger package is always cheaper per unit.

Support (if the student is struggling)

  • Use money language: how many 3-cent pieces in $1.50?
  • Provide a place-value chart to show both decimal points moving.
  • Check every quotient by multiplying.

Challenge (if the student is ready for more)

  • Compare unit prices across three sizes where the middle size is the best deal.
  • Find a real shelf tag that uses a different unit than its neighbor and convert to compare.

Background for the teacher

Many states require unit pricing on shelf tags, but rules on units vary, so comparing requires care. Unit price, savings and budget constraints prepare students for ratios and rates in Unit 3.

How the lesson runs

During the live lesson, the student shares their screen and answers the Reconnect questions and the Learn checks live while you teach. The Hands-On activity, Think Deeper and Exit Check are completed after class.

Scoring

The interactive lesson scores itself: full points on the first try, half points on the second try, then the answer and explanation appear. Mastery is 85. Written responses arrive in your Formspree email with the score; score them with the rubric below before the student moves on.

Answer key: interactive lesson

Reconnect

  1. In 2.4 × 1.35 = 3.24, what is 3.24 called, and how many decimal places does the product have before you simplify it?
    Answer: the product; 3 decimal places (3.240)
    3.24 is the product. 2.4 has one decimal place and 1.35 has two, so the product starts with three: 3.240.
  2. 2.6 × 1.5 = ?
    Answer: 3.9
    26 × 15 = 390, with 2 decimal places: 3.90 = 3.9.
  3. 0.4 × 0.07 = ?
    Answer: 0.028
    4 × 7 = 28, with 3 decimal places: 0.028.
  4. 3.25 × 100 = ?
    Answer: 325
    Each digit moves two places left.
  5. Kwame earns $15.75 an hour and works 5.5 hours on Saturday. What is his pay, rounded to the nearest cent?
    Answer: $86.63
    15.75 × 5.5 = 86.625, which rounds to $86.63.
  6. Apples are $2.49 per pound. What do 3.2 pounds cost, rounded to the nearest cent?
    Answer: $7.97
    2.49 × 3.2 = 7.968, which rounds to $7.97.
  7. Without calculating, will 40 × 0.85 be greater or less than 40? Explain your reasoning in one sentence.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Says less; Explains that 0.85 is less than 1, so the product is only part of 40
    Model answer: Less, because 0.85 is less than 1, so I am taking only part of 40 (it is 34).

Learn 1: Dividing a Decimal by a Whole Number

  1. 8.4 ÷ 4 = ?
    Answer: 2.1
    8 ÷ 4 = 2, then 4 ÷ 4 = 1: 2.1. Check: 4 × 2.1 = 8.4.
  2. 38.40 ÷ 12 = ?
    Answer: 3.2
    38 ÷ 12 = 3 R2, bring down 4: 24 ÷ 12 = 2, then 0: 3.20 = 3.2.
  3. 6.48 ÷ 24 = ?
    Answer: 0.27
    64 ÷ 24 = 2 R16, 168 ÷ 24 = 7: 0.27. Check: 24 × 0.27 = 6.48.
  4. 6.3 ÷ 4 = ?
    Answer: 1.575
    6.300 ÷ 4 = 1.575. Check: 4 × 1.575 = 6.3.
  5. Three friends split a $10.00 lunch bill equally. What does each person owe, to the nearest cent?
    Answer: $3.33
    10 ÷ 3 = 3.333..., a repeating decimal. Each owes about $3.33, so one person pays $3.34 to make exactly $10.00.

Learn 2: Dividing by a Decimal

  1. 24.8 ÷ 0.8 = ?
    Answer: 31
    Multiply both by 10: 248 ÷ 8 = 31.
  2. 45.6 ÷ 0.12 = ?
    Answer: 380
    4,560 ÷ 12 = 380. Check: 0.12 × 380 = 45.6.
  3. 0.96 ÷ 1.2 = ?
    Answer: 0.8
    Multiply both by 10: 9.6 ÷ 12 = 0.8.
  4. Without calculating, will 18 ÷ 0.45 be greater than or less than 18? Type greater or less.
    Answer: greater
    Dividing by a number less than 1 asks how many small pieces fit, so the quotient (40) is greater than 18.
  5. A 9.75-meter rope is cut into pieces 2.5 meters long. How many full pieces can be cut?
    Answer: 3 pieces
    9.75 ÷ 2.5 = 3.9. Only 3 full pieces can be cut; 0.9 of a piece (2.25 m) is left over.

Learn 3: Unit Prices and the Better Buy

  1. What is the unit price of the 5-pound bag of rice, per pound, to the nearest cent?
    Answer: $0.86 per pound
    4.29 ÷ 5 = 0.858, which rounds to $0.86 per pound.
  2. What is the unit price of the 20-pound bag, per pound?
    Answer: $0.74 per pound
    14.80 ÷ 20 = $0.74 per pound. The big bag is the better buy.
  3. The 12 cans of juice hold 144 fluid ounces in total. What is the price per fluid ounce?
    Answer: $0.05 per fl oz
    7.20 ÷ 144 = $0.05. The bottle is 3.84 ÷ 64 = $0.06, so the cans are cheaper per ounce.
  4. What is the price of one egg in the 18-pack?
    Answer: $0.29
    5.22 ÷ 18 = $0.29, compared with 3.60 ÷ 12 = $0.30 in the dozen.
  5. If Amara's family uses 20 pounds of rice, how much money does buying the big bag save compared with buying four 5-pound bags?
    Answer: $2.36
    Four small bags cost 4 × 4.29 = $17.16. 17.16 − 14.80 = $2.36 saved.

Learn 4: When Bulk Is Not a Bargain

  1. A 3-pound bag of bananas costs $1.47 ($0.49 per pound). Loose bananas cost $0.59 per pound. Amara's family eats about 1 pound of bananas before they turn brown. Which should they buy, and why? Use unit price and real life in your answer.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Compares unit prices correctly ($0.49 vs $0.59); Recognizes most of the bag would spoil; Calculates or explains the true cost of what they actually eat; Makes a clear recommendation
    Model answer: The bag has the lower unit price, $0.49 versus $0.59 per pound, but they would eat only 1 of the 3 pounds before it turns brown. Buying 1 pound loose costs $0.59, while the bag costs $1.47 for the same amount eaten, so loose bananas are the better choice for them.
  2. A family has $12.00 left in its grocery budget. Can they buy the 20-pound rice bag for $14.80? How much more would they need?
    Answer: $2.80
    14.80 − 12.00 = $2.80 short, so they would need to buy a smaller bag this week.

Hands-On

  1. Type a table with each item, its price, its amount and its unit price. Show the division for one of them.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Four items with price, amount and unit price; Division shown for at least one; Unit prices rounded to the cent and labeled with units
    Model answer: Oats: $4.79 for 42 oz, 4.79 ÷ 42 ≈ 0.114, so $0.11 per oz. Rice: $3.49 for 32 oz, $0.11 per oz. Milk: $3.89 for 128 fl oz, $0.03 per fl oz. Eggs: $3.60 for 12, $0.30 each.
  2. Which comparison did you make, and which is the better buy for your family? Explain using the unit price and at least one real-life factor.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Names the comparison; Gives both unit prices; Uses one real-life factor; Makes a clear decision
    Model answer: I compared 18 oz of oats for $3.29 ($0.18 per oz) with 42 oz for $4.79 ($0.11 per oz). The big canister is the better buy for us because we eat oatmeal every day and it keeps for months in the pantry.

Think Deeper

  1. Andre says 2.1 ÷ 0.07 = 3. What is the correct quotient?
    Answer: 30
    Multiply both by 100: 210 ÷ 7 = 30.
  2. Explain Andre's mistake and show two ways to check the correct answer.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Identifies that he did not move both decimal points the same number of places; Shows the correct shift (210 ÷ 7); Checks by multiplying (0.07 × 30 = 2.1); Checks with an estimate or reasoning (how many 7-cent pieces in $2.10)
    Model answer: Andre probably moved the decimal in 0.07 two places but in 2.1 only one place, so he divided 21 by 7. Both must move two places: 210 ÷ 7 = 30. Check by multiplying: 0.07 × 30 = 2.10. Or think of money: there are 30 seven-cent pieces in $2.10.
  3. A store labels one cereal box "$0.27 per ounce" and the other "$4.32 per pound." Which is the better buy? Explain how you compared them. (There are 16 ounces in a pound.)
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Converts to the same unit; Correct comparison: $4.32 per pound is $0.27 per ounce, so they are the same price; Explains why stores might use different units
    Model answer: I converted $4.32 per pound to ounces: 4.32 ÷ 16 = $0.27 per ounce. They cost exactly the same per ounce, so neither is a better buy on price. Stores sometimes use different units on tags, which makes comparing harder, so it is smart to convert.

Exit Check

  1. A 7.92-meter board is cut into shelves 3.3 meters long. How many meters of board are left after cutting as many full shelves as possible?
    Answer: 1.32 m
    7.92 ÷ 3.3 = 2.4, so only 2 full shelves (6.6 m) can be cut. 7.92 − 6.6 = 1.32 m is left over.
  2. A gardener splits 12.6 pounds of potting soil into pots that each hold 0.35 pound. How many pots can she fill?
    Answer: 36 pots
    12.6 ÷ 0.35 = 1,260 ÷ 35 = 36 pots.
  3. 5.04 ÷ 0.9 = ?
    Answer: 5.6
    Multiply both by 10: 50.4 ÷ 9 = 5.6.
  4. A 32-ounce bottle of olive oil costs $15.99. What is the price per ounce, to the nearest cent?
    Answer: $0.50 per ounce
    15.99 ÷ 32 = 0.4996..., which rounds to $0.50 per ounce.
  5. A 16-ounce jar of peanut butter costs $3.99 and a 40-ounce jar costs $8.80. What is the unit price of the 40-ounce jar, per ounce?
    Answer: $0.22 per ounce
    8.80 ÷ 40 = $0.22 per ounce, compared with 3.99 ÷ 16 ≈ $0.25 for the small jar.
  6. A runner finished 94.5 kilometers of training in 3.5 weeks. What was her average distance per week?
    Answer: 27 km
    94.5 ÷ 3.5 = 945 ÷ 35 = 27 km per week.

  7. Explain why 8 ÷ 0.5 is greater than 8.
    Answer: Dividing by 0.5 asks how many halves are in 8; there are 16 halves, so the quotient is greater.
  8. Give one reason a family might NOT choose the item with the lowest unit price.
    Answer: It might spoil before they use it, they may not have storage space, or they may not be able to afford the larger total right now.
