Unit 1: Stories That Shape Us (Narrative) · Week 4 · ELA · Teacher Plan

Crafting Narrative: Dialogue, Pacing and Description

Lesson length: 60 minutes live, plus the after-class interactive lesson

Objectives

  • Punctuate dialogue correctly and use it to reveal character and move the plot forward.
  • Control pacing by slowing down the climax and summarizing less important moments.
  • Write vivid description with sensory details, precise nouns and strong verbs.
  • Use transitional words and phrases to show the order of events.
  • Use the roots chron, tele, photo and meter/metr, and draft a complete personal narrative.

Standards

  • ELA.6.C.1.2: Write personal or fictional narratives using narrative techniques, precise words and phrases, and figurative language.
  • ELA.6.C.3.1: Follow the rules of standard English grammar, punctuation, capitalization, and spelling appropriate to grade level (dialogue punctuation).
  • ELA.6.C.1.5 (preview): Improve writing by planning, revising, and editing, considering feedback from adults and peers.
  • ELA.6.V.1.2: Apply knowledge of Greek and Latin roots and affixes to determine meanings of words and phrases in grade-level content.

Materials

  • The interactive lesson
  • The student's story mountain cards and opening paragraph from Week 1
  • Space to act out the climax
  • Printable practice page

Vocabulary

  • dialogue: The words characters speak to each other in a story, shown in quotation marks.
  • dialogue tag: The words that tell who is speaking, such as Abuelo said.
  • pacing: How fast or slow a story moves. Writers slow down important moments and speed through less important ones.
  • strong verb: A precise action word that creates a picture, such as smacked instead of hit.
  • precise noun: A specific naming word, such as pinfish instead of fish.
  • transition: A word or phrase that connects ideas and shows order, such as at first, suddenly, later that night or finally.
  • chronological order: Events told in the order they happened in time.
  • chron / tele: Greek roots meaning time (chronological, chronic) and far (telephone, telescope).
  • photo / meter, metr: Greek roots meaning light (photograph) and measure (thermometer, metric).

Lesson flow

6 minReconnect
Run the Reconnect questions.
4 minHook
Discuss slow motion in movies. Ask the student to name a movie moment that was slowed down and why.
12 minDialogue
Study the five rules using The Cast Net. The student punctuates two sentences live, then writes two lines of dialogue from their own story.
12 minPacing
Read Versions A and B aloud. Have the student list every technique in Version B. Show the pacing bar graph.
10 minDescription
Revise three weak sentences together, then the student revises one from their own opening paragraph.
6 minRoots
Build words with chron, tele, photo and meter.
10 minPlan the draft
Review the student's story mountain. Decide together where the climax is and what dialogue to include. Assign the slow-motion activity before drafting.

Key questions to ask

  • What job is this line of dialogue doing?
  • Which moment in your story deserves slow motion? Why that one?
  • Can you replace that verb with one that shows how?
  • Where can you fast-forward?
  • How did you change by the end of your story?

Common mistakes to watch for

  • Putting commas and periods outside the closing quotation mark.
  • Writing all dialogue in one paragraph regardless of speaker.
  • Thinking every tag must be a fancy word instead of said.
  • Spending equal time on every event, so the climax feels rushed.

Support (if the student is struggling)

  • Provide a dialogue punctuation card with the five rules.
  • Let the student dictate the climax aloud while you type, then they revise it.
  • Give a transition word bank sorted by time order.

Challenge (if the student is ready for more)

  • Write the climax twice: once in present tense and once in past tense, and decide which is more powerful.
  • Use a flashback for one paragraph, marked with clear transitions.

Background for the teacher

The draft written this week is revised and finished in Week 5 as the Unit 1 Personal Narrative Project, scored with the project rubric. Mentor Version B is original to KCE. The draft is scored this week for completion and craft; the finished narrative is scored with the Week 5 project rubric.

Answer key: interactive lesson

Reconnect

  1. Which part of the plot is the turning point, where the tension is highest?
    Answer: climax
    The climax. Today you learn to slow it down.
  2. Your personal narrative is a true story about you, told with I and me. What point of view is that?
    Answer: first person
    First person uses I and me.
  3. Is perseverance a topic or a theme?
    Answer: topic
    One word is a topic. A theme is a full sentence about life.
  4. In "Mother to Son," what is life compared to, and what does the mother tell her son not to do? Answer in one or two sentences.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Life is compared to a staircase; Do not turn back, sit down or fall
    Model answer: Life is compared to a staircase, and the mother tells her son not to turn back, sit down on the steps or fall.
  5. Using the root struct, what does construct mean?
    Answer: to build
    con (together) + struct (build).
  6. Which STEAL method reveals character through what the character says aloud?
    Answer: speech
    Speech. Today you learn to write it correctly.

Learn 1: Writing Dialogue That Works

  1. Punctuate this correctly by typing it with quotation marks, commas and capitals: lets go home now said Mom
    Answer: "Let's go home now," said Mom.
    Quotation marks around the spoken words, a comma inside the closing quotation mark, then the tag. (Let's needs an apostrophe: let us.)
  2. When the speaker changes in dialogue, what must a writer start?
    Answer: a new paragraph
    Each new speaker gets a new paragraph so the reader can follow who is talking.
  3. Replace said with a tag that shows the speaker was scared: "Don't look down," I said. Type only the new tag.
    Answer: whispered (or stammered, gasped)
    A precise tag shows how the words were spoken. Use one only when it adds meaning.
  4. Write two lines of dialogue between yourself and someone in your personal narrative. Make the dialogue reveal something about one of you. Punctuate it correctly.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Correct quotation marks, commas and capitals; New paragraph (new line) for each speaker; Reveals character or moves the plot forward
    Model answer: "You're not scared, are you?" my cousin asked, already halfway up the ladder. "Of course not," I said, gripping the bottom rung so hard my knuckles turned white.

Learn 2: Pacing: Slow Down the Moment

  1. Find the simile in Version B. What is the quiet gym compared to?
    Answer: someone pressed mute
    "Like someone had pressed mute" compares the sudden silence to a muted sound.
  2. Name two senses Version B uses besides sight.
    Answer: smell (floor wax), hearing (heartbeat, buzzer), touch (the ball in the narrator's palms)
    Smell, sound and touch put the reader inside the moment.
  3. Write a one-sentence transition that fast-forwards through a week of time.
    Answer: For example: The rest of the week crawled by until Saturday finally came.
    A transition moves the reader quickly past time that does not matter to the story.

Learn 3: Description: Strong Verbs and Precise Nouns

  1. Revise this sentence with a strong verb and a precise noun: The bird went up into the tree.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Replaces went with a strong verb; Replaces bird or tree with a precise noun
    Model answer: The blue jay darted into the live oak.
  2. Which word is the strongest verb: moved, walked, stomped ?
    Answer: stomped
    Stomped shows how the person walked, and probably how they felt.
  3. Add transitions to show the order of events.
    Answer: At first / Then / Finally (other transitions that show order are fine)
    Transitions guide the reader through time.

Learn 4: Roots: Time, Distance, Light and Measure

  1. A personal narrative is usually told in chronological order. Using the root, what does that mean?
    Answer: in the order events happened in time
    Chron means time.
  2. Using roots, what does a telescope help you do?
    Answer: see things that are far away
    tele (far) + scope (look).
  3. Complete with root words.
    Answer: photosynthesis / thermometer / photo
    photosynthesis (light + putting together), thermometer (heat + measure), photograph (light + write).

Hands-On

  1. List the details you said out loud while acting in slow motion, and circle (put stars next to) your three best ones.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: At least six details from the acted climax; Details use several senses and thoughts; Three strongest details marked
    Model answer: rope burns my palms *; the water below looks brown and still; my cousin counting "three... two..."; I smell sunscreen and mud *; my stomach flips; I think "just let go" * (stars mark my three best details)

Think Deeper

  1. Write the complete first draft of your personal narrative. Use your story mountain, your Week 1 opening paragraph (revise it if you like), at least three lines of correctly punctuated dialogue, a slowed-down climax with sensory details, transitions, and an ending that shows how you changed or what you learned.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Ideas: a true story with a clear problem, climax and change; Organization: chronological order with transitions; strong opening and ending; Narrative craft: dialogue, sensory details, strong verbs, a slowed-down climax; Point of view: consistent first person; Conventions: correct dialogue punctuation, capitals and spelling
    Model answer: (Teacher note: no single model fits a personal story. A strong Grade 6 draft is 300 to 700 words, opens in the setting with sensory detail, uses at least three lines of punctuated dialogue, spends the most words on the climax, and ends by reflecting on what the writer learned, for example: "I still think about that ladder every time I am scared to try something. My hands shook then too, but I climbed anyway.")
  2. Reread your draft. Which part did you slow down the most, and why is that the right moment? Name one place you will add a strong verb or a sensory detail when you revise.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Identifies the slowed-down moment; Explains why it is the climax or most important moment; Names a specific revision
    Model answer: I slowed down the moment I let go of the rope swing, because that is when I stopped being afraid. When I revise, I will change "I went into the water" to "I plunged into the cold, brown water" in the falling action.

Exit Check

  1. Punctuate by typing it with quotation marks, capitals and punctuation: wait for me yelled Jordan
    Answer: "Wait for me!" yelled Jordan. (A comma also works.)
    Quotation marks around the spoken words with the comma or exclamation point inside, then the tag.
  2. Which part of a story should usually get the most words?
    Answer: climax
    Slow down the most important moment.
  3. Revise with a strong verb: The car went down the street fast.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Replaces went ... fast with a strong verb such as raced or zoomed
    Model answer: The car raced down the street.
  4. What does the root chron mean?
    Answer: time
    Chron means time.
  5. What does the root photo mean?
    Answer: light
    Photo means light.
  6. Name one transition word or phrase that shows something happened all at once.
    Answer: suddenly (or all of a sudden, without warning)
    These transitions signal a sudden change.

Answer key: printable page

  1. Rewrite with correct punctuation and paragraphs: did you finish your chores asked Dad not yet I said
    Answer: "Did you finish your chores?" asked Dad. / "Not yet," I said. (New paragraph for each speaker.)
  2. Write a rushed one-sentence version of an exciting moment from your life. Then rewrite it slowed down with at least two senses and one thought.
    Answer: Answers vary; the slowed version must use two or more senses and a thought.
  3. Replace each weak verb with a strong verb: The baby cried ______ She walked into the room ______ He ate his lunch ______
    Answer: For example: wailed; stormed or tiptoed; devoured
  4. Write three transitions that show time order.
    Answer: For example: at first, later that day, finally
  5. Write the meaning of each root and one word: chron ____ tele ____ photo ____ meter ____
    Answer: time (chronological); far (telescope); light (photograph); measure (thermometer)
  6. Why should a writer slow down the climax but speed through other parts?
    Answer: The climax is the most important moment, so slowing it down builds tension and lets the reader feel it; other parts only connect events.
