Unit 1: How We Know the Past · Week 4 · World and American History · Teacher Plan

Farming, Villages and the First Towns

Lesson length: 60 minutes live, plus the after-class interactive lesson

Objectives

  • Explain what domestication is and how it differs from gathering and hunting.
  • Locate the places where farming began and recognize that it started independently on several continents, including Africa.
  • Compare the lifestyles of hunter-gatherers and early farmers.
  • Describe early towns such as Jericho and Çatalhöyük and the evidence historians use to study them.
  • Analyze the chain of effects of farming, including its costs, and argue whether it was a good trade.

Standards

  • SS.6.W.2.1: Compare the lifestyles of hunter-gatherers with those of settlers of early agricultural communities.
  • SS.6.W.2.2: Describe how the developments of agriculture and metallurgy related to settlement, population growth, and the emergence of civilization.
  • SS.6.W.2.3 (preview): Identify the characteristics of civilization.
  • SS.6.G.2.4 (preview): Explain how the geographical location of ancient civilizations contributed to the culture and politics of those societies.
  • C3 Framework D2.His.14.6-8: Explain multiple causes and effects of events and developments in the past.

Materials

  • The interactive lesson
  • A world map or globe
  • A flat stone or cutting board, a smooth stone or mortar and pestle, and 1/4 cup of dry grain
  • A timer, a tablespoon, a fine strainer and a towel
  • Printable practice page

Vocabulary

  • domestication: Changing plants or animals over many generations, by choosing which ones to grow or breed, so they become more useful to people.
  • agriculture: Growing crops and raising animals for food and other products.
  • Neolithic: The New Stone Age, when many people began farming and living in permanent villages.
  • surplus: More than is needed right away; extra food that can be stored or traded.
  • specialization: When people do one kind of work, such as making pottery, instead of all producing their own food.
  • pastoralist: A person who raises herds of animals, often moving with them to find grass and water.
  • settlement: A place where people live permanently.
  • independent invention: When different groups develop the same idea on their own, without learning it from each other.

Key questions to ask

  • What evidence do we have for this, and how sure are scientists?
  • Who gained from farming, and who paid the costs?
  • Why did farming start in so many places in roughly the same era?
  • What foods on your table today came from Africa, the Americas or Asia?

Common mistakes to watch for

  • Farming was invented once in the Middle East and spread everywhere.
  • Farming was an obvious improvement that made everyone healthier.
  • Early towns looked like modern towns with streets and stores.
  • Africa had no role in early agriculture.

Support (if the student is struggling)

  • Provide the crop table as a printed card.
  • Give sentence starters for the argument: I believe... One piece of evidence is... Some people argue... However...
  • Let the student talk through the argument aloud before writing.

Challenge (if the student is ready for more)

  • Research the Green Sahara: how did people in the Sahara live when it was grassland about 10,000 to 5,000 years ago, and what happened when it dried?
  • Investigate one crop's journey, such as coffee from Ethiopia (cultivated much later, about 500 to 600 years ago), and map how it spread around the world.

Background for the teacher

Archaeologists generally recognize somewhere between about seven and more than a dozen independent or early centers of plant domestication, depending on definitions. The dates in the table are rounded and represent current estimates. Early dates for cattle management at Nabta Playa and Bir Kiseiba (around 9,000 or more years ago) are argued by Fred Wendorf and colleagues and remain debated. Sorghum domestication in eastern Sudan is supported by finds at Butana Group site KG23 near Kassala around 5,000 years ago. Pearl millet domestication in the West African Sahel is dated to about 4,500 years ago. Göbekli Tepe's earliest construction is about 9500 BCE; recent work has found evidence of more permanent occupation than first thought, but its builders did not yet rely on domesticated crops. Çatalhöyük was occupied roughly 7100 to 5950 BCE. The evidence that early farming populations often show reduced stature and increased dental disease comes from bioarchaeological studies across several regions, though patterns vary.

Answer key: interactive lesson

Reconnect

  1. On which continent did Homo sapiens first appear?
    Answer: africa
    The oldest known Homo sapiens fossils, from Jebel Irhoud in Morocco, are about 300,000 years old.
  2. About how many years old are the Jebel Irhoud fossils?
    Answer: 300000 years
    About 300,000 years.
  3. In undisturbed layers of earth, are deeper layers usually older or younger?
    Answer: older
    Layers pile up over time, so deeper usually means older (the law of superposition).
  4. All humans share about what percent of their DNA?
    Answer: 99.9 %
    About 99.9%, which is why races are not separate biological groups.
  5. Describe how people got food for almost all of human history before farming. Give two specific examples.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Gathering and hunting (or fishing); Two concrete examples
    Model answer: They gathered and hunted. For example, they collected wild seeds, fruits and shellfish, and hunted animals like antelope or fished in rivers and lakes.
  6. Name one kind of evidence from Week 3 that tells us early humans planned and made art, and explain what it shows.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Names real evidence (Blombos ochre or beads, tools); Explains what it shows about thinking
    Model answer: At Blombos Cave in South Africa, people carved patterns into ochre about 75,000 years ago, which shows they used symbols and planned ahead.

Learn 1: Domestication: Changing Plants and Animals

  1. Explain in your own words how farmers changed wild wheat without any modern science. Use the word generations .
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Farmers chose seeds from useful plants; Replanted those seeds again and again; The plant changed over many generations
    Model answer: Farmers kept seeds from the wheat plants that held on to their seeds and planted them the next year. They did this again and again for many generations, so the wheat slowly changed into a plant that keeps its seeds until harvest.
  2. What wild plant in Mexico did farmers turn into maize?
    Answer: teosinte
    Teosinte, a wild grass with tiny ears.
  3. Why would early herders breed the calmest animals instead of the biggest or strongest? Give one reason.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Calm animals are safer and easier to manage, gather and keep
    Model answer: Calm animals are easier and safer to keep in a pen and lead to water, so they are less likely to run away or hurt someone.

Learn 2: Farming Began in Many Places

  1. About how many years ago did farming begin in the Fertile Crescent?
    Answer: 11000 years
    About 11,000 years ago.
  2. Name the region where each crop was first domesticated.
    Answer: Andes; Sudan (eastern Sahel); China
    Potatoes in the Andes, sorghum in Sudan and the eastern Sahel, rice in southern China.
  3. Why is it important that farming began independently in many places, instead of being invented once and spread? What does this tell us about people around the world?
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Explains independent invention; Concludes that people everywhere were inventive and solved problems; Avoids the idea that one group taught everyone
    Model answer: It shows that people in Africa, Asia, the Americas and New Guinea all figured out farming on their own, using the plants around them. No single group was the only inventor; people everywhere were smart problem solvers.
  4. Name two different crops first domesticated in Africa.
    Answer: For example: sorghum and coffee
    Sorghum, pearl millet, African rice, yams, coffee, okra, watermelon and teff all come from Africa.

Learn 3: The First Towns

  1. How did people get into their homes at Çatalhöyük?
    Answer: through a hole in the roof, by ladder
    Houses had no front doors on streets; people climbed down ladders through roof openings.
  2. Göbekli Tepe was built by people who mostly gathered and hunted. Why did this surprise archaeologists, and what new question did it raise?
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Archaeologists expected big building only after farming; New question: did gathering for building or ceremonies help lead to farming?
    Model answer: Archaeologists thought people needed farming and stored food before they could build huge stone monuments. Göbekli Tepe raised a new question: maybe gathering in large groups to build and hold ceremonies helped lead people toward farming.
  3. Look at the drawing of Çatalhöyük. Give one advantage and one disadvantage of building houses wall to wall with roof entrances.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: A reasonable advantage (protection, warmth, shared walls save material); A reasonable disadvantage (fire spreads, hard to carry things, crowded, disease)
    Model answer: An advantage is protection: with no doors at ground level, animals and raiders could not easily get in, and shared walls saved mud bricks. A disadvantage is that carrying heavy loads up and down ladders would be hard, and a fire or disease could spread quickly.

Learn 4: Was Farming a Good Trade?

  1. What is the word for extra food that can be stored or traded?
    Answer: surplus
    A surplus made specialization possible.
  2. Explain why a surplus made it possible for some people to become potters or builders.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Others produced enough food for them; So they could spend time on other work
    Model answer: If farmers grew more food than their families needed, they could trade some to the potter, so the potter did not have to grow food and could spend the day making pots.
  3. Give two pieces of evidence that early farmers were sometimes less healthy than foragers, and explain why.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Two pieces of skeletal or other evidence (height, teeth, disease); Explains the cause (less varied diet, crowding, animals)
    Model answer: Skeletons show many early farmers were shorter and had more tooth decay than foragers. They ate mostly a few starchy crops instead of many wild foods, and living crowded together with animals spread disease.

Hands-On

  1. Record how much flour you made in 10 minutes, then calculate how long it would take to grind 3 cups (48 tablespoons). Show your math.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Records the amount ground with units; Correctly sets up the rate calculation; Gives an answer in minutes or hours
    Model answer: I made 2 tablespoons in 10 minutes, which is 5 minutes per tablespoon. 48 tablespoons × 5 minutes = 240 minutes, or 4 hours for one loaf.
  2. At Abu Hureyra in Syria, archaeologists found skeletons of early farming women with worn toes, knees and lower backs. Using your experience, explain what might have caused this.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Connects kneeling and pushing to the injuries; Notes the daily hours of grinding; Uses the skeleton as evidence about daily life
    Model answer: Grinding meant kneeling with toes bent and pushing forward with the back and arms for hours every day. After years, this would wear down the toes, knees and lower back, so the skeletons are evidence of how hard daily grinding was.

Think Deeper

  1. Was the switch to farming a good trade for ordinary people in the first villages? Take a side. Give at least two pieces of evidence for your side and answer one argument from the other side.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Clear claim; Two pieces of accurate evidence; Responds to a counterargument; Organized paragraph with a conclusion
    Model answer: For ordinary people in the first villages, farming was not a good trade at first. Skeletons show farmers were often shorter and had more tooth decay than foragers, because they ate a few crops instead of many wild foods. Farming also meant hours of hard work, like grinding grain on knees every day, and one drought could cause hunger. Some people argue farming was worth it because a surplus let villages grow and specialists like potters appear. That is true for society over thousands of years, but the first farmers paid the cost in their health and work. So farming helped humanity in the long run, but it was a hard deal for the first people who made it.
  2. Compare a day in the life of a forager family and an early farming family. Write at least two similarities and three differences.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Two accurate similarities; Three accurate differences (food, housing, possessions, work, group size); Specific details
    Model answer: Both families needed water, made stone tools and shared food with their group. Foragers moved with the seasons and carried few belongings, while farmers lived in one place in mud-brick houses with stored grain. Foragers ate many kinds of wild food, but farmers ate mostly a few crops. Farmers worked long hours weeding and grinding, and they lived in larger groups with more chance of disease.

Exit Check

  1. What does domestication mean?
    Answer: changing plants or animals over generations to be more useful to people
    Domestication is changing plants or animals over generations by choosing which to grow or breed.
  2. Name three different regions where farming began independently.
    Answer: For example: the Fertile Crescent, the Andes and the West African Sahel
    Regions include the Fertile Crescent, China, Mesoamerica, the Andes, New Guinea, Sudan and the eastern Sahel, the West African Sahel, the Ethiopian Highlands and eastern North America.
  3. Name one crop first domesticated in Africa.
    Answer: sorghum (or pearl millet, coffee, yams, okra, teff)
    Africa gave the world sorghum, pearl millet, African rice, yams, coffee, okra, watermelon and teff.
  4. Which early town had houses with no streets, entered through the roof?
    Answer: Çatalhöyük
    Çatalhöyük, in central Türkiye.
  5. Name one benefit and one cost of farming for early villages.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: A real benefit (surplus, specialists, larger groups); A real cost (poorer diet, disease, hard labor, famine risk, inequality)
    Model answer: A benefit was a surplus of stored food that let villages grow. A cost was a less varied diet that made many people shorter and less healthy.

Answer key: printable page

  1. Define domestication and give one plant and one animal example.
    Answer: Changing plants or animals over generations by choosing which to grow or breed. Examples: wheat, maize; goats, cattle.
  2. List four regions where farming began independently and one crop from each.
    Answer: For example: Fertile Crescent, wheat; China, rice; Andes, potatoes; Sudan, sorghum.
  3. Name three crops first domesticated in Africa.
    Answer: For example: sorghum, pearl millet, coffee (also yams, okra, watermelon, teff, African rice).
  4. Describe one feature of Çatalhöyük and one of Göbekli Tepe.
    Answer: Çatalhöyük: houses packed together, entered through the roof. Göbekli Tepe: huge carved stone pillars built by people who mostly foraged.
  5. Fill in the chain: Farming → ________ → More people → ________ → Trade and rules
    Answer: Surplus; Specialists
  6. Give two costs of early farming and explain one.
    Answer: For example: poorer diet (fewer kinds of food), more disease (crowding and animals), hard labor, famine risk, inequality.
