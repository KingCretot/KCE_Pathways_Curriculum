Unit 2: Fractions and Ratios · Week 9 · Math · Teacher Plan

Ratio Tables and Equivalent Ratios

Lesson length: 60 minutes live, plus the after-class interactive lesson

Objectives

  • Build ratio tables using multiplication, division and addition of columns.
  • Find missing values in a ratio table.
  • Decide whether two ratios are equivalent using a table.
  • Graph the pairs in a ratio table and describe the pattern.
  • Use price tables to compare deals and choose a better buy.

Standards

  • MA.6.AR.3.1: Given a real-world context, write and interpret ratios to show the relative sizes of two quantities using appropriate notation: a/b, a to b, or a:b where b ≠ 0.
  • MA.6.AR.3.5: Solve mathematical and real-world problems involving ratios, rates and unit rates, including comparisons, mixtures, ratios of lengths and conversions within the same measurement system.
  • MA.K12.MTR.2.1: Demonstrate understanding by representing problems in multiple ways.
  • MA.K12.MTR.6.1: Assess the reasonableness of solutions.
  • Financial literacy: comparing prices and weighing costs such as travel (Jump$tart Spending).

Materials

  • The interactive lesson
  • Flour, salt, water, measuring cups, a bowl
  • Graph paper and colored pencils
  • A grocery ad or two receipts
  • Printable practice page

Vocabulary

  • ratio table: A table that lists equivalent ratios in columns.
  • scale factor: The number you multiply (or divide) both quantities by to get an equivalent ratio.
  • equivalent ratios: Ratios that make the same comparison, such as 2 : 1 and 6 : 3.
  • price table: A ratio table that pairs a quantity of an item with its cost.
  • ordered pair: A pair of numbers (x, y) that locates a point on a graph.
  • origin: The point (0, 0) where the axes of a graph meet.

Key questions to ask

  • What did you do to the top number? Did you do the same to the bottom?
  • Which column can you add or multiply to reach the number you need?
  • What does a steeper line mean?
  • Is the cheaper price worth the extra trip?

Common mistakes to watch for

  • Adding the same number to both rows of a table.
  • Thinking ratio tables must go up by ones.
  • Reading the steeper line as the better deal.
  • Comparing prices at different quantities.

Support (if the student is struggling)

  • Use a two-row blank table template.
  • Have the student write the scale factor above each column.
  • Use real measuring cups for the dough table.

Challenge (if the student is ready for more)

  • Graph the fertilizer table and the dough table on the same axes and compare the lines.
  • Write your own price table problem from a real grocery ad.

Background for the teacher

This lesson builds on MA.6.AR.3.1 and the ratio-reasoning parts of MA.6.AR.3.5 using ratio tables and graphs, without computing unit rates; unit rates (MA.6.AR.3.2) are taught in Unit 3. The graph section previews proportional relationships formally taught in Grade 7. The salt dough recipe (2 parts flour : 1 part salt : 1 part water) matches the Social Studies coin activity this week. Fertilizer ratios vary by product; the 3 tablespoons per 2 gallons figure is a made-up example for practice.

Answer key: interactive lesson

Reconnect

  1. Write 16 : 24 in simplest form.
    Answer: 2 : 3
    The GCF of 16 and 24 is 8: 16 ÷ 8 = 2 and 24 ÷ 8 = 3.
  2. Hummingbird nectar is 1 cup of sugar for every 4 cups of water. How many cups of water go with 3 cups of sugar?
    Answer: 12 cups
    1 : 4 = 3 : 12. Multiply both parts by 3.
  3. Week 8 warned that adding the same number to both parts of a ratio does not keep it equivalent. Explain why, using 1 : 4.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Adding changes the comparison; e.g. 1 : 4 to 2 : 5 has more sugar per cup of water; Multiplying (2 : 8) keeps the comparison
    Model answer: If you add 1 to each part, 1 : 4 becomes 2 : 5, which has more sugar for each cup of water, so it is sweeter. Multiplying both by 2 gives 2 : 8, which keeps the same comparison.
  4. Which costs less for 12 cans: 4 for $3 or 6 for $5? Type the lower price for 12 cans.
    Answer: $9
    4 for $3 × 3 = 12 for $9; 6 for $5 × 2 = 12 for $10. Today, price tables make comparisons like this faster.

Learn 1: Building a Ratio Table

  1. In the salt dough table, how many cups of flour go with 7 cups of salt?
    Answer: 14 cups
    Flour is always 2 times the salt: 2 × 7 = 14.
  2. Complete the ratio table for fertilizer: 3 tablespoons for every 2 gallons of water.
    Answer: 9; 21; 10
    Each column is a multiple of 3 : 2. 3 × 3 = 9; 3 × 7 = 21 (since 14 = 2 × 7); 2 × 5 = 10 (since 15 = 3 × 5).
  3. A ratio table for a smoothie shows 6 bananas : 4 cups of milk. Divide to find how much milk goes with 3 bananas.
    Answer: 2 cups
    Divide both by 2: 6 : 4 = 3 : 2.
  4. Explain why adding the columns 4 : 2 and 6 : 3 gives an equivalent ratio, but adding 2 to each part of 4 : 2 does not.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Both columns already have the same 2 : 1 relationship, so their sum (10 : 5) does too; Adding 2 to each part (6 : 4) changes the relationship
    Model answer: 4 : 2 and 6 : 3 both have flour twice the salt, so together, 10 : 5, flour is still twice the salt. Adding 2 to each part of 4 : 2 gives 6 : 4, and 6 is not twice 4, so the relationship changes.

Learn 2: Are These Ratios Equivalent?

  1. Is 6 : 8 equivalent to 15 : 20? Type yes or no.
    Answer: yes
    6 : 8 = 3 : 4 and 15 : 20 = 3 : 4.
  2. Is 5 : 3 equivalent to 12 : 8? Type yes or no.
    Answer: no
    12 : 8 = 3 : 2, but 5 : 3 is already in simplest form, and 5 : 3 is not 3 : 2.
  3. Kwame mixes 3 cans of yellow paint with 2 cans of blue. Ama mixes 9 cans of yellow with 5 cans of blue. Will their greens be the same shade? Use a ratio table to explain.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Table for 3 : 2 shows 9 : 6, not 9 : 5; Not the same; Ama's has less blue for the same yellow, so it is a lighter, more yellow green
    Model answer: A ratio table for 3 : 2 goes 3 : 2, 6 : 4, 9 : 6. Ama used 9 : 5, not 9 : 6, so the ratios are not equivalent. Her green has less blue for the same amount of yellow, so it will be lighter and more yellow.

Learn 3: Graphing a Ratio Table

  1. Write the ordered pairs (limes, cost) for the store's first three columns: 5 for $2, 10 for ?, 15 for ?.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: (5, 2), (10, 4), (15, 6)
    Model answer: (5, 2), (10, 4), (15, 6)
  2. Explain how you can tell from the graph which store has the better price, without doing any arithmetic.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: The less steep line is cheaper: for the same number of limes, its point is lower (costs less)
    Model answer: The market's line is less steep, so for the same number of limes its points are lower on the graph, which means they cost less.
  3. Why does the line for a price table pass through (0, 0)? Would that be true if the store charged a $1 bag fee no matter how many limes you bought?
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: 0 items cost $0, so (0, 0) fits the ratio; With a fixed fee, 0 limes would cost $1, so the line would not pass through the origin and the table would not show equivalent ratios
    Model answer: Zero limes cost zero dollars, so (0, 0) is on the line. With a $1 bag fee, even 0 limes would cost $1, so the line would start at $1 instead of the origin, and the costs would not be equivalent ratios anymore.

Learn 4: Money: Price Tables and Better Buys

  1. Using the butcher's table, how much would 15 pounds of chicken cost?
    Answer: $35
    15 = 3 × 5, so the cost is $7 × 5 = $35 (or add the 3-pound and 12-pound columns: $7 + $28).
  2. How much would a family save on 24 pounds by buying from the butcher instead of the grocery store?
    Answer: $4
    Butcher: 24 lb = 8 × 3 lb, so 8 × $7 = $56. Grocery: 24 lb = 6 × 4 lb, so 6 × $10 = $60. Savings: $60 − $56 = $4.
  3. The butcher is 20 miles farther away than the grocery store, round trip. Should the family drive there to save $2 on 12 pounds? Explain what else they should consider.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Considers the cost of gas and time for the extra miles (likely more than $2); Concludes with reasoning: probably not worth it just for chicken, unless combined with other errands or quality matters
    Model answer: Probably not. Driving 20 extra miles uses gas that likely costs more than the $2 saved, plus extra time. It might be worth it if they were going that way anyway or if the butcher's chicken is better quality.

Hands-On

  1. Copy your ratio table for flour, salt and water at 1/4, 1/2, 1, 2 and 3 batches.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Correct amounts: 1/4 batch = 1/2 c flour, 1/4 c salt, 1/4 c water; 1/2 batch = 1, 1/2, 1/2; 1 = 2, 1, 1; 2 = 4, 2, 2; 3 = 6, 3, 3
    Model answer: 1/4 batch: 1/2 cup flour, 1/4 cup salt, 1/4 cup water. 1/2 batch: 1, 1/2, 1/2. 1 batch: 2, 1, 1. 2 batches: 4, 2, 2. 3 batches: 6, 3, 3.
  2. How many coins did your test batch make? Show how you used that number and your table to decide how much dough your family needed. Did it work?
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Reports test result; Uses ratio reasoning to scale up to the number needed; Reflects on the outcome
    Model answer: The 1/4 batch made 3 coins. Our family of 4 needs 8 coins. 1/2 batch makes 6 coins and 1 batch makes 12, so I made 1 batch to be safe. We had dough for 4 extra coins, so 3/4 of a batch would have been closer.

Think Deeper

  1. A family recipe for fufu-style plantain balls uses 6 plantains for every 4 servings. Make a ratio table to find how many plantains are needed for 2, 10 and 18 servings. Explain each step and the operation you used.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Correct values: 2 servings 3 plantains; 10 servings 15; 18 servings 27; Names operations (divide by 2, multiply, add columns); Clear table
    Model answer: Start: 6 plantains : 4 servings. Divide both by 2: 3 plantains : 2 servings. Multiply 3 : 2 by 5: 15 plantains : 10 servings. Multiply 3 : 2 by 9: 27 plantains : 18 servings. I could also add the columns for 10 and 8 servings (15 + 12 = 27) to check.
  2. Two phone plans: Plan A costs $15 for every 3 GB of data. Plan B costs $24 for every 4 GB. Make a price table for each up to 12 GB, decide which is the better buy for 12 GB, and explain one reason a family might still pick the other plan.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Plan A: 3, 6, 9, 12 GB = $15, $30, $45, $60; Plan B: 4, 8, 12 GB = $24, $48, $72; Plan A is cheaper for 12 GB ($60 vs $72); Reasonable reason for B (better coverage, comes in different bundle sizes, other features)
    Model answer: Plan A: 3 GB $15, 6 GB $30, 9 GB $45, 12 GB $60. Plan B: 4 GB $24, 8 GB $48, 12 GB $72. Plan A is the better buy for 12 GB by $12. A family might still pick Plan B if it has better coverage where they live, since cheap data does not help if the phone has no signal.

Exit Check

  1. Complete the ratio table for 5 : 2.
    Answer: 20; 4
    5 × 4 = 20 (because 8 = 2 × 4); 2 × 2 = 4 (because 10 = 5 × 2).
  2. Is 4 : 10 equivalent to 6 : 15? Type yes or no.
    Answer: yes
    Both simplify to 2 : 5.
  3. Bananas are 6 for $2. Using a price table, how much do 21 bananas cost?
    Answer: $7
    3 bananas cost $1 (divide by 2), and 21 = 3 × 7, so $7.
  4. Two price tables are graphed. Line A is steeper than Line B. Which shows the better buy, and why?
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Line B; Less steep: lower cost for the same quantity
    Model answer: Line B, because it is less steep, so the same amount costs less.

Answer key: printable page

  1. Complete the ratio table for 4 : 3: top row 4, 8, ___, 20; bottom row 3, ___, 9, ___.
    Answer: 12; 6; 15
  2. A drink mix is 2 scoops for 3 cups of water. How many scoops for 15 cups?
    Answer: 10 scoops
  3. Is 12 : 18 equivalent to 10 : 15? Explain.
    Answer: Yes; both simplify to 2 : 3.
  4. Write ordered pairs for 3 apples for $2 at 3, 6 and 9 apples.
    Answer: (3, 2), (6, 4), (9, 6)
  5. Pasta: Store A, 2 boxes for $3. Store B, 3 boxes for $5. Use price tables to compare 6 boxes. Which is better?
    Answer: A: $9; B: $10. Store A.
  6. Explain why adding two columns of a ratio table gives an equivalent ratio.
    Answer: Both columns have the same relationship, so their sum keeps it.
