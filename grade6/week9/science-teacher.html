Unit 2: Cells, the Building Blocks of Life · Week 9 · Life Science · Teacher Plan

How Things Move In and Out of Cells

Lesson length: 60 minutes live, plus the after-class interactive lesson

Objectives

  • Explain diffusion as the movement of particles from higher to lower concentration.
  • Explain osmosis as the diffusion of water across a membrane, and predict which way water will move.
  • Distinguish passive transport from active transport.
  • Run a controlled osmosis investigation with potato strips and explain the results.
  • Apply osmosis to real situations such as wilting plants and drinking seawater.

Standards

  • SC.6.L.14.3: Recognize and explore how cells of all organisms undergo similar processes to maintain homeostasis, including extracting energy from food, getting rid of waste, and reproducing.
  • SC.6.L.14.4: Compare and contrast the structure and function of major organelles of plant and animal cells, including cell wall, cell membrane, nucleus, cytoplasm, chloroplasts, mitochondria, and vacuoles.
  • SC.6.N.1.1: Define a problem from the sixth grade curriculum, use appropriate reference materials to support scientific understanding, plan and carry out scientific investigation of various types, such as systematic observations or experiments, identify variables, collect and organize data, interpret data in charts, tables, and graphics, analyze information, make predictions, and defend conclusions.
  • SC.6.N.1.2: Explain why scientific investigations should be replicable.
  • NGSS MS-LS1-2: Develop and use a model to describe the function of a cell as a whole and ways the parts of cells contribute to the function.

Materials

  • The interactive lesson
  • A clear glass of hot tap water, a glass of cold water and food coloring (for a quick diffusion demo)
  • A raw potato, a knife and cutting board (adult), a ruler with millimeters, two cups and a plate, salt, paper towels, a timer
  • Optional: a stalk of limp celery in water overnight; a kitchen scale
  • Printable practice page

Vocabulary

  • concentration: How much of a substance is packed into a certain space or amount of liquid.
  • diffusion: The movement of particles from an area of higher concentration to an area of lower concentration.
  • osmosis: The diffusion of water across a membrane that lets water through but blocks some other particles.
  • selectively permeable: Letting some substances pass through but not others; the cell membrane is selectively permeable.
  • passive transport: Movement across a membrane that needs no energy from the cell (diffusion and osmosis).
  • active transport: Movement across a membrane that uses the cell's energy, often to move particles from lower to higher concentration.
  • aquaporin: A protein channel in the membrane that lets water pass through quickly.

Key questions to ask

  • Where are the particles crowded, and where will they go?
  • Which side has more dissolved particles? Which way will water move?
  • Does this need energy? How do you know?
  • What was your control, and why did you need it?

Common mistakes to watch for

  • Salt moves into the cell in osmosis (it is water that moves).
  • Diffusion stops when particles are evenly spread (they keep moving, but evenly in both directions).
  • Water moves toward the side with more water.
  • Only animal cells use active transport.

Support (if the student is struggling)

  • Use the phrase "water follows salt" as a memory aid.
  • Provide a blank data table for the potato test.
  • Act out diffusion: the family crowds in one corner, then slowly spreads around the room.

Challenge (if the student is ready for more)

  • Run the design from Think Deeper and graph the results.
  • Research saltwater intrusion into Florida's coastal aquifers and explain the role of osmosis in its effects on plants.

Background for the teacher

Osmosis is described here at a Grade 6 level as water diffusing toward higher solute concentration; terms like hypotonic and hypertonic are deliberately left out. Seawater averages about 3.5 percent salt, nearly four times the salt concentration of human blood plasma (about 0.9 percent, the concentration of normal saline). Peter Agre identified the aquaporin protein (AQP1) in 1992 and shared the 2003 Nobel Prize in Chemistry. Mangroves use salt exclusion at the roots (red mangroves) and salt-excreting glands in the leaves (black and white mangroves). The Coleridge lines are from The Rime of the Ancient Mariner as revised in 1817 (the 1798 original reads "Ne any drop to drink"), in the public domain. The 30-minute potato soak gives measurable changes of a few millimeters; longer soaks give larger changes.

Answer key: interactive lesson

Reconnect

  1. Which large plant organelle stores water and keeps the cell firm?
    Answer: central vacuole
    The central vacuole. Today you will see how water moves into and out of it.
  2. Through what tiny openings does carbon dioxide enter a leaf?
    Answer: stomata
    Stomata. Carbon dioxide reaches the leaf cells by diffusion, one of today's processes.
  3. In your own words, explain why a drooping basil plant stood up again after it was watered.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Water refilled the central vacuoles, which pushed against the cell walls and made the cells firm
    Model answer: The water filled the central vacuoles again, and they pushed against the stiff cell walls, so the cells became firm and the plant stood up.
  4. In the leaf-disk investigation, why did the disks float, and what was the independent variable?
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Oxygen from photosynthesis collected in the disks; Independent variable: baking soda (carbon dioxide) or light
    Model answer: Photosynthesis made oxygen, which filled the disks and made them float. The independent variable was the baking soda, which supplied carbon dioxide.

Learn 1: Diffusion: From Crowded to Spread Out

  1. Someone opens a jar of garlic in the kitchen. A few minutes later, you smell it in the living room. Explain this using the words particles, concentration and diffusion.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Garlic particles move from high concentration (near the jar) to low concentration (living room); This spreading is diffusion
    Model answer: Garlic particles were crowded near the jar, where the concentration was high. They moved and spread out toward the living room, where the concentration was low, until I could smell them. That is diffusion.
  2. Predict: will food coloring spread faster in hot water or cold water? Explain why.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Hot water; Particles move faster at higher temperature, so diffusion is faster
    Model answer: Hot water, because particles move faster when they are warmer, so the coloring spreads more quickly.
  3. Oxygen is more concentrated in your blood than inside your muscle cells. Will oxygen diffuse into the cells or out of them?
    Answer: into the cells
    Particles diffuse from high to low concentration: from the blood into the cells.

Learn 2: Osmosis: When Water Moves

  1. A plant cell is placed in very salty water. Will water move into the cell or out of it?
    Answer: out of the cell
    There are more dissolved particles outside, so water moves out toward the salty side, and the cell shrinks.
  2. Limp celery is put in a glass of fresh water overnight and becomes crisp. Explain what happened, using osmosis, vacuole and cell wall.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Fresh water has fewer dissolved particles than the cells, so water moved into the cells by osmosis; The vacuoles filled and pressed against the cell walls, making the cells firm
    Model answer: The cells had more dissolved particles than the fresh water, so water moved into them by osmosis. The central vacuoles filled up and pushed against the cell walls, which made the cells firm and the celery crisp.
  3. Return to the sailors. Explain why drinking seawater makes a person more dehydrated, not less.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Seawater is saltier than body fluids, so water moves out of cells by osmosis; The kidneys need extra water to remove the salt, so the body loses more water than it gained
    Model answer: Seawater is nearly four times as salty as blood, so the salty fluid around the cells pulls water out of them by osmosis. The kidneys also need extra water to get rid of all that salt, so the person loses more water than they drank.
  4. Why would it be dangerous to put pure water directly into a person's bloodstream instead of an IV solution with the right amount of salt?
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Pure water has fewer dissolved particles, so water would rush into blood cells; Animal cells have no wall, so they could swell and burst
    Model answer: Pure water has fewer dissolved particles than the blood cells, so water would rush into them by osmosis. Blood cells have no cell wall, so they could swell and burst.

Learn 3: Active Transport: Pumping Uphill

  1. Explain the difference between passive and active transport. Use the hill comparison and the word energy.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Passive: high to low concentration, no energy, like rolling downhill (diffusion, osmosis); Active: low to high, needs energy and pumps, like pushing uphill
    Model answer: Passive transport moves particles from high to low concentration, like a ball rolling downhill, so it needs no energy. Active transport moves particles from low to high concentration, like pushing a ball uphill, so the cell must use energy and protein pumps.
  2. A root cell already holds more of a mineral than the soil around it, yet it keeps taking more in.
    Answer: active; mitochondria
    Moving from low to high concentration needs energy from the mitochondria.
  3. Most garden plants die when salty seawater floods their soil, but mangroves thrive. Use osmosis to explain why salt kills garden plants, and explain how mangroves survive.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Salty soil pulls water out of root cells (or stops them from taking water in) by osmosis, so plants wilt and die; Mangroves use membranes and pumps (active transport) to keep out or remove salt
    Model answer: In salty soil there are more dissolved particles outside the roots, so osmosis pulls water out of the root cells instead of in, and the plant wilts and dies. Mangroves use special membranes and pumps in their roots and leaves to keep salt out or get rid of it, which takes energy.

Hands-On

  1. Make a data table: cup, starting length, ending length, change, and how stiff or floppy each strip felt. What pattern do you see?
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Complete table with measurements for all three conditions; Change calculated; Clear description of the pattern
    Model answer: Fresh: 60 mm to 62 mm (+2), stiff and snappy. Salty: 60 mm to 56 mm (−4), soft and bendy. Air: 60 mm to 60 mm (0), a little dry. Fresh-water strips grew and got stiff; salt-water strips shrank and went floppy.
  2. Write a claim, evidence and reasoning (CER) explaining your results. Name your independent variable, dependent variable and control.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Claim: water moved into cells in fresh water and out of cells in salt water; Evidence: specific measurements and stiffness from the table; Reasoning: osmosis; water moves toward the side with more dissolved particles; vacuoles filled or shrank; Variables: independent = type of water; dependent = change in length (or stiffness); control = air strips (or same size, same time)
    Model answer: Claim: Water moved into the potato cells in fresh water and out of them in salt water. Evidence: The fresh-water strips grew from 60 to 62 mm and became stiff, while the salt-water strips shrank from 60 to 56 mm and went floppy. Reasoning: By osmosis, water moves toward the side with more dissolved particles. The potato cells had more dissolved particles than fresh water, so water moved in, filling the vacuoles. The salt water had more dissolved particles than the cells, so water moved out and the vacuoles shrank. The independent variable was the type of water, the dependent variable was the change in length, and the strips on the dry plate were the control.

Think Deeper

  1. Cucumbers become pickles by soaking in salty brine. Pickles are shrunken and wrinkly compared with fresh cucumbers. Explain what happens to the cucumber's cells during pickling, then explain why salting meat or fish helped people preserve food for thousands of years before refrigerators.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Brine is saltier than the cells, so water moves out by osmosis, and the cucumber shrinks; Salt also pulls water out of bacteria and mold cells, so they cannot grow, which preserves food; Uses osmosis and concentration correctly
    Model answer: The brine has many more dissolved particles than the cucumber's cells, so water moves out of the cells by osmosis. The cells shrink, so the pickle becomes smaller and wrinkly. Salting meat or fish works the same way on bacteria and mold: the salt pulls water out of their cells by osmosis, so they cannot grow and spoil the food. That is why people have used salt to preserve food for thousands of years.
  2. Design an investigation to find the salt concentration at which a potato strip neither gains nor loses length. Describe the solutions you would make, what you would measure and how you would find the answer.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Several solutions with increasing salt (for example 0, 1/4, 1/2, 1 and 2 teaspoons per cup); Same size strips and time; measure change in length; repeated trials; Find where change is zero (between the last gain and the first loss), possibly using a graph
    Model answer: I would make five cups with 0, 1/4, 1/2, 1 and 2 teaspoons of salt in 1 cup of water. I would put two strips of the same size in each for 30 minutes and measure the change in length. Then I would graph salt amount against change in length. Where the line crosses zero is the concentration that matches the potato cells, so the strips neither gain nor lose water.

Exit Check

  1. Define diffusion in your own words and give one everyday example.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Particles moving from high to low concentration; A correct example (smell spreading, dye in water, oxygen into cells)
    Model answer: Diffusion is particles spreading from where they are crowded to where they are less crowded, like the smell of cookies spreading through a house.
  2. What is the name for the diffusion of water across a membrane?
    Answer: osmosis
    Osmosis.
  3. Which kind of transport needs energy from the cell?
    Answer: active transport
    Active transport uses energy and protein pumps to move particles from low to high concentration.
  4. A salad with salty dressing goes limp after an hour. Why?
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Dressing has more dissolved particles than the lettuce cells, so water moves out of the cells by osmosis
    Model answer: The salty dressing has more dissolved particles than the lettuce cells, so water moves out of the cells by osmosis, the vacuoles shrink and the leaves go limp.

Answer key: printable page

  1. Define diffusion and give an example.
    Answer: Particles move from high to low concentration, such as a smell spreading through a room.
  2. What is osmosis?
    Answer: The diffusion of water across a selectively permeable membrane.
  3. A plant cell is placed in fresh water. Which way does water move, and what happens to the cell?
    Answer: Water moves in; the vacuole swells and the cell becomes firm.
  4. Why does a potato strip become floppy in salt water?
    Answer: Water moves out of its cells by osmosis toward the saltier water, so the vacuoles shrink.
  5. How is active transport different from diffusion?
    Answer: Active transport uses energy and pumps to move particles from low to high concentration; diffusion needs no energy.
  6. Why can't people drink seawater to stay alive?
    Answer: It is saltier than body fluids, so it pulls water out of cells, and the kidneys need extra water to remove the salt.
