Unit 2: The Nile Valley and Ancient Africa · Week 9 · World and American History · Teacher Plan

The Kushite Pharaohs, Meroe and Aksum

Lesson length: 60 minutes live, plus the after-class interactive lesson

Objectives

  • Explain how Kush's kings came to rule Egypt as the 25th Dynasty, and identify Piye and Taharqa.
  • Describe Meroe's achievements: iron working, the Meroitic script and its pyramids.
  • Explain the role of the kandakes and the war between Queen Amanirenas and Rome.
  • Describe how Aksum grew rich through Red Sea trade, minted its own coins and adopted Christianity.
  • Use evidence to explain the rise and fall of Kush and Aksum.

Standards

  • SS.6.W.3.18: Describe the rise and fall of the ancient east African kingdoms of Kush and Axum and Christianity's development in Ethiopia.
  • SS.6.W.2.6: Determine the contributions of key figures from ancient Egypt.
  • SS.6.E.3.3: Describe traditional economies (Egypt, Greece, Rome, Kush) and elements of those economies that led to the rise of a merchant class and trading partners.
  • SS.6.W.1.3: Interpret primary and secondary sources.
  • SS.6.G.2.6: Explain the concept of cultural diffusion, and identify the influences of different ancient cultures on one another.

Materials

  • The interactive lesson
  • A map of Africa
  • Air-dry clay or salt dough (flour, salt, water) or cardboard and foil; a jar lid; a toothpick; gold paint or a marker
  • Printable practice page

Vocabulary

  • dynasty: A series of rulers from the same family.
  • stela: An upright carved stone slab with writing or pictures (plural: stelae).
  • kandake: The Meroitic title for a royal woman, a queen mother or ruling queen; the Greeks wrote it as Candace.
  • Meroitic: The writing system and language of Meroe; scholars can read its sounds but understand only some of its words.
  • slag: Waste rock left over when iron is melted out of ore.
  • obelisk: A tall stone pillar; at Aksum, giant carved stelae marked royal tombs.
  • Ge'ez: The ancient language and script of Aksum, still used in the Ethiopian Orthodox Church.
  • decline: A gradual loss of power or wealth.

Key questions to ask

  • Were the Kushite kings invaders or restorers? Who would answer each way?
  • What can a coin tell us that a book cannot?
  • Whose point of view does Strabo give?
  • What made each kingdom rise, and what made it decline?

Common mistakes to watch for

  • Thinking the 25th Dynasty were foreigners who did not respect Egyptian culture.
  • Believing pyramids are only Egyptian; Nubia has more of them.
  • Assuming Meroitic is fully understood.
  • Thinking Christianity came to Africa only through European missionaries; Aksum adopted it in the 300s CE.

Support (if the student is struggling)

  • Provide a three-column chart: Napata (25th Dynasty), Meroe, Aksum.
  • Give a timeline card from 728 BCE to 940 CE.
  • Read Strabo's quotation together and underline the opinion words.

Challenge (if the student is ready for more)

  • Research the black granite statues of Taharqa and other Kushite kings found at Dukki Gel (Kerma) in 2003.
  • Read about Henry Aubin's argument in The Rescue of Jerusalem (2002) that a Kushite army helped save Jerusalem from Assyria in 701 BCE, and explain why historians debate it.

Background for the teacher

Piye's campaign is dated to about 728 BCE (his Victory Stela, Cairo JE 48862, from Jebel Barkal, includes his rebuke of Nimlot for starving his horses). Shabaka (about 716 or 705 to 690s BCE) completed control of Egypt; the Shabaka Stone (British Museum EA 498) preserves the Memphite Theology. Taharqa (690 to 664 BCE) is the Tirhakah of 2 Kings 19:9 and Isaiah 37:9; his pyramid at Nuri is the largest in Nubia. Esarhaddon took Memphis in 671 BCE; Ashurbanipal's army sacked Thebes in 663 BCE under Tantamani. Psamtik II's campaign reached Napata about 593 BCE; the royal center moved to Meroe over the following centuries (royal burials at Meroe from about 270 BCE). F. Ll. Griffith deciphered the Meroitic phonetic values in 1909. Ferlini looted the pyramid of Amanishakheto in 1834 (collections in Munich and Berlin). The war with Rome (about 25 to 21/20 BCE) is described by Strabo (Geography 17.1.54) and Cassius Dio; the Meroe Head of Augustus (British Museum 1911,0901.1) was excavated by John Garstang in 1910. Aksum's coinage began under Endubis (about 270s CE); Mani's Kephalaia names Aksum among four great kingdoms. Ezana's conversion (about 330s to 340s CE, through Frumentius) is shown on his coins; his Ge'ez and Greek inscriptions record a campaign against the Noba and Kasu around 350 CE. The Obelisk of Aksum was taken in 1937, returned in April 2005 and re-erected in 2008. Note that "Black Pharaohs" is a popular modern label; use it with the explanation given in the lesson.

Answer key: interactive lesson

Reconnect

  1. Which Kushite capital stood beside Jebel Barkal, the "Pure Mountain"?
    Answer: Napata
    Napata, near the Fourth Cataract. Today its kings march north.
  2. Which god did both Kushites and Egyptians believe lived inside Jebel Barkal?
    Answer: Amun
    Amun. Kush's kings saw themselves as Amun's chosen rulers, which helped justify their conquest of Egypt.
  3. Last week you saw that whenever one kingdom on the Nile was weak, the other grew stronger. Give one example from Week 8.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Accurate example: Kush attacked when Egypt was divided (about 1575 BCE), Egypt conquered Kerma (about 1500 BCE), or Kush became independent when Egypt weakened (about 1070 BCE)
    Model answer: When Egypt was weak and divided around 1575 BCE, Kush's army attacked deep into Egypt, as the Sobeknakht inscription shows.
  4. Kush borrowed Egyptian gods and hieroglyphs but kept its own customs. In your own words, does borrowing make a culture less important? Why?
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: No; all cultures borrow and adapt ideas; borrowing shows contact and choice, not weakness
    Model answer: No. Every culture borrows from its neighbors and adapts what it takes. Kush chose what to borrow and made it its own.

Learn 1: The 25th Dynasty: Kush Rules Egypt

  1. The Kushite kings saw themselves as restorers, not invaders. Give two pieces of evidence from the lesson that support that view.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Worshiped Amun and believed they were restoring tradition; Repaired temples, revived classic art styles, built at Karnak, preserved the old text on the Shabaka Stone
    Model answer: They worshiped Amun, the same god as the Egyptians, and repaired old Egyptian temples. Shabaka even had an old religious text copied onto stone so it would not be lost.
  2. The 25th Dynasty ruled Egypt from about 747 to 656 BCE. About how many years is that?
    Answer: 91 years
    747 − 656 = 91 years, so about 90 years.
  3. What caused the end of Kushite rule in Egypt? Use the idea of balance of power from last week.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: The Assyrian Empire invaded with iron weapons, took Memphis (671 BCE) and sacked Thebes (663 BCE); A stronger power rose, so Kush pulled back south
    Model answer: A stronger power, the Assyrian Empire, invaded Egypt with iron weapons. They took Memphis in 671 BCE and sacked Thebes in 663 BCE, so the Kushite kings pulled back to Napata. The balance of power had shifted again.
  4. Which Kushite king is mentioned in the Hebrew Bible as Tirhakah?
    Answer: Taharqa
    Taharqa, who ruled about 690 to 664 BCE.

Learn 2: Meroe: City of Iron and Queens

  1. Explain why Meroe was a good location for the capital of Kush. Give at least three reasons.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Iron ore and acacia forests for fuel; Farmland and summer rain; Trade routes to the Red Sea; farther from Egyptian and Assyrian armies
    Model answer: Meroe had iron ore and acacia forests to fuel furnaces, so it could make iron. It had farmland and summer rain to grow food, and it was on trade routes to the Red Sea. It was also farther from the armies that had attacked Napata.
  2. Scholars can read Meroitic aloud but cannot fully understand it. Explain the difference, and why it makes Meroe harder to study.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Knowing the sounds of the letters is not the same as knowing what the words mean; Many Meroitic texts cannot be fully translated, so historians rely more on archaeology and outside sources
    Model answer: Knowing what sound each letter makes lets you pronounce a word, but not know what it means, just as you could read a Swahili word aloud without understanding it. Because many Meroitic texts cannot be translated, historians depend on archaeology and on what Egyptians, Greeks and Romans wrote about Meroe.
  3. Ferlini destroyed pyramids and took jewelry that is now in European museums. Some people argue these objects should be returned to Sudan. Using what you learned about the Benin Bronzes in Week 5, give one argument for returning them and one question that would need to be answered first.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Argument for return: taken through destruction without permission; belongs to Sudanese heritage; A reasonable question: who would receive and care for them, how to keep them safe during conflict in Sudan, what the museums and Sudan agree
    Model answer: They were taken by destroying royal tombs without anyone's permission, so they belong to Sudan's heritage. But first we would need to answer how they could be kept safe, since Sudan has been at war, and which museum or group in Sudan would care for them.

Learn 3: The Kandakes: Queens Who Faced Rome

  1. Who won the war between Kush and Rome? Support your answer with evidence from the peace agreement.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Kush gained most of what it wanted, so it can be seen as a Kushite success (or a draw); Evidence: Rome canceled the tax and pulled its border back at Samos
    Model answer: Kush came out ahead. Even though Rome sacked Napata, at the peace talks on Samos Rome agreed to cancel the tax and pull back its border, which is what Kush had been fighting for.
  2. Strabo called Amanirenas "a masculine sort of woman, and blind in one eye." What does this description tell us about Strabo's point of view? What can we still learn from it?
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Point of view: a Roman enemy who did not expect a woman to lead in war; Still learn: she was a real ruler who led in war, may have lost an eye, and Rome took her seriously
    Model answer: Strabo was a Roman writing about an enemy, and he seemed surprised that a woman led an army, so he called her masculine. We still learn that she was a real queen who led Kush in war, may have lost an eye in battle, and was important enough for Rome to write about.
  3. Why do you think the Kushites buried the head of Augustus under the steps of a temple? What message did it send?
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: To show victory over Rome; everyone walking in stepped on the emperor; Message: Kush was not afraid of Rome; pride and power
    Model answer: Burying the emperor's head under the steps meant everyone walking into the temple stepped on him. It sent the message that Kush had defeated Rome and was not afraid of it.

Learn 4: Aksum: A Trading Empire on the Red Sea

  1. Explain two ways Aksum's location and choices made it rich.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Location: port of Adulis on the Red Sea, linking Africa, Rome, Arabia and India; Choices: minting its own coins (with Greek writing) to trade with foreign merchants; trading ivory, gold, incense
    Model answer: Aksum controlled the port of Adulis on the Red Sea, where African goods like ivory and gold met traders from Rome, Arabia and India. It also minted its own gold coins with Greek writing, which made trading with foreign merchants easier.
  2. Look at the coin drawing. How can coins be used as historical evidence? Give two things historians learned about Aksum from its coins.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Coins show kings' names, titles, symbols and dates; Learned: names of rulers; trade with Greek-speaking merchants; change of religion from crescent and disk to cross (Christianity)
    Model answer: Coins carry names, pictures and symbols chosen by rulers. From Aksum's coins historians learned the names of its kings, that they traded with Greek-speaking merchants, and that Aksum became Christian, because the crescent and disk were replaced by a cross.
  3. The Aksum stela taken to Rome in 1937 was returned in 2005. How is this case like the Benin Bronzes (Week 5)? How is it different?
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Alike: taken from Africa by a European power without consent; returned or demanded after many years; Different: the stela was returned and re-erected; many Benin Bronzes are still in European museums (or other accurate difference)
    Model answer: Both were taken from Africa by European powers without permission and became symbols of the fight to return heritage. The difference is that the stela was fully returned and raised again in Aksum, while many Benin Bronzes are still held in European museums.
  4. Put these events in time order, earliest first.
    Answer: Piye conquers Egypt → The Assyrians sack Thebes → The royal center of Kush moves to Meroe → Amanirenas's forces raid Roman Egypt → Ezana makes Aksum a Christian kingdom → Aksum's stela is returned from Rome
    About 728 BCE, 663 BCE, after 590 BCE, about 25 BCE, about 340 CE and 2005 CE.

Hands-On

  1. Describe your coin: what is on each side, and what message does each symbol send about your kingdom?
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Both sides described; Each symbol explained with its message; Connects to how Aksum used coins
    Model answer: Front: a crowned queen holding a spear, to show strong leadership. Back: a ship and a star, because my kingdom trades by sea and guides ships by the stars. Edge: "Peace through trade." Like Aksum's coins, it tells foreign traders who rules and what the kingdom values.
  2. What did your family member conclude from your coin without being told? What did they get right, and what did they miss? What does that teach you about using objects as evidence?
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Reports the other person's reading; Compares with intended meaning; Lesson: objects show some things clearly but can be misread; historians combine evidence
    Model answer: My mom guessed it was a sea-trading kingdom ruled by a queen, which was right, but she thought the star meant a religion. That shows objects can tell us a lot but can also be misread, so historians compare coins with writing and other evidence.

Think Deeper

  1. Write a paragraph comparing the rise and fall of Kush (including Meroe) and Aksum. For each, give one cause of its rise and one cause of its decline, then explain one way they were alike.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Kush rise: gold, iron, trade, strong armies, Egypt's division; decline: Assyrian invasion ended rule in Egypt; Meroe declined around 350 CE as trade shifted and Aksum attacked; Aksum rise: Red Sea trade, port of Adulis, coins; decline: trade shifted, land worn out (about 940 CE); A sound comparison (trade, resources, outside powers); Organized paragraph with transitions
    Model answer: Kush and Aksum both grew rich by controlling trade, but each fell when conditions changed. Kush rose because it had gold, iron and control of the Nile trade routes, and in 728 BCE it took advantage of a divided Egypt to conquer it. Its rule in Egypt ended when the Assyrians invaded, and later Meroe declined around 350 CE as trade shifted and Aksum's army attacked. Aksum rose because its port of Adulis linked Africa with Rome, Arabia and India, and its gold coins made trade easier. It declined by about 940 CE as Red Sea trade moved away and its farmland wore out. The two kingdoms were alike because their power depended on trade: when trade routes changed, their strength faded.
  2. Taharqa, Amanirenas and Ezana are far less famous in many schools than Tutankhamun or Cleopatra. Choose one of them and write a short argument for why every student should learn about this person.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Accurate facts about the chosen ruler; A clear reason the person matters; Persuasive reasoning about why the story should be taught
    Model answer: Every student should learn about Kandake Amanirenas. When Rome, the most powerful empire in the world, tried to tax Kush, she led her army against it, and at Samos Rome agreed to cancel the tax and move its border. Her story shows that African women led nations and stood up to empires, a fact that is often left out of history books, and it gives a fuller picture of the ancient world.

Exit Check

  1. Which Kushite king conquered Egypt around 728 BCE and carved his victory on a stela at Jebel Barkal?
    Answer: Piye
    Piye, also spelled Piankhi.
  2. Why was Meroe a center of iron making?
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: It had iron ore and acacia forests for fuel
    Model answer: It had iron ore and acacia forests to burn as fuel for the furnaces.
  3. What was a kandake, and what did Kandake Amanirenas achieve?
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: A royal woman, queen mother or ruling queen of Meroe; Fought Rome; peace at Samos canceled the tax and moved the border back
    Model answer: A kandake was a queen or queen mother of Meroe. Amanirenas fought Rome, and the peace at Samos made Rome cancel its tax and move its border back.
  4. Name two achievements of Aksum.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Any two: Red Sea trade through Adulis, its own coins, giant stelae, early Christian state, Ge'ez script
    Model answer: Aksum minted its own gold coins and became one of the first Christian kingdoms.

Answer key: printable page

  1. Who was Piye, and what did he accomplish?
    Answer: A king of Kush who conquered Egypt around 728 BCE, beginning the 25th Dynasty.
  2. Why did Kushite rule in Egypt end?
    Answer: The Assyrians invaded with iron weapons, took Memphis in 671 BCE and sacked Thebes in 663 BCE.
  3. Give two reasons Meroe became a center of iron making and trade.
    Answer: Iron ore and acacia forests for fuel; trade routes to the Red Sea and good farmland.
  4. What did Kandake Amanirenas achieve in her war with Rome?
    Answer: At Samos, Rome agreed to cancel its tax and pull back its border.
  5. How did Aksum grow rich?
    Answer: Red Sea trade through Adulis with Rome, Arabia and India; it minted its own coins.
  6. How do Aksum's coins show that the kingdom became Christian?
    Answer: The crescent and disk on the coins were replaced by a cross under Ezana.
