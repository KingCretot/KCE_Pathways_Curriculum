Unit 2: Explaining the World (Informational) · Week 9 · ELA · Teacher Plan

Writing to Inform: Research Notes and Citations

Lesson length: 60 minutes live, plus the after-class interactive lesson

Objectives

  • Write a focused research question.
  • Take note cards that are organized by subtopic, labeled by source and written in your own words.
  • Use quotation marks for exact words and record where they came from.
  • Combine information from two sources on the same subtopic.
  • Write MLA-style works cited entries and in-text citations.

Standards

  • ELA.6.C.4.1: Conduct research to answer a question, drawing on multiple reliable and valid sources, and refocusing the inquiry when appropriate.
  • ELA.6.C.1.4: Write expository texts to explain and/or analyze information from multiple sources, using a logical organizational structure, relevant elaboration, and varied transitions.
  • ELA.6.R.3.2: Paraphrase content from grade-level texts.
  • ELA.K12.EE.1.1: Cite evidence to explain and justify reasoning.
  • ELA.6.V.1.2: Apply knowledge of Greek and Latin roots and affixes to determine meanings of words and phrases in grade-level content (reconnect).

Materials

  • The interactive lesson
  • Index cards (at least 12), two highlighters or markers in different colors, paper clips
  • A library visit or access to trusted websites for the student's own topic
  • Printable practice page

Vocabulary

  • research question: A focused question that guides what information you look for.
  • source: A book, article, website, interview or other place information comes from.
  • note card: A card with notes on one subtopic from one source.
  • quotation: An author's exact words, shown with quotation marks and credited.
  • citation: Information that tells readers exactly where an idea or quote came from.
  • works cited: The list of sources at the end of a piece of writing, in alphabetical order.
  • in-text citation: A short note in parentheses, such as (Okafor), that points to a works cited entry.
  • synthesize: To combine information from more than one source into one idea.

Lesson flow

6 minReconnect
Run the Week 8 Reconnect questions.
4 minHook
Ask where the 97 percent figure came from and how a reader could check it.
8 minResearch questions
Sort questions into too broad, too narrow and just right. The student drafts a project question.
10 minRead sources
Read Sources A and B. Compare the two kinds of sources and discuss reliability.
14 minNote cards
Model one card, then the student makes two. Hunt for copied strings without quotation marks.
12 minSynthesize and cite
Write the synthesized sentence together. Build both works cited entries.
6 minAssign
Assign the note card sort (library or online), Think Deeper and the Exit Check.

Key questions to ask

  • Can this question be answered in one sentence? Is it too big?
  • Which source is this card from?
  • Are these your words or the author's?
  • Where would a reader go to check this fact?

Common mistakes to watch for

  • Only direct quotations need citations.
  • Notes should be copied sentences.
  • Any website is a reliable source.
  • A works cited list is in the order sources were used.

Support (if the student is struggling)

  • Provide note card templates with labeled spaces.
  • Give a works cited frame with blanks for each element.
  • Limit the project search to two pre-selected sources.

Challenge (if the student is ready for more)

  • Find a third source and add it to your synthesis.
  • Compare MLA and APA citation styles for one source.

Background for the teacher

Sources A and B are practice sources written for this lesson; the author name (Grace Okafor) and the organization ("Fact Sheet") are invented and labeled as such on the page. Their facts are real: Florida's Coral Reef runs about 350 miles from the Dry Tortugas to St. Lucie Inlet (Florida Department of Environmental Protection); the 97 percent wave-energy figure is from Ferrario et al. (2014), Nature Communications; the Manatee Bay buoy recorded about 101°F (38.4°C) on July 24, 2023 (NOAA); stony coral tissue loss disease was first reported off Miami-Dade County in 2014 and affects more than 20 stony coral species; NOAA launched Mission: Iconic Reefs in 2019 for seven Keys sites; the Coral Restoration Foundation in Key Largo uses coral tree nurseries. Citation format follows MLA 9th edition, simplified for Grade 6. When students use real websites, require the URL and teach them to prefer .gov, .edu, museum and established science organization sites.

During the live lesson, the student shares their screen and answers the Reconnect questions and the Learn checks live while you teach. The Hands-On activity, Think Deeper and Exit Check are completed after class.

Answer key: interactive lesson

Reconnect

  1. What is the word for presenting someone else's words or ideas as your own?
    Answer: plagiarism
    Plagiarism. Today you learn how to avoid it with citations.
  2. Under last week's warning rule, if this many words in a row match the original, you should rewrite them or use quotation marks. What is the number?
    Answer: 4 words
    The 4-word check.
  3. Paraphrase this sentence from Source A in today's lesson: "Reefs are called the rainforests of the sea because so many living things depend on them."
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Same meaning; new wording and structure; no 4 or more words in a row copied
    Model answer: Because they support such a huge variety of life, coral reefs are often compared to rainforests.
  4. Which root, taught last week, means "sailor" and appears in astronaut?
    Answer: naut
    naut = sailor: astronaut, nautical, aeronautics.

Learn 1: Start with a Research Question

  1. Label each research question too broad, too narrow or just right, and explain one: (1) How do sea turtles find their way across the ocean? (2) What can you tell me about ocean animals? (3) How many eggs did one turtle lay on Juno Beach in 2024?
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: 1 just right; 2 too broad (and simple); 3 too narrow; Explanation tied to scope (one-sentence answer, or too much information)
    Model answer: (1) Just right, (2) too broad, (3) too narrow. Question 3 is too narrow because one number answers it, so there is nothing to research or explain.
  2. Write a just-right research question about a topic you care about that you could research for your Unit 2 informational article.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Open-ended (not yes/no); Focused enough for a short article; Needs more than one source
    Model answer: How do hurricanes form, and how do scientists predict where they will go?

Learn 2: Read Two Sources

  1. Name one subtopic both sources cover, and one piece of information that appears in only one source.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Shared subtopic: the 2023 heat wave or bleaching (or threats to the reef); A fact found in only one source (disease, nurseries, wave protection, Mission: Iconic Reefs, what you can do)
    Model answer: Both sources cover the 2023 heat wave. Only Source B tells about stony coral tissue loss disease.
  2. In Source A, paragraph 2, what living things inside coral make food through photosynthesis?
    Answer: algae
    Microscopic algae live inside the polyps and share the food they make. You learned about photosynthesis in Science last week.
  3. Source B lists no author. What else could you check to decide whether a fact sheet like this is reliable?
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Who published it (a known science or government organization); date updated; whether facts match other trusted sources; whether it names its sources; Notices that a reader could not verify an unknown organization
    Model answer: I could check who published it and whether it is a trusted science group, how recently it was updated, and whether its facts match other reliable sources, like NOAA. This one is a practice source with an invented organization, so in real research I could not verify it and would not use it.

Learn 3: Take Note Cards

  1. Make a note card from Source A, paragraph 3. Write a subtopic heading, the source label and two or three notes in your own words.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Heading names a subtopic (Why reefs matter); Labeled Source A; Notes in own words: home and food for many animals; protect coasts by reducing wave energy an average of 97 percent (2014 study); No 4 or more words in a row copied without quotation marks
    Model answer: Heading: Why reefs matter. Source A. Notes: Fish, lobsters, turtles and sponges live and eat there. Reefs shield coasts from storms. 2014 study: they cut wave energy by an average of 97 percent before waves hit land.
  2. A student's note card says: Corals push out their algae and turn white. It is labeled Source A but has no quotation marks. What is the problem, and how should the student fix it?
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Copied 8 words in a row ("corals push out their algae and turn white") without quotation marks, which could become plagiarism; Fix: put the exact words in quotation marks with para. 4, or paraphrase
    Model answer: "Corals push out their algae and turn white" is copied word for word without quotation marks, so it could end up as plagiarism. The student should put those words in quotation marks and add para. 4, or rewrite them, such as: corals lose their algae and go pale.
  3. Make a note card from Source B on the subtopic "Ways people are helping." Include at least three notes in your own words.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Heading and Source B label; Three accurate notes: nurseries grow corals on underwater trees and replant them; Mission: Iconic Reefs (NOAA, 2019) restoring seven sites; moving corals to cooler water or land tanks in 2023; individual actions; Own words
    Model answer: Heading: Ways people are helping. Source B. Notes: Groups grow baby corals on pipe frames underwater and replant them. NOAA's 2019 Mission: Iconic Reefs plan aims to fix 7 sites in the Keys. In the 2023 heat wave, scientists rescued nursery corals by moving them deeper or onto land.

Learn 4: Combine Sources and Give Credit

  1. Complete the MLA entry for this book: Author Mary Ellen Tanaka (practice example); title Coral Kingdoms; publisher Pineapple Press; year 2021.
    Answer: Tanaka; 2021
    The author's last name comes first, then the first name. The year comes after the publisher. (Book titles go in italics.)
  2. Write the in-text citation for a fact from Source A. Include the parentheses.
    Answer: (Okafor)
    An MLA in-text citation uses the author's last name: (Okafor). Add a page number if there is one.
  3. Write two sentences that synthesize both sources on the subtopic "Threats to Florida's reef." Include an in-text citation after each fact.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Combines a fact from Source A (bleaching from hot water, 2023 heat wave) and Source B (stony coral tissue loss disease); An in-text citation after each fact: (Okafor) and ("Fact Sheet"); Own words
    Model answer: Florida's reef faces more than one threat. Water that stays too hot causes corals to lose their algae and bleach, as happened across the reef in 2023 (Okafor), and a disease first found near Miami in 2014 now attacks more than 20 kinds of coral ("Fact Sheet").
  4. Which entry comes first in a works cited list, the Okafor article or the fact sheet? Explain the rule.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: The fact sheet; Its entry begins with the title (F) because no person is the author; entries are alphabetical, and F comes before O
    Model answer: The fact sheet comes first. No person is named as its author, so its entry begins with the title, "Fact Sheet," and works cited entries are alphabetical, so F comes before O for Okafor.

Hands-On

  1. List your research question, your subtopic groups in order, and how many cards are in each group. Which group came from both sources?
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Research question; Two to four subtopic groups in a logical order; Card counts; Identifies at least one group combining both sources
    Model answer: Question: How do hurricanes form, and how do scientists predict their paths? Groups: 1. Warm water and wind (3 cards, both sources). 2. How a hurricane grows (2 cards). 3. Hurricane hunters and satellites (2 cards). 4. Staying safe (1 card). Group 1 combines both sources.
  2. Write the works cited list for your two sources in MLA style, in alphabetical order.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Two entries with author (or title/organization), title in quotation marks, publication or website, date, URL; Alphabetical order; Correct punctuation
    Model answer: National Weather Service. "Hurricane Safety Tips and Resources." National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration, www.weather.gov/safety/hurricane. Accessed 10 Nov. 2026. Simon, Seymour. Hurricanes. HarperCollins, 2007.

Think Deeper

  1. Using your note cards from this lesson, write a short informational paragraph (5 to 7 sentences) answering: What is threatening Florida's Coral Reef, and what are people doing to save it? Begin with a topic sentence, use facts from both sources with in-text citations, and end with a concluding sentence. Then write the works cited list.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Topic sentence answers the question; Facts from both sources, accurate and in own words; In-text citations after borrowed facts; Concluding sentence; Correct works cited list in alphabetical order
    Model answer: Florida's Coral Reef, the only coral barrier reef in the continental United States, faces serious threats, but people are fighting to save it. When ocean water stays too hot, corals lose the algae that feed them and turn white, which happened across the reef during the record heat wave of 2023 (Okafor). A disease first found near Miami in 2014 has also spread along most of the reef ("Fact Sheet"). To help, groups grow young corals on underwater frames and plant them back on the reef, and NOAA has a plan to restore seven key sites in the Keys ("Fact Sheet"). Ordinary people can help, too, by using less fertilizer and never touching coral. With science and care, the reef may still have a future. Works Cited "Fact Sheet: Saving Florida's Reef." Sunshine Ocean Alliance, 2026. Okafor, Grace. "Rainforests of the Sea: Florida's Coral Reef." KCE Practice Reader, 2026.
  2. Bessie Coleman's story (Week 8) was written using books and museum websites. Explain two reasons writers cite their sources, even when they put everything in their own words.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Gives credit for others' ideas and research, even when paraphrased (avoids plagiarism); Lets readers check facts and learn more; builds trust
    Model answer: First, ideas belong to the people who discovered or wrote them, so even a paraphrase needs credit, or it is still plagiarism. Second, citations let readers check the facts and find more information, which makes the writing more trustworthy.

Exit Check

  1. Name the four parts of a research note card.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Subtopic heading, source label, notes in own words, quotations in quotation marks with location
    Model answer: A subtopic heading, the source label, notes in my own words, and any exact words in quotation marks with the paragraph or page.
  2. What is the name of the list of sources at the end of an MLA paper?
    Answer: works cited
    In MLA style, the list is called Works Cited. Some styles call it a bibliography.
  3. What does it mean to synthesize sources?
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Combine information from more than one source on the same idea
    Model answer: To combine information from two or more sources into one idea or paragraph.
  4. A source has no author. What does its works cited entry begin with: the title, the date or the URL?
    Answer: the title
    With no person named as author, start with the title; the organization goes after it as the publisher.
