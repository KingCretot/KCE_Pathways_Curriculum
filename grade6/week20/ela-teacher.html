Unit 4: Making the Case (Argument) · Week 20 · ELA · Teacher Plan

Unit 4 Checkpoint and Argument Project

Lesson length: 60 minutes live, plus the after-class interactive lesson

Objectives

  • Analyze a new argument: its claim, reasons, evidence, reasoning and counterclaim.
  • Identify fallacies and misleading evidence, and explain why they are weak.
  • Write and revise a counterclaim paragraph with a fair concession and a rebuttal.
  • Complete and present a researched argumentative essay.
  • Demonstrate mastery of Unit 4 on the checkpoint (85 or higher).

Standards

  • ELA.6.R.2.4: Track the development of an argument, identifying the types of reasoning used.
  • ELA.6.R.3.4: Identify rhetorical appeals in a text.
  • ELA.6.C.1.3: Write and support a claim using logical reasoning, relevant evidence from sources, elaboration, and a logical organizational structure with varied transitions.
  • ELA.6.C.2.1: Present information orally, in a logical sequence, using nonverbal cues, appropriate volume, clear pronunciation, and appropriate pacing.
  • ELA.6.C.4.1: Conduct research to answer a question, drawing on multiple reliable and valid sources, and refocusing the inquiry when appropriate.
  • ELA.6.V.1.2: Apply knowledge of Greek and Latin roots and affixes to determine meanings of words and phrases in grade-level content.

Materials

  • The interactive lesson
  • The student's project essay and 3 to 5 index cards
  • A timer, a phone or tablet to record, a mirror
  • Printable practice page

Vocabulary

  • claim: The main point an argument wants the reader to accept.
  • evidence: Facts, statistics, examples or expert words that prove a reason.
  • fallacy: A mistake in reasoning that makes an argument seem stronger than it is.
  • counterclaim: An opposing view that an argument answers.
  • rebuttal: A response that shows why a counterclaim is wrong or weaker.
  • thesis: The sentence that states an essay's claim.

Key questions to ask

  • Is the evidence relevant, sufficient and credible?
  • Is this sound reasoning or a fallacy?
  • Has the other side been stated fairly?
  • Does every source say what the writer claims?

Common mistakes to watch for

  • A speech should be read word for word.
  • A counterclaim weakens an argument.
  • Popular opinions are proven.
  • Revising means fixing spelling.

Support (if the student is struggling)

  • Allow the essay to be dictated and then edited.
  • Provide a speech note-card template.
  • Read the checkpoint letter aloud if reading is a barrier.

Challenge (if the student is ready for more)

  • Write a rebuttal to your own essay as if you were your strongest opponent.
  • Deliver the speech to a new audience (grandparents by video call, a co-op group).

Background for the teacher

The checkpoint is scored out of 44 points: Part A 15, Part B 13, Part C 16. Open items are scored with the rubric after submission. A score of 85 percent is about 38 of 44 points. The splash-pad letter is a KCE practice text; the National Weather Service does list heat among the leading weather-related causes of death in the United States.

How the lesson runs

During the live lesson, the student shares their screen and answers the Reconnect questions and the Learn checks live while you teach. The Hands-On activity, Think Deeper and Exit Check are completed after class.

Scoring

The interactive lesson scores itself: full points on the first try, half points on the second try, then the answer and explanation appear. Mastery is 85. Written responses arrive in your results email with the score; score them with the rubric below before the student moves on.

Checkpoint scoring

Checkpoint questions are scored separately from the lesson. The student gets one try per question and the answers are not shown, so the checkpoint measures independent mastery and a retake stays fair. Your results email shows the auto-scored checkpoint points, a list of each item marked correct or MISSED, and the points available for the written items. Score the written items with their rubrics, then: checkpoint score = (auto points + rubric points) / total possible × 100. Mastery is 85. Below 85: teach a targeted review lesson on the parts missed (A to D), then the student retakes the checkpoint. Record both attempts in the Weekly Score Log.

Answer key: interactive lesson

Reconnect

  1. Week 16: "According to the CDC, ..." introduces what type of evidence: an anecdote or expert testimony?
    Answer: expert testimony (or a statistic from an expert organization)
    The CDC is a credible expert organization.
  2. Week 17: "If we let you have one cookie, you'll eat the whole jar and never eat vegetables again." Name the fallacy.
    Answer: slippery slope
    An unsupported chain of disasters.
  3. Week 18: Which word means to admit that part of the other side's point is true?
    Answer: concede
    con + cede: to yield.
  4. Week 19: Restating a source's information in your own words is called ___.
    Answer: paraphrasing
    Then cite the source.

Learn 1: Review: Building and Testing Arguments

  1. "Our soccer team won after I got a new haircut, so my haircut brings us luck." Which weak reasoning is this?
    Answer: false cause
    One thing coming first doesn't prove it caused the other.
  2. Write one sentence for a claim about recycling that uses credibility, and one that uses emotion. Label each.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: A credibility sentence (expert or experience); An emotion sentence (vivid image, feeling); Correctly labeled
    Model answer: Credibility: Our county's recycling coordinator, who has worked in waste management for 20 years, says recycling saves the county money. Emotion: Picture a sea turtle tangled in a plastic bag that could have been recycled.

Learn 2: Review: Counterclaims and Essays

  1. Claim: "Families should limit phone use during dinner." Write a counterclaim paragraph of three to four sentences.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: A fair counterclaim; A concession; A rebuttal with a reason; Transitions
    Model answer: Some people argue that phones at dinner let families share photos and look up answers to questions that come up. It is true that phones can start conversations. However, when everyone is checking messages, people stop listening to each other, and dinner may be the only time all day a family talks face to face. A few phone-free minutes protect that time.

Learn 3: Finishing the Argument Project

  1. Write your project question, your thesis, your three sources (title and author or organization) and the counterclaim you will answer.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: A question from the list or approved by a parent; An arguable thesis; Three credible sources named; A fair counterclaim
    Model answer: Question: Should our county add bike lanes on main roads? Thesis: Our county should add protected bike lanes on main roads, because they make cycling safer and give families a way to travel without a car. Sources: the Federal Highway Administration's bike lane safety page; the League of American Bicyclists' Bicycle Friendly State ranking for Florida (bikeleague.org); a county news article about traffic. Counterclaim: bike lanes take space from cars and cost money.

Hands-On

  1. Record your practice times, the feedback you got, and what you changed between your first and final recordings.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Times recorded; Specific feedback; Specific changes made
    Model answer: First run: 3 minutes 40 seconds, too long. I cut one example. Final: 2 minutes 50 seconds. My dad said my evidence about the injury statistics was strong, but I talked too fast at the start. In my final recording I paused after my hook and looked up more often.

Think Deeper

  1. Compare your Week 16 C-R-E-E paragraph with your final project essay. Name three specific ways your argument writing grew during Unit 4, with an example of each.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Three specific areas of growth (evidence quality, explanation, counterclaims, honest citation, organization, transitions, avoiding fallacies); An example from the student's own writing for each; Thoughtful reflection
    Model answer: First, my evidence is stronger: in Week 16 I used one anecdote, but my project essay uses three credible sources, including a federal safety report. Second, I explain my evidence now: I used to stop after the fact, but now I add a sentence about why it matters. Third, I answer the other side: my essay has a full counterclaim paragraph that concedes bike lanes cost money but shows how grants can pay for them.

Checkpoint: Part A: Analyze a new argument

  1. Our Town Needs a Splash Pad A practice letter to the editor written by KingCretot Experience for this checkpoint. Paragraphs are numbered. [1] Every summer, families in our town drive 40 minutes to the nearest splash pad because we have nowhere close to cool off. Our town should build a splash pad in Central Park this year. [2] First, a splash pad keeps children safe in the heat. The National Weather Service warns that heat is one of the leading weather-related causes of death in the United States, and children are especially at risk. A shaded splash pad gives kids a safe place to play outdoors on hot days. [3] Second, a splash pad is safer than a pool for young children because there is no standing water deep enough to swim in. My neighbor's twins love the splash pad in the next town and have never had a single problem there. [4] Everyone I've talked to wants one. The people who oppose it are just grumpy residents who don't like children. [5] Some council members say a splash pad costs too much. It is true that building one can cost tens of thousands of dollars. However, our state offers recreation grants for small towns, and a splash pad needs no lifeguards, so it costs far less to run than a pool. The council should apply for a grant this spring. What is the writer's claim? Quote or paraphrase it.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric (3 points toward the checkpoint).
    Rubric: Our town should build a splash pad in Central Park this year
    Model answer: The town should build a splash pad in Central Park this year.
  2. Compare the evidence in paragraphs 2 and 3. Which is stronger? Use the words relevant, sufficient or credible.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric (4 points toward the checkpoint).
    Rubric: Paragraph 2 is stronger: credible expert source (National Weather Service), relevant to safety; Paragraph 3 is one anecdote: not sufficient, though relevant
    Model answer: Paragraph 2 is stronger because it cites the National Weather Service, a credible source, and the fact is relevant to keeping kids safe in the heat. Paragraph 3 is only one neighbor's story, which is not sufficient to prove splash pads are always safe.
  3. Paragraph 4 contains two fallacies. Name both and explain why each is weak.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric (4 points toward the checkpoint).
    Rubric: Bandwagon: "Everyone I've talked to wants one" (popularity isn't proof); Attacking the person or straw man: opponents are "grumpy residents who don't like children" (attacks people instead of answering their real concerns)
    Model answer: "Everyone I've talked to wants one" is bandwagon: popularity doesn't prove it's a good idea. Calling opponents "grumpy residents who don't like children" attacks the people (and creates a straw man) instead of answering their real concerns, like cost.
  4. In paragraph 5, identify the counterclaim, the concession and the rebuttal.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric (4 points toward the checkpoint).
    Rubric: Counterclaim: a splash pad costs too much; Concession: it can cost tens of thousands of dollars; Rebuttal: state grants and lower running costs than a pool (no lifeguards)
    Model answer: Counterclaim: some council members say it costs too much. Concession: it is true it can cost tens of thousands of dollars. Rebuttal: state grants can help pay, and it needs no lifeguards, so it costs less to run than a pool.

Checkpoint: Part B: Reasoning and texts from Unit 4

  1. "Either we buy a new car or we'll be stranded forever." Name the fallacy.
    Answer: either-or
    There are other choices.
  2. Which document did Susan B. Anthony use as her main evidence in 1873?
    Answer: the U.S. Constitution (its preamble)
    "We, the people."
  3. Douglass said "scorching irony, not convincing argument, is needed." Which appeal did he mean to rely on most: logic, emotion or credibility?
    Answer: emotion (pathos)
    Fire and thunder, not gentle light.
  4. Paine compared America to a child who outgrows milk. Explain how this analogy rebuts the claim that America needed Britain because it had done well under Britain.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric (4 points toward the checkpoint).
    Rubric: What helped at one stage of growth isn't needed forever; So past success under Britain doesn't prove America still needs Britain
    Model answer: A baby needs milk, but it grows and needs other food. Paine means that even if Britain helped America when it was young, that doesn't mean America needs Britain forever.

Checkpoint: Part C: Write and revise

  1. Revise this weak paragraph into a strong C-R-E-E paragraph (you may invent a realistic example, but not fake statistics): "Kids should learn to cook. It's good. Everyone should do it."
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric (5 points toward the checkpoint).
    Rubric: A clear claim; A reason with because; Evidence (a realistic example or general knowledge, honestly presented); An explanation connecting evidence to the reason; No fallacies (removes the bandwagon)
    Model answer: Kids ages 11 to 13 should learn to cook simple meals, because cooking builds independence and healthy habits. For example, a student who can make scrambled eggs and a salad can feed themselves a healthy lunch instead of reaching for chips. Learning to cook now means that when they live on their own someday, they'll know how to eat well and save money.
  2. Write a counterclaim paragraph for this claim: "Our town should build a splash pad." Use a different counterclaim from the one in paragraph 5.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric (5 points toward the checkpoint).
    Rubric: A fair counterclaim different from cost (water use, maintenance, crowding, location, safety); A concession; A rebuttal with a reason; Transitions
    Model answer: Some residents worry that a splash pad wastes water during droughts. It is true that Florida sometimes faces water restrictions. However, many splash pads recirculate and filter their water, and the town could choose a recirculating design and close it during droughts. A well-designed splash pad can keep kids cool without wasting water.
  3. The root cred means ___.
    Answer: believe
    credible, incredible.
  4. The root vers means ___.
    Answer: turn
    reverse, adversary.

Answer key: printable page

  1. Define claim, reason, evidence and explanation.
    Answer: Claim: the main arguable point. Reason: why it's true. Evidence: facts, statistics, examples, expert words. Explanation: how the evidence proves the reason.
  2. Name and give an example of four fallacies.
    Answer: Any four of overgeneralization, bandwagon, attacking the person, either-or, slippery slope, straw man, with examples.
  3. Compare how Anthony, Douglass and Paine each used evidence.
    Answer: Anthony: the Constitution's preamble. Douglass: slaveholders' own laws and the Declaration. Paine: an analogy and reasoning about Britain's motives.
  4. Write a thesis for: Should every family have an emergency plan for hurricanes?
    Answer: Model: Every Florida family should make a written hurricane plan before June, because it saves time, money and possibly lives when a storm comes.
  5. Write a counterclaim paragraph for your thesis above.
    Answer: Model: Some families argue they can just watch the news and decide when a storm comes. It is true that forecasts give several days' warning. However, stores sell out of water and supplies quickly, so a plan made in advance keeps families from scrambling.
  6. What do the roots cred, ver, contra, log, path, fall, ced and vers mean?
    Answer: believe, true, against, word/reason, feeling, deceive, go/yield, turn.
