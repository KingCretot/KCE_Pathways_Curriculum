Unit 4: The Human Body · Week 20 · Life Science · Teacher Plan

Unit 4 Checkpoint and Body Systems Project

Lesson length: 60 minutes live, plus the after-class interactive lesson

Objectives

  • Describe the levels of organization from atoms to organisms.
  • Explain the functions of the skeletal, muscular, circulatory, respiratory, digestive and nervous systems.
  • Explain how body systems interact to maintain homeostasis in a real situation.
  • Analyze data from a body investigation and use models to explain body functions.
  • Demonstrate mastery of Unit 4 on the checkpoint (85 or higher).

Standards

  • SC.6.L.14.1: Describe and identify patterns in the hierarchical organization of organisms from atoms to molecules to cells to tissues to organs to organ systems to organisms.
  • SC.6.L.14.5: Identify and investigate the general functions of the major systems of the human body (digestive, respiratory, circulatory, reproductive, excretory, immune, nervous, and musculoskeletal) and describe ways these systems interact with each other to maintain homeostasis.
  • SC.6.N.3.4: Identify the role of models in the context of the sixth grade science benchmarks.
  • SC.6.N.1.1: Define a problem from the sixth grade curriculum, use appropriate reference materials to support scientific understanding, plan and carry out scientific investigation of various types, such as systematic observations or experiments, identify variables, collect and organize data, interpret data in charts, tables, and graphics, analyze information, make predictions, and defend conclusions.

Materials

  • The interactive lesson
  • Butcher paper (about 5 feet), markers, colored pencils, construction paper, scissors, glue or tape, red and blue yarn
  • Notes from Weeks 16 to 19
  • Printable practice page

Vocabulary

  • homeostasis: Keeping stable internal conditions, such as temperature and oxygen level.
  • organ system: A group of organs that work together to do a major job.
  • structure and function: The idea that a part's shape fits its job.
  • interaction: When two or more systems affect or depend on each other.
  • model: A representation that helps explain how something works, with limits.

Key questions to ask

  • What level of organization is this?
  • How does this structure fit its job?
  • Which systems are working together?
  • How does the body keep conditions stable?

Common mistakes to watch for

  • Each system works alone.
  • Bones are not alive.
  • Blood in veins is blue.
  • Most digestion happens in the stomach.

Answer key: interactive lesson

Reconnect

  1. Week 16: Which tissue type covers and lines body surfaces?
    Answer: epithelial
    Skin, stomach lining.
  2. Week 17: What connects bone to bone at a joint?
    Answer: a ligament
    Tendons connect muscle to bone.
  3. Week 18: Which side of the heart pumps blood to the lungs?
    Answer: the right side
    The left side pumps to the body.
  4. Week 19: Where are most nutrients absorbed?
    Answer: the small intestine
    Through the villi.

Learn 1: Review: Levels and Structure-Function

  1. Name the level of organization.
    Answer: cell; organ; organ
    A bone is an organ made of several tissues.
  2. Choose two structures from different weeks of this unit that both have a large surface area. Explain why a large surface helps each one do its job.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Two correct structures (alveoli, villi, capillary networks, dendrites); Explains that more surface means more exchange or absorption for each
    Model answer: Alveoli in the lungs and villi in the small intestine both have huge surface areas. More surface on the alveoli lets more oxygen move into the blood at once, and more surface on the villi lets more nutrients be absorbed.

Learn 2: Review: Systems and Their Jobs

  1. Choose any two systems and describe one way they depend on each other.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Two correct systems; An accurate dependence (e.g., muscles need oxygen from the circulatory system; the nervous system signals muscles; bones make blood cells for the circulatory system)
    Model answer: The skeletal and circulatory systems depend on each other: red marrow in bones makes the red blood cells the circulatory system uses to carry oxygen, and blood brings bone cells the nutrients they need.

Learn 3: Putting It Together: Homeostasis

  1. Why can exercising hard on a hot, humid Florida day without drinking water be dangerous? Use at least two body systems in your answer.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Sweat removes heat but uses body water; humidity slows evaporation; Losing water makes it harder for the circulatory system to carry heat to the skin and supply muscles; Body temperature can rise too high (homeostasis fails)
    Model answer: Sweating cools the body, but it uses up water, and in humid air sweat evaporates slowly so it cools less. Without enough water, there's less fluid for the circulatory system to carry heat to the skin and oxygen to muscles, so body temperature can climb too high and homeostasis breaks down.

Hands-On

  1. Describe your body map: which organs you placed, the three interactions you explained, and the activity you narrated. What was the most surprising thing you noticed about where organs sit in the body?
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Organs from at least five systems; Three accurate interactions; An activity narrated with several systems; A thoughtful observation
    Model answer: I placed the brain, spinal cord, lungs, heart, stomach, small and large intestines, liver, femur, ribs, skull and biceps. Interactions: the diaphragm and lungs bring in oxygen that the heart pumps to muscles; red marrow in my femur makes blood cells; nerves signal my biceps to contract. I narrated swimming: my brain coordinates strokes, my muscles pull, and I breathe in rhythm. The surprise: my liver is much bigger than I thought and sits high on the right, under my ribs.

Think Deeper

  1. An astronaut spends six months on the International Space Station. Predict how at least three body systems are affected by living in near-weightlessness, and explain how exercise and other habits help. Use what you learned across Unit 4.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Skeletal: bone loss without weight-bearing (about 1 to 1.5 percent per month in weight-bearing bones); Muscular: muscles weaken without use; Circulatory or other: heart works less against gravity, fluids shift; Explains exercise (treadmill with straps, resistance machine) and nutrition (calcium, vitamin D) as countermeasures
    Model answer: Without gravity, astronauts' bones carry almost no weight, so they lose about 1 to 1.5 percent of the mineral in their leg and hip bones each month. Their muscles weaken too, because they don't have to hold the body up. Their heart doesn't have to pump blood upward against gravity, so it can lose some strength. Astronauts exercise about two hours a day on a treadmill with straps and a resistance machine to load their bones and muscles, and eat foods with calcium and vitamin D to help rebuild bone.

Checkpoint: Part A: Levels of organization

  1. Put these levels in order from smallest to largest.
    Answer: molecule → cell → tissue → organ → organ system → organism
    Each level builds on the one before.
  2. Which tissue type carries electrical signals?
    Answer: nervous tissue
    Brain, spinal cord, nerves.
  3. Explain how a nerve cell and a muscle cell can have the same DNA but different structures and jobs.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric (4 points toward the checkpoint).
    Rubric: Cells use (switch on) different parts of the same DNA; so they make different proteins and become specialized (differentiation)
    Model answer: Each cell uses different parts of the same DNA instructions, so they make different proteins and develop different shapes and jobs. This is called differentiation.

Checkpoint: Part B: Support and movement

  1. What part of a bone makes blood cells?
    Answer: red marrow
    Inside spongy bone.
  2. Which type of joint is your knee?
    Answer: a hinge joint
    Back and forth, like a door.
  3. Explain how your biceps and triceps let you lift a cup to your mouth and put it back down.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric (4 points toward the checkpoint).
    Rubric: Biceps contracts to bend the elbow (lift) while triceps relaxes; Triceps contracts to straighten (lower) while biceps relaxes; muscles only pull
    Model answer: To lift the cup, the biceps contracts and bends my elbow while the triceps relaxes. To put it down, the triceps contracts and straightens my arm while the biceps relaxes, because muscles can only pull.

Checkpoint: Part C: Transport and exchange

  1. Which blood vessels carry blood back to the heart and have valves?
    Answer: veins
    Arteries carry blood away.
  2. You count 23 heartbeats in 15 seconds. What is your heart rate in beats per minute?
    Answer: 92 beats per minute
    23 × 4 = 92.
  3. Trace the path of an oxygen molecule from the air you breathe in to a muscle cell in your leg. Name at least five structures.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric (5 points toward the checkpoint).
    Rubric: Air path: nose/mouth, trachea, bronchi, bronchioles, alveoli; Into capillaries, to the left side of the heart (via lungs), pumped through arteries; Into leg capillaries and the muscle cell; At least five structures in correct order
    Model answer: The oxygen enters my nose, travels down the trachea, through the bronchi and bronchioles, into an alveolus. It passes into a capillary, travels to the left side of my heart, gets pumped out through the aorta and arteries to my leg, and leaves a capillary to enter the muscle cell.

Checkpoint: Part D: Fuel and control

  1. Chewing is an example of mechanical or chemical digestion?
    Answer: mechanical
    It changes size, not molecules.
  2. A fast, automatic response that goes through the spinal cord without waiting for the brain is called a ___.
    Answer: reflex
    Like pulling away from a hot pan.
  3. After a big lunch, a student goes for a run. Describe how the digestive, circulatory and nervous systems work together during the next hour.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric (5 points toward the checkpoint).
    Rubric: Digestive: breaking down food, absorbing nutrients in the small intestine; Circulatory: carrying nutrients and oxygen to muscles; Nervous: controlling heart rate, breathing and muscles; coordinating digestion; Shows interaction among all three
    Model answer: The digestive system breaks the lunch into nutrients and absorbs them through the villi. The circulatory system carries those nutrients and oxygen to the leg muscles during the run. The nervous system speeds up the heart and breathing and tells the muscles when to contract, while still controlling digestion in the background.

Answer key: printable page

  1. List the levels of organization from atom to organism, with an example of each.
    Answer: Atom (oxygen), molecule (water), cell (neuron), tissue (muscle), organ (heart), organ system (circulatory), organism (a person).
  2. Name one function each of the skeletal, muscular, circulatory, respiratory, digestive and nervous systems.
    Answer: Support/protection; movement; transport; gas exchange; breaking down and absorbing food; sensing and control.
  3. Give two examples of structure fitting function from this unit.
    Answer: Thin capillary walls for exchange; alveoli/villi for surface area; thick left ventricle for pressure; hinge knee for stability.
  4. Explain the path of blood through the double loop.
    Answer: Right heart → lungs → left heart → body → right heart.
  5. Compare mechanical and chemical digestion with examples.
    Answer: Mechanical: chewing, churning. Chemical: enzymes and acid breaking food into molecules.
  6. Describe how three systems respond when you exercise on a hot day.
    Answer: Nervous signals; circulatory pumps faster and sends blood to skin; respiratory breathes faster; integumentary sweats.
