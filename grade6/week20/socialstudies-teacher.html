Unit 4: The Mediterranean World · Week 20 · World and American History · Teacher Plan

Unit 4 Checkpoint and Mediterranean Project

Lesson length: 60 minutes live, plus the after-class interactive lesson

Objectives

  • Compare how Persia, Athens, Sparta, Rome and Carthage were governed.
  • Explain the causes and effects of the Persian Wars and the Punic Wars.
  • Trace cultural diffusion around the Mediterranean, including ideas from Africa and Asia.
  • Analyze primary sources by asking who wrote them, when and why.
  • Demonstrate mastery of Unit 4 on the checkpoint (85 or higher).

Standards

  • SS.6.W.3.1: Analyze the cultural impact the ancient Phoenicians had on the Mediterranean world with regard to colonization (Carthage), exploration, maritime commerce (purple dye, tin), and written communication (alphabet).
  • SS.6.W.3.2: Explain the democratic concepts (polis, civic participation and voting rights, legislative bodies, written constitutions, rule of law) developed in ancient Greece.
  • SS.6.W.3.4: Explain the causes and effects of the Persian and Peloponnesian Wars.
  • SS.6.W.3.9: Explain the impact of the Punic Wars on the development of the Roman Empire.
  • SS.6.W.3.11: Explain the transition from Roman Republic to empire and Imperial Rome, and compare Roman life and culture under each one.
  • SS.6.CG.1.2: Analyze the influence of ancient Rome on the United States' constitutional republic.
  • SS.6.G.2.6: Explain the concept of cultural diffusion, and identify the influences of different ancient cultures on one another.
  • SS.6.W.1.3: Interpret primary and secondary sources.

Materials

  • The interactive lesson
  • Index cards, markers, small household items as trade goods
  • A world map, atlas or globe
  • Notes from Weeks 16 to 19
  • Printable practice page

Vocabulary

  • empire: Many lands and peoples ruled by one government.
  • republic: A government in which citizens elect leaders.
  • democracy: Government by the people.
  • cultural diffusion: The spread of ideas, goods and customs between cultures.
  • primary source: A source created at the time or by people who were there.
  • bias: A one-sided point of view.

Key questions to ask

  • Who held power, and who was left out?
  • What caused this war, and what changed because of it?
  • Where did this idea come from?
  • Who wrote this source, and why?

Common mistakes to watch for

  • Greece and Rome invented civilization on their own.
  • Carthage was not an African city.
  • All Athenians could vote.
  • Julius Caesar was the first emperor.

Support (if the student is struggling)

  • Use a four-column organizer (Persia, Greece, Rome, Carthage).
  • Keep the scaled timeline visible during review (not during the checkpoint).
  • Read checkpoint questions aloud if reading is a barrier.

Challenge (if the student is ready for more)

  • Add Egypt (Kemet) and Kush as a fifth and sixth column in the organizer, using Unit 2.
  • Research the Library of Alexandria and what may have caused its decline.

How the lesson runs

During the live lesson, the student shares their screen and answers the Reconnect questions and the Learn checks live while you teach. The Hands-On activity, Think Deeper and Exit Check are completed after class.

Scoring

The interactive lesson scores itself: full points on the first try, half points on the second try, then the answer and explanation appear. Mastery is 85. Written responses arrive in your results email with the score; score them with the rubric below before the student moves on.

Checkpoint scoring

Checkpoint questions are scored separately from the lesson. The student gets one try per question and the answers are not shown, so the checkpoint measures independent mastery and a retake stays fair. Your results email shows the auto-scored checkpoint points, a list of each item marked correct or MISSED, and the points available for the written items. Score the written items with their rubrics, then: checkpoint score = (auto points + rubric points) / total possible × 100. Mastery is 85. Below 85: teach a targeted review lesson on the parts missed (A to D), then the student retakes the checkpoint. Record both attempts in the Weekly Score Log.

Answer key: interactive lesson

Reconnect

  1. Week 16: What was the title of a Persian provincial governor?
    Answer: satrap
    Under Darius.
  2. Week 17: Which Athenian leader's Funeral Oration praised democracy?
    Answer: Pericles
    Recorded by Thucydides.
  3. Week 18: What were Rome's first written laws, displayed in the Forum, called?
    Answer: the Twelve Tables
    About 450 BCE.
  4. Week 19: Which Numidian king helped Rome win at Zama?
    Answer: Masinissa
    202 BCE.

Learn 1: Review: Four Ways to Govern

  1. Name the government.
    Answer: direct democracy (Athens); republic (Rome); empire (Persia)
    Unit 4 governments.
  2. Compare who was left out of power in Sparta and Carthage, then explain which exclusion you think most weakened its society.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Sparta: helots (enslaved), women, foreigners excluded; Carthage: power held mainly by wealthy families; women and enslaved people excluded; A reasoned judgment
    Model answer: In Sparta, the enslaved helots, who outnumbered citizens, had no rights, and women could not vote. In Carthage, power was held mostly by wealthy families through elected officials and a council, and women and enslaved people had no voice. I think Sparta's exclusion weakened it most, because fear of a helot revolt forced every citizen to live as a soldier.

Learn 2: Review: Wars and Their Effects

  1. Compare one effect of the Persian Wars with one effect of the Punic Wars.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: A correct effect of each war; A comparison (both made a winner the leading sea power; both changed the balance of power)
    Model answer: The Persian Wars kept Greece independent and made Athens a powerful sea power. The Punic Wars made Rome the strongest power in the western Mediterranean. In both, the winner became the leading naval power in its region.

Learn 3: Review: Ideas That Traveled

  1. Trace one idea or invention as it traveled between at least three Unit 4 civilizations.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: An accurate idea (alphabet, calendar, republic, roads); At least three civilizations in correct order
    Model answer: The alphabet began with Semitic-speaking workers in Egypt adapting hieroglyphs. The Phoenicians, including the people of Carthage, carried it around the Mediterranean, the Greeks added vowels, and the Romans adapted it into the Latin alphabet we use today.

Hands-On

  1. List three trades you made (goods and ideas), and explain which idea you think spread farthest in real history and why.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Three trades with goods and ideas, historically reasonable; A real-history judgment with a reason
    Model answer: 1. My Carthage purple dye and the alphabet for Athens' olive oil and jury trials. 2. Persia's road-and-relay-messenger idea for Rome's bronze tools. 3. Persia's dates and woven textiles for Carthage's grain. In real history, the alphabet spread farthest, because it was easy to learn and traders carried it to every port, and we still use a version of it.

Think Deeper

  1. If you could live in Persia under Darius, Athens under Pericles, the Roman Republic or Carthage, which would you choose? Defend your choice with evidence about government, rights and daily life, and acknowledge one drawback.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: A clear choice; Evidence about government and rights; Evidence about daily life or economy; An honest drawback; Considers who you would be (citizen, woman, enslaved person, foreigner)
    Model answer: I would choose the Roman Republic. Citizens elected consuls each year, tribunes could veto unfair laws, and the Twelve Tables let everyone know the laws. Roman roads and trade made goods available across the Mediterranean. The drawback is that if I were a woman or enslaved person, I would have had no vote, and over time generals like Caesar took power away from citizens. Still, the Republic's idea of elected leaders checking each other inspired the United States.

Checkpoint: Part A: Persia and Greece

  1. Name the founder of the Persian Empire and explain one policy that helped him hold it together.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric (3 points toward the checkpoint).
    Rubric: Cyrus the Great; A policy: letting conquered peoples keep religions, customs or local leaders (tolerance) if they paid tribute
    Model answer: Cyrus the Great founded it. He let conquered peoples keep their religions and customs as long as they paid tribute and stayed loyal, which reduced rebellions.
  2. Which road about 1,600 miles long linked Sardis to Susa?
    Answer: the Royal Road
    Relay messengers used it.
  3. Compare how Sparta and Athens trained boys, and say what each choice shows about what the city valued.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric (3 points toward the checkpoint).
    Rubric: Sparta: army training from age 7, valuing military strength (to control helots); Athens: reading, music, sport and public speaking, valuing citizenship and debate
    Model answer: Spartan boys began army training at age 7, which shows Sparta valued military strength above all. Athenian boys studied reading, music, sport and speaking, which shows Athens valued thinking and taking part as citizens.
  4. Explain why Greece's geography led to many independent city-states instead of one united country.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric (4 points toward the checkpoint).
    Rubric: Mountains separated valleys/communities; Each grew its own government (polis)
    Model answer: Mountains divided Greece into separate valleys, so communities grew up apart and each became its own city-state with its own government.

Checkpoint: Part B: Rome and Carthage

  1. Explain why plebeians demanded tribunes and what power tribunes gained.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric (3 points toward the checkpoint).
    Rubric: Patricians held most power; plebeians walked out in 494 BCE; Tribunes, elected by plebeians, could veto laws that harmed plebeians
    Model answer: Wealthy patricians held almost all the power, so plebeians walked out of Rome in 494 BCE. To bring them back, patricians agreed to tribunes, elected by plebeians, who could veto laws that harmed them.
  2. Hannibal won at Cannae but lost the Second Punic War. Give one reason why.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric (3 points toward the checkpoint).
    Rubric: A valid reason (far from home with few supplies; Rome's Italian allies stayed loyal; Scipio attacked Africa; Masinissa's cavalry at Zama)
    Model answer: Most of Rome's allies in Italy stayed loyal, so Rome kept raising new armies, while Hannibal got few soldiers or supplies from home. Then Scipio attacked Africa and won at Zama.
  3. Name the Roman emperor born in North Africa whose reign began in 193 CE.
    Answer: Septimius Severus
    Born in Leptis Magna, in modern Libya.
  4. Give two causes that turned the Roman Republic into an empire.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric (4 points toward the checkpoint).
    Rubric: Two valid causes (armies loyal to generals; civil wars; wealth gap after conquests such as the Punic Wars; Caesar's seizure of power; Augustus)
    Model answer: Conquests like the Punic Wars made the rich richer and hurt small farmers, which caused unrest, and generals like Julius Caesar had armies loyal to them instead of to Rome, which let them seize power.

Checkpoint: Part C: Sources and connections

  1. Herodotus (a Greek) wrote about Persia, and Livy (a Roman) wrote about Hannibal. What do these two sources have in common that a historian should keep in mind? Give one way to check them.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric (5 points toward the checkpoint).
    Rubric: Both were written by enemies or outsiders of the people they describe, and often long after the events; Check against other evidence (archaeology, inscriptions, tablets, other writers)
    Model answer: Both men were outsiders writing about their own side's enemies, Herodotus about 50 years after the Persian Wars and Livy nearly 200 years after Hannibal, so they might be biased or repeat stories they couldn't check. A historian can compare them with archaeology, inscriptions like the Persepolis tablets, or other writers.
  2. Name two ideas from ancient Rome or Greece that influenced the government of the United States, and explain one of them.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric (5 points toward the checkpoint).
    Rubric: Two valid ideas (republic, senate, veto, checks and balances, written laws/rule of law, democracy, civic virtue like Cincinnatus); An accurate explanation of one
    Model answer: The Senate and the veto both came from Rome. The U.S. president can veto laws passed by Congress, just as Roman consuls and tribunes could block decisions, so no one part of the government has all the power.
  3. Explain one way Africa shaped the Mediterranean world in Unit 4.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric (4 points toward the checkpoint).
    Rubric: A valid example (alphabet from Egypt; Carthage as a great power; Alexandria's learning; North Africa as Rome's breadbasket; Terence, Severus, Augustine; Masinissa)
    Model answer: North Africa, including Egypt, grew much of the grain that fed the city of Rome, so the empire depended on Africa to survive.

Answer key: printable page

  1. Describe the government of Persia, Athens, Sparta, Rome and Carthage in one sentence each.
    Answer: Persia: Great King with satraps. Athens: direct democracy for citizen men. Sparta: two kings, council, military assembly. Rome: republic of consuls, Senate, assemblies and tribunes, later an empire. Carthage: trading republic with elected officials and a council.
  2. Explain a cause and an effect of the Persian Wars.
    Answer: Cause: Athens helped the Ionian Revolt. Effect: Greek independence and Athens' rise as a sea power.
  3. Explain a cause and an effect of the Punic Wars.
    Answer: Cause: rivalry over Sicily and the western Mediterranean. Effect: Carthage destroyed; Rome dominant and wealthier.
  4. Trace the alphabet from Egypt to Rome.
    Answer: Semitic-speaking workers in Egypt adapted hieroglyphs; Phoenicians spread it; Greeks added vowels; Romans adapted it into Latin letters.
  5. Name three North Africans who shaped the Roman world.
    Answer: Terence, Septimius Severus, Augustine (also Masinissa).
  6. Why should historians read Herodotus and Livy carefully?
    Answer: They wrote about enemies, sometimes long after the events, and repeated stories they could not check.
