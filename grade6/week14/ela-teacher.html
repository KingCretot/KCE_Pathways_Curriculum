Unit 3: The Power of Poetry · Week 14 · ELA · Teacher Plan

Poems in Conversation Across Time

Lesson length: 60 minutes live, plus the after-class interactive lesson

Objectives

  • Explain how poems can be "in conversation" through response, echo and allusion.
  • Compare how Walt Whitman and Langston Hughes address America and work, and explain how Hughes answers Whitman.
  • Compare how Paul Laurence Dunbar and Georgia Douglas Johnson use the image of a caged bird.
  • Identify the parts of a villanelle and explain how its refrains shape the meaning of Robinson's "The House on the Hill."
  • Use the roots vers, log, dia and lud, and write a poem in conversation with another poem.

Standards

  • ELA.6.R.1.4: Describe the impact of various poetic forms on meaning and style.
  • ELA.6.R.3.3: Compare and contrast how authors from different time periods address the same or related topics.
  • ELA.6.R.1.2: Analyze the development of stated or implied theme(s) throughout a literary text.
  • ELA.6.V.1.2: Apply knowledge of Greek and Latin roots and affixes to determine meanings of words and phrases in grade-level content.
  • ELA.K12.EE.1.1: Cite evidence to explain and justify reasoning.

Materials

  • The interactive lesson
  • About 30 index cards, blue and red markers, a pencil, tape and paper
  • Printable practice page

Vocabulary

  • allusion: A brief reference to a well-known person, place, event or work, which the reader is expected to recognize.
  • response poem: A poem written to answer, argue with or build on an earlier poem.
  • echo: Repeating or closely imitating words from an earlier text.
  • villanelle: A 19-line poem with five tercets and a final quatrain, two rhymes, and two refrains that repeat in a fixed pattern.
  • tercet: A stanza of three lines.
  • extended metaphor: A metaphor that continues through several lines or a whole poem.

Key questions to ask

  • What is the later poem answering?
  • What does the earlier poem leave out?
  • How does the form shape the feeling?
  • Why repeat a line?

Common mistakes to watch for

  • An allusion is the same as an illusion.
  • A response poem must disagree with the original.
  • Repetition in a poem is just filler.
  • Every refrain repeats in exactly the same place in every form (the villanelle's pattern is specific).

Support (if the student is struggling)

  • Give a two-column chart: first poem / answering poem.
  • Color-code the villanelle refrains before reading.
  • Offer sentence frames: "Hughes answers Whitman by ___."

Challenge (if the student is ready for more)

  • Read Whitman's longer "Song of Myself" section 1 (public domain) and look for more ideas Hughes might be answering.
  • Write a full 19-line villanelle.

Background for the teacher

Whitman's "I Hear America Singing" first appeared untitled in the 1860 Leaves of Grass and took its final title and form in later editions; the text here is the 1891–92 edition (Project Gutenberg 1322), including Whitman's spelling "ploughboy." Hughes's poem appears as "Epilogue" at the end of The Weary Blues (1926; Gutenberg 74745); it was first published in 1925 and is widely anthologized as "I, Too." Georgia Douglas Johnson (1880 to 1966) published "The Heart of a Woman" as the title poem of her 1918 collection; text from The Book of American Negro Poetry (1922; Gutenberg 11986). Her Washington, D.C., home was a gathering place for writers (the "S Street Salon"). Dunbar's "Sympathy" (1899) was taught in Week 11; Maya Angelou's memoir I Know Why the Caged Bird Sings appeared in 1969 (only the title is mentioned). Robinson's "The House on the Hill" appeared in the Globe in 1894 and in The Children of the Night (1897; Gutenberg 313). ELA.6.R.1.4's clarification names the sonnet and the villanelle.

Answer key: interactive lesson

Reconnect

  1. In "The Negro Speaks of Rivers," the "I" speaks for a whole people across history. What kind of voice is that?
    Answer: a collective voice
    One speaker who lived through all of history stands for a people.
  2. What is the name for the turn in a sonnet, like line 11 of McKay's "The Tired Worker"?
    Answer: the volta
    "But what steals out..." turns the poem.
  3. Recall last week's roots.
    Answer: move; born
    migrate; native, nation, renaissance.
  4. Why did Jessie Fauset begin "Oriflamme" with an epigraph from Sojourner Truth? Explain in one or two sentences.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: The epigraph introduces the mother and the stars; The poem builds on and answers that memory
    Model answer: Truth's memory of her mother looking at the stars gave Fauset the image her poem builds on, so the poem answers and continues Truth's words.

Learn 1: How Poems Talk to Each Other

  1. What is the term for a brief reference to a well-known person, event or work that the reader is expected to recognize?
    Answer: allusion
    From lud, play: an allusion plays on something the reader already knows.
  2. Use the roots.
    Answer: dialogue; reverse
    dia (across) + log (word); re (back) + vers (turn).
  3. Why might a poet choose to answer an earlier poem instead of writing a completely new one? Give one reason.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: A reasonable purpose: to add a missing voice, correct or challenge an idea, honor an earlier poet, or make readers compare two points of view
    Model answer: A poet might answer an earlier poem to add a voice that was left out. Readers who know the first poem will compare the two and notice what was missing.

Learn 2: Whitman and Hughes: Who Sings America?

  1. In the last line of Hughes's poem, one word replaces sing from the first line. Type that word.
    Answer: am
    "I, too, sing America" becomes "I, too, am America."
  2. What does being sent "to eat in the kitchen / When company comes" stand for? Explain the image.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Stands for segregation or exclusion: being treated as less than a full member of the family or nation; Notes he is still part of the household ("darker brother") but kept out of sight
    Model answer: It stands for segregation. The speaker is part of the family, a "darker brother," but he is hidden away when guests come, the way Black Americans were kept separate and treated as less than full citizens.
  3. Why is changing "sing" to "am" in the last line so powerful? Connect it to Whitman's poem.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Moves from taking part in America's song to claiming to be America itself; Answers Whitman: not just one more singer added to the list, but part of America's identity
    Model answer: In Whitman's poem, Americans sing; in Hughes's first line, he joins that singing. But in the last line he claims more: he doesn't just sing America, he is America. He is not asking to be added to Whitman's list; he is saying America cannot be described without him.
  4. Compare the two poems.
    Answer: (any worker such as mason, shoemaker, hatter); brother
    Whitman lists many workers; Hughes is a "brother" in the American family.

Learn 3: Dunbar and Johnson: The Caged Bird

  1. In Johnson's first stanza, what does the heart of a woman go forth with?
    Answer: the dawn
    "The heart of a woman goes forth with the dawn"; stanza 2 begins with night.
  2. Johnson calls the cage's bars "sheltering." Why is that word surprising, and what does it suggest about the woman's life?
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Surprising: bars usually trap or imprison, but sheltering means protecting; Suggests her limits are also her safety (home, expectations), so she is trapped and protected at once; a mixed or painful feeling
    Model answer: Bars usually trap you, so calling them "sheltering" is surprising. It suggests her limits, like home and what society expects of women, also keep her safe. She is protected and trapped at the same time, which makes her situation more painful and complicated.
  3. Both Dunbar and Johnson use a bird in a cage. Name one similarity and one difference in how they use it.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Similarity: the bird stands for a person whose freedom is limited; the bird hurts itself on the bars; Difference: Dunbar's bird is caged all the time and sings or prays; Johnson's flies free by day and returns at night, and its bars are "sheltering"; Johnson focuses on women
    Model answer: Both poets use the caged bird for a person whose freedom is limited, and both birds hurt themselves on the bars. But Dunbar's bird is caged all the time and sings a prayer, while Johnson's flies free by day and only returns to the cage at night, and Johnson focuses on a woman's life.
  4. In Johnson's last line, which word is repeated three times in a row?
    Answer: breaks
    "While it breaks, breaks, breaks": the repetition sounds like the heart hitting the bars again and again.

Learn 4: The Villanelle: A Form That Cannot Let Go

  1. How many lines are in a villanelle? Count Robinson's poem to check.
    Answer: 19 lines
    Five tercets (15 lines) + one quatrain (4 lines) = 19.
  2. Type the rhyme scheme of the first tercet of "The House on the Hill." Type the three letters with no spaces.
    Answer: aba
    away (a), still (b), say (a).
  3. How do the two refrains, "They are all gone away" and "There is nothing more to say," create the poem's feeling? Use the word refrain in your answer.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: The refrains repeat throughout, creating a feeling of grief, emptiness or loss that won't go away; The repetition imitates someone who cannot stop thinking about the loss (or the circling of the house); saying "nothing more to say" again and again is ironic
    Model answer: Each refrain keeps returning, like a sad thought someone can't escape. Repeating "They are all gone away" makes the emptiness feel permanent. And repeating "There is nothing more to say" is ironic: the speaker keeps saying it because he cannot stop thinking about the people who left.
  4. Robinson's poem uses only two rhyme sounds. One is the sound in away, say and gray. Type a word from the poem that has the other rhyme sound.
    Answer: still, shrill, ill, sill, skill or Hill
    The b rhymes: still, shrill, ill, sill, skill, Hill.

Hands-On

  1. How many times did each refrain appear in Robinson's poem? What did you notice when you stomped on every refrain?
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Each refrain appears 4 times (blue: lines 1, 6, 12, 18; red: lines 3, 9, 15, 19); A thoughtful observation about the rhythm of repetition and the feeling it creates
    Model answer: Each refrain appeared 4 times. Stomping on them made me notice that they come every third line, like footsteps walking around the house over and over, which matches the feeling of someone who cannot leave the past behind.
  2. Write your response poem. Then, in one or two sentences, name the poem you answered and the line or image you echoed.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: 8 to 14 lines (or a villanelle); Clearly answers a poem from Weeks 11 to 14; Echoes at least one line or image; Explanation names the poem and the echo
    Model answer: I, Too, Build America I hear the hammers ringing on my street, the roofer's song, my uncle on the beam, my mother's footsteps, steady, strong and fleet. They never wrote our names in Whitman's dream, but here we are, the ones who pour the floor, who frame the walls and paint the doors of cream. Tomorrow, we will walk in through the door. I answered Whitman's "I Hear America Singing" and Hughes's "Epilogue." I echoed Whitman's list of workers and Hughes's "Tomorrow" and "I, too."

Think Deeper

  1. Is Hughes rejecting Whitman's America, or claiming his place in it? Take a position and support it with at least two quotations from "Epilogue" and one from "I Hear America Singing."
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Clear position (claiming his place is the strongest reading, but a well-supported reading of rejection is acceptable); At least two accurate quotations from Hughes; At least one accurate quotation from Whitman; Explains how the quotations support the position
    Model answer: Hughes is claiming his place in Whitman's America, not rejecting it. He uses Whitman's own verb when he writes "I, too, sing America," which shows he wants to join the chorus Whitman described, where everyone sings "what belongs to him or her and to none else." He criticizes the way he is treated, sent "to eat in the kitchen / When company comes," but he does not leave the house; he predicts "Tomorrow, / I'll sit at the table." His last line, "I, too, am America," shows he believes in America's promise and insists it include him.
  2. Why do you think Robinson chose the villanelle, with its repeating refrains, for a poem about an empty house? Would the poem be as powerful as a short paragraph? Explain.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Connects the repeating refrains to grief, memory or the speaker's inability to let go; Explains that a paragraph would lose the repetition, rhythm and circling effect; Uses at least one quotation
    Model answer: Robinson chose the villanelle because its refrains circle back, just as the speaker keeps returning to the house. "They are all gone away" comes back four times, so the reader feels the loss growing heavier. As a paragraph, the same words would say it once and move on, and we would lose the feeling of someone who cannot stop remembering.

Exit Check

  1. How many lines does a villanelle have?
    Answer: 19 lines
    Five tercets and a quatrain.
  2. In a villanelle, what are the two lines that keep repeating called?
    Answer: refrains
    A1 and A2 repeat in a fixed pattern.
  3. Type the root that means turn, found in verse and conversation.
    Answer: vers
    vers = turn.
  4. Give one way Hughes's "Epilogue" answers Whitman's "I Hear America Singing."
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: An accurate connection (adds the left-out voice of Black Americans; reuses "sing America"; moves from singing to being America)
    Model answer: Whitman's list of singing Americans left out Black Americans, so Hughes adds his voice with "I, too, sing America."

Answer key: printable page

  1. Define allusion and response poem.
    Answer: Allusion: a brief reference to a well-known person, event or work. Response poem: a poem that answers an earlier poem.
  2. Explain how Hughes's "Epilogue" answers Whitman's "I Hear America Singing."
    Answer: Hughes adds the voice of Black Americans left out of Whitman's list, reuses "sing America," and ends by claiming "I, too, am America."
  3. What does the image of eating in the kitchen "when company comes" represent?
    Answer: Segregation: being treated as less than a full member of the family and nation.
  4. Explain Johnson's extended metaphor in "The Heart of a Woman."
    Answer: The heart is a bird that flies free by day and returns at night to a cage with "sheltering bars."
  5. Label the villanelle pattern: which lines are refrains in Robinson's poem?
    Answer: "They are all gone away" (lines 1, 6, 12, 18) and "There is nothing more to say" (lines 3, 9, 15, 19).
  6. Use the roots: what do dialogue and monologue share, and what does dia add?
    Answer: Both contain log (word, speech); dia means across, so a dialogue is speech across two or more people.
