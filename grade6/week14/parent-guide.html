Week 14: The Silk Roads, Microorganisms, Unit Conversions and Poems in Conversation

This week your child follows silk, spices, ivory and ideas along the Silk Roads and the Indian Ocean sea routes, discovers the microorganisms that shape life on Earth, converts measurements and money with ratios, and reads poems that answer one another across time, including a villanelle.

How scoring works: each lesson scores itself. Your child gets two tries per question: full points on the first try, half points on the second, then the answer and explanation appear. A score of 85 or higher means the lesson is mastered. Written answers go to Jonathan, who scores them with a rubric.

Time this week: About 60 minutes of live lessons per subject, plus 30 to 45 minutes of after-class work per subject.

Gather this week: A scarf, ribbon or strip of fabric, 6 index cards or sheets of paper, paper and pencil, a timer or phone, and optionally a calculator (Social Studies); 3 slices of the same bread, 3 zip-top bags, a permanent marker, a fine-tipped marker, clean tongs, soap and water (Science); a tape measure or yardstick with inches and centimeters, a 1-cup measure, a clean gallon jug or pitcher, water, paper and pencil, a calculator for checking only, and optionally a kitchen scale (Math); about 30 index cards or paper strips, a blue and a red marker, a pencil, tape and paper (ELA).

Heads up: Science: the bread test runs for 5 to 7 days. The bags must stay sealed the whole time and be thrown away unopened; keep them away from food, pets and younger children, and skip the test if anyone at home is allergic to mold. Social Studies sets up six stations around the house, so please clear the walking path. Math pours water to fill a gallon jug; a sink or the outdoors works best. ELA's Langston Hughes poem uses the image of being sent to eat in the kitchen "when company comes" to describe segregation.

ELA: Poems in Conversation Across Time

Your child reads Walt Whitman's "I Hear America Singing" and Langston Hughes's answer, "I, too, sing America"; compares Paul Laurence Dunbar's caged bird with Georgia Douglas Johnson's "The Heart of a Woman"; and learns the villanelle through Edwin Arlington Robinson's "The House on the Hill." They build the villanelle with cards and write a response poem. Roots: vers, log, dia and lud.

Ask at dinner: "What is Hughes answering in Whitman's poem?"

Try it together: Help lay out the villanelle cards and stomp on each refrain together.

Signs it's clicking: They explain how a later poem answers an earlier one and how a form like the villanelle shapes a poem's feeling. Signs they need help: They think repetition is filler. Ask why the speaker keeps saying "There is nothing more to say."

Math: Converting Units with Ratios, and Currency Exchange

Your child converts customary units of length, capacity and weight with ratio tables, converts metric units by powers of 10, and uses exchange rates (with example rates) to convert dollars to euros, Mexican pesos and Jamaican dollars, including exchange fees.

Ask at dinner: "Should the number get bigger or smaller when you convert?"

Try it together: Measure a room together in inches, then have your child convert it to feet and yards.

Signs it's clicking: They multiply when converting to a smaller unit and divide when converting to a larger one. Signs they need help: They multiply by the exchange rate when converting back to dollars. Ask: are pesos worth more or less than dollars?

Life Science: Microorganisms

Your child learns about bacteria, archaea, protists and microscopic fungi (and why viruses are not considered living), compares their sizes in micrometers, explores how microbes help ecosystems and food, compares the pathogens that cause infections, and learns about Alexander Fleming and chemist Alice Ball. They run a week-long hand-washing test with bread.

Ask at dinner: "Why won't antibiotics help a cold?"

Try it together: Look at the bread bags together each day and count the moldy grid squares.

Signs it's clicking: They give examples of helpful microbes, not just germs. Signs they need help: They think viruses are tiny bacteria. Ask what makes a bacterium a living cell.

World and American History: The Silk Road and the Sea Roads

Your child learns how Zhang Qian helped open the Silk Roads, how relay trade raised prices, how monsoon winds powered Indian Ocean trade, how Aksum's port of Adulis connected Africa to Rome and India (using a first-century merchant's guidebook), and how Buddhism, papermaking and even disease spread along trade routes.

Ask at dinner: "What traveled the Silk Roads besides silk?"

Try it together: Walk the relay route with your child and compare the first and last message cards.

Signs it's clicking: They explain both goods and ideas, and why prices rose along the route. Signs they need help: They picture one paved road. Ask why historians say "Silk Roads."
