Unit 3: Ancient Asia · Week 14 · World and American History · Teacher Plan

The Silk Road and the Sea Roads

Lesson length: 60 minutes live, plus the after-class interactive lesson

Objectives

  • Trace the land Silk Roads and the Indian Ocean sea routes and explain how geography shaped each one.
  • Explain how Zhang Qian's missions helped open the Silk Roads under the Han dynasty.
  • Describe the goods traded and explain how relay trade made prices rise.
  • Explain how monsoon winds and East African ports such as Adulis connected Africa to Asia and the Mediterranean, using a primary source.
  • Analyze how religions, inventions and diseases spread along trade routes (cultural diffusion).

Standards

  • SS.6.W.4.10: Explain the significance of the silk roads and maritime routes across the Indian Ocean to the movement of goods and ideas among Asia, East Africa, and the Mediterranean Basin.
  • SS.6.W.4.4: Explain the teachings of Buddha, the importance of Asoka, and how Buddhism spread in India, Ceylon, and other parts of Asia (spread along trade routes).
  • SS.6.W.3.18: Describe the rise and fall of the ancient east African kingdoms of Kush and Axum and Christianity's development in Ethiopia (reconnect: Aksum's trade).
  • SS.6.G.2.6: Explain the concept of cultural diffusion, and identify the influences of different ancient cultures on one another.
  • SS.6.W.1.3: Interpret primary and secondary sources.

Materials

  • The interactive lesson
  • A scarf or ribbon, 6 index cards, paper and pencil, a timer
  • A world map or globe
  • Printable practice page

Vocabulary

  • Silk Roads: Networks of land routes linking China with Central Asia, Persia, India and the Mediterranean.
  • caravan: A group of traders traveling together with pack animals, for safety.
  • oasis: A place in a desert with water, where plants grow and travelers can rest.
  • relay trade: Trade in which goods pass from merchant to merchant along a route instead of one trader carrying them all the way.
  • monsoon: A seasonal wind that blows from one direction in summer and the opposite direction in winter.
  • cultural diffusion: The spread of ideas, beliefs, inventions and customs from one culture to another.
  • pilgrim: A person who travels to a holy place for religious reasons.

Key questions to ask

  • Why did prices rise along the route?
  • What besides goods moved along trade routes?
  • How did geography shape each route?
  • What can a merchant's guidebook tell us, and what can't it?

Common mistakes to watch for

  • The Silk Road was one paved road.
  • Merchants traveled the whole route from China to Rome.
  • Only silk was traded.
  • Africa was outside ancient world trade.

Support (if the student is struggling)

  • Give a station chart with columns for cost, profit and new price.
  • Pre-teach monsoon with a two-arrow drawing.
  • Read the Periplus excerpt aloud and define Auxumites first.

Challenge (if the student is ready for more)

  • Read more of the Periplus (free online) and list five goods traded at one port.
  • Research the Mogao Caves and the Diamond Sutra (868 CE), the oldest dated printed book.

Background for the teacher

Pliny the Elder (Natural History 12.84, about 77 CE) estimated that India, the Seres and Arabia drew 100 million sesterces a year from the empire. Zhang Qian left about 138 BCE, was held by the Xiongnu for about ten years and returned about 126/125 BCE; Sima Qian's Shiji records his report, including the Ferghana horses. Ferdinand von Richthofen coined Seidenstrasse in 1877. Sogdian was a lingua franca of the Silk Roads. The Periplus of the Erythraean Sea dates to the mid-first century CE; quotations are from W. H. Schoff's 1912 translation, sections 4 and 5. Monsoon timing: southwest winds about May to September, northeast about November to March. Mogao cave carving began in 366 CE; Faxian set out in 399 CE. Paper reached the Islamic world in the 700s (Samarkand). The Plague of Justinian began in 541 CE; trade-route spread is the mainstream view. Relay prices in the chart are illustrative examples, not historical figures.

Answer key: interactive lesson

Reconnect

  1. Shang priests carved questions to the ancestors on turtle shells and ox bones. What are these objects called?
    Answer: oracle bones
    They hold the oldest known Chinese writing.
  2. Complete the Confucian idea.
    Answer: piety
    Filial piety was central to Confucius's teaching.
  3. In the dynastic cycle, what comes after a dynasty declines? What did people say had happened to the Mandate of Heaven?
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Rebellion follows decline; People said the dynasty had lost the Mandate of Heaven, and a new dynasty claimed it
    Model answer: After decline comes rebellion. People said the rulers had lost the Mandate of Heaven, and a new dynasty claimed it.
  4. Which dynasty followed the Qin and ruled China from 206 BCE to 220 CE?
    Answer: the Han dynasty
    The Han kept Qin's unity but ruled with Confucian ideas.

Learn 1: Opening the Road: Zhang Qian

  1. Who was the Han officer whose journeys west, starting about 138 BCE, helped open the Silk Roads?
    Answer: Zhang Qian
    Zhang Qian's reports to Emperor Wudi led to more missions and trade.
  2. Why were oases so important on the Silk Roads? Explain using the geography of the route.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Routes crossed or skirted deserts like the Taklamakan with little water; Oases gave water, food, rest and markets, so caravans traveled from oasis to oasis and towns grew there
    Model answer: The routes skirted the Taklamakan Desert, where there is almost no water. Oases provided water, food and rest for people and camels, so caravans moved from one oasis to the next, and trading towns like Dunhuang grew there.
  3. Historians often say "Silk Roads" instead of "the Silk Road." Why is the plural more accurate?
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: There was no single road but a web of many routes (and sea routes too); Routes changed with politics, weather and danger
    Model answer: There was never one road. Traders used a web of different routes that changed with wars, weather and danger, and there were sea routes too, so "roads" is more accurate.
  4. What kind of camel, with two humps, carried goods through the cold deserts and mountains of Central Asia?
    Answer: the Bactrian camel
    Bactrian camels handle cold and drought well.

Learn 2: Silk, Spices and Relay Trade

  1. Use the bar chart. By what percent did the silk's price rise between Antioch (36 coins) and Rome (72 coins)?
    Answer: 100 %
    It rose by 36, and 36 is 100% of 36: the price doubled.
  2. How many times the Chang'an price is the Rome price? (72 ÷ 10)
    Answer: 7.2 times
    72 ÷ 10 = 7.2.
  3. Give two reasons the price of silk rose at each stop along the route.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Costs: camels, food, guards, tolls, taxes, risk of loss; Each merchant added a profit (markup)
    Model answer: Each merchant had to pay for camels, food, guards and tolls, and each one added a profit for himself, so the price grew at every stop.
  4. Match the region to a good it sent along the routes.
    Answer: pepper (or cotton, gems); glass (or gold, silver, wool); horses
    India: pepper, cotton, gems. Rome: glass, gold, silver, wool. Ferghana: "heavenly horses."

Learn 3: The Sea Roads and the Monsoon

  1. What seasonal winds let sailors cross the Indian Ocean one way in summer and back in winter?
    Answer: the monsoon winds
    Southwest in summer, northeast in winter.
  2. According to the Periplus, what valuable good was brought to the Auxumites from beyond the Nile and then shipped from Adulis?
    Answer: ivory
    "all the ivory is brought from the country beyond the Nile"
  3. The Periplus says Zoscales was "acquainted with Greek literature." What does this detail suggest about Aksum's place in the ancient world?
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Aksum was connected to the Greek-speaking Mediterranean world through trade; Its leaders were educated and cosmopolitan; an African kingdom at the center of international trade
    Model answer: It suggests Aksum was closely connected to the Greek-speaking Mediterranean world through trade. Its ruler was educated and could deal with foreign merchants in their own language, which shows an African kingdom at the center of international trade.
  4. The Periplus was written by a merchant, not a historian. Name one way that could make it a useful source and one way it could be limited.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Useful: firsthand knowledge of ports, goods, distances and prices; Limited: a merchant's point of view (interested in profit, may judge local people unfairly, may not see inland life)
    Model answer: It is useful because the writer saw the ports himself and knew exactly what goods were traded and where. It is limited because he cared mostly about trade and profit, so he says little about ordinary people's lives and sometimes describes local people unfairly.

Learn 4: Ideas on the Move

  1. Which religion began in India and spread to China along the Silk Roads?
    Answer: Buddhism
    Merchants and monks carried Buddhism east; the Mogao Caves at Dunhuang are evidence.
  2. Give one example of cultural diffusion from the lesson and explain how trade made it possible.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: An accurate example (Buddhism to China, paper west, grapes to China, Greek art in Gandhara); Explains the role of traders, monks or travelers on the routes
    Model answer: Buddhism spread from India to China because merchants and monks traveled the same routes as the silk caravans, bringing teachings and texts with them.
  3. Trade routes spread religions and inventions, but also disease. What does this tell you about connecting distant parts of the world?
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Connection brings both benefits and risks; A thoughtful comparison to today (travel, pandemics, global trade)
    Model answer: Connecting the world brings good things like new ideas, foods and beliefs, but it also brings risks, because sickness can travel as fast as goods. The same is true today, when airplanes spread both trade and diseases quickly.

Hands-On

  1. Show the silk's price at each station and the final price in Rome. How many times the starting price was it?
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Correct prices: Dunhuang 15, Kashgar 22.50, Samarkand 33.75, Antioch 56.63, Rome 56.63; Final price compared with 10 coins (about 5.7 times)
    Model answer: Chang'an 10. Dunhuang: 10 + 2 = 12, then 25% more = 15. Kashgar: 15 + 3 = 18, then 25% more = 22.50. Samarkand: 50% more = 33.75. Antioch: 33.75 + 4 = 37.75, then 50% more = 56.625, about 56.63. Rome sells it for 56.63 coins, about 5.7 times the starting price.
  2. How did the message change from the first card to the last? What does this suggest about how ideas changed as they traveled the Silk Roads?
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Specific changes described; Connects to real diffusion: ideas were adapted, simplified or blended with local beliefs as they traveled (Buddhism in China took on Chinese features)
    Model answer: By Rome, my message said "Monks carried Buddha's teachings and people carved temples." Details like India and Dunhuang dropped out. Real ideas changed the same way: as Buddhism traveled, people adapted it to their own languages and customs, so Chinese Buddhism looked different from Indian Buddhism.

Think Deeper

  1. Were the Silk Roads and sea roads more important for the goods they carried or for the ideas they spread? Take a position and support it with at least two pieces of evidence from the lesson.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Clear position; At least two accurate pieces of evidence; Acknowledges the other side; Logical conclusion
    Model answer: I think the ideas were more important. Silk and pepper were used up long ago, but Buddhism, which traveled from India to China along the routes, still shapes the lives of hundreds of millions of people. Papermaking, which spread west from China, changed how the whole world records knowledge. Goods mattered too, because trade made cities like Samarkand and Adulis rich, and without the goods no one would have made the journey. But the goods were the reason for the trip, while the ideas were the lasting result.
  2. You are a merchant in 100 CE. You have 1,000 kilograms of Indian pepper to send to Rome. Would you choose the land route or the sea route? Explain using at least three factors (cost, amount, speed, danger, timing).
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: A clear choice (sea is the stronger answer for heavy bulk goods); At least three factors explained accurately (ships carry far more; fewer tolls and middlemen; monsoon timing; storms and pirates; deserts and raiders); Reasoning connects factors to the decision
    Model answer: I would choose the sea route. A ship can carry far more than a camel caravan, so moving 1,000 kilograms costs much less per kilogram. By sea there are fewer middlemen and tolls than on the land route. The risks are storms and pirates, and I would have to wait for the right monsoon wind, but with good timing a ship can reach Egypt within months, while camels would need many more animals, guards and border taxes.

Exit Check

  1. Which seasonal wind pattern made sea trade across the Indian Ocean possible?
    Answer: the monsoon
    Ships sailed east in summer and west in winter.
  2. Silk passed from Chinese to Sogdian to Parthian merchants before reaching Rome. What is this kind of trade called?
    Answer: relay trade
    Goods passed from merchant to merchant.
  3. What is the term for the spread of beliefs, inventions and customs from one culture to another?
    Answer: cultural diffusion
    Example: Buddhism spreading from India to China.
  4. Explain how Aksum was connected to the trade routes you studied this week.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Through its Red Sea port of Adulis on the sea routes; Sent ivory (and gold) and traded with Rome, Arabia and India
    Model answer: Aksum's port of Adulis on the Red Sea linked it to the sea routes. It sent ivory brought from beyond the Nile to Rome and traded with Arabia and India.

Answer key: printable page

  1. Who was Zhang Qian, and why is he important?
    Answer: A Han officer sent west about 138 BCE; his reports led to Han missions and trade, opening the Silk Roads.
  2. Explain relay trade and why it made silk expensive in Rome.
    Answer: Goods passed from merchant to merchant; each added costs and profit.
  3. How did the monsoon winds make Indian Ocean trade possible?
    Answer: Ships sailed east with the summer southwest wind and back west with the winter northeast wind.
  4. What does the Periplus tell us about Aksum?
    Answer: Ivory was brought to the Auxumites and shipped from Adulis; its ruler knew Greek literature; Aksum was a major trading power.
  5. Give two examples of cultural diffusion along trade routes.
    Answer: Buddhism from India to China; papermaking west; grapes to China; Greek styles in Gandhara.
  6. A silk bolt cost 20 coins in Dunhuang and 50 coins in Antioch. By what percent did the price rise?
    Answer: Rose by 30; 30 ÷ 20 = 150%.
