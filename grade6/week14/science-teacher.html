Unit 3: The Diversity of Life · Week 14 · Life Science · Teacher Plan

Microorganisms

Lesson length: 60 minutes live, plus the after-class interactive lesson

Objectives

  • Describe the main groups of microorganisms (bacteria, archaea, protists and microscopic fungi) and explain why viruses are not counted as living.
  • Use metric units to compare the sizes of microorganisms.
  • Explain how microorganisms help ecosystems, people and food production.
  • Compare bacteria, viruses, fungi and protists that cause infections, and explain how hand-washing and antibiotics work.
  • Design and carry out a fair test of hand-washing, identifying variables.

Standards

  • SC.6.L.14.6: Compare and contrast types of infectious agents that may infect the human body, including viruses, bacteria, fungi, and parasites.
  • SC.6.L.15.1: Analyze and describe how and why organisms are classified according to shared characteristics with emphasis on the Linnaean system combined with the concept of Domains.
  • SC.6.N.1.1: Define a problem from the sixth grade curriculum, use appropriate reference materials to support scientific understanding, plan and carry out scientific investigation of various types, such as systematic observations or experiments, identify variables, collect and organize data, interpret data in charts, tables, and graphics, analyze information, make predictions, and defend conclusions.
  • SC.6.N.1.2: Explain why scientific investigations should be replicable.
  • SC.6.N.2.3: Recognize that scientists who make contributions to scientific knowledge come from all kinds of backgrounds and possess varied talents, interests, and goals.

Materials

  • The interactive lesson
  • 3 slices of bread, 3 zip-top bags, tongs, soap, water, a clean towel, paper and pencil, a permanent marker and a fine-tipped marker
  • Printable practice page

Vocabulary

  • microorganism: A living thing too small to see without a microscope; also called a microbe.
  • bacteria: Single-celled organisms with no nucleus; found almost everywhere.
  • protist: A usually single-celled organism with a nucleus, such as an amoeba, a paramecium or an alga.
  • decomposer: An organism that breaks down dead plants and animals and returns nutrients to the soil.
  • pathogen: A microorganism or virus that causes disease.
  • antibiotic: A medicine that kills bacteria or stops them from growing; it does not work on viruses.
  • micrometer (µm): One millionth of a meter; 1,000 micrometers = 1 millimeter.

Key questions to ask

  • What makes something a microorganism?
  • How do microbes help us?
  • Why don't antibiotics cure a cold?
  • What makes a test fair?

Common mistakes to watch for

  • All bacteria are harmful.
  • Antibiotics kill viruses.
  • Viruses are tiny bacteria.
  • Hand sanitizer and hand-washing remove every germ.

Support (if the student is struggling)

  • Review metric conversions before the size items.

Challenge (if the student is ready for more)

Background for the teacher

Karenia brevis is a dinoflagellate protist; Florida red tides produce brevetoxins (FWC Fish and Wildlife Research Institute). Phytoplankton produce roughly half of the oxygen generated on Earth (NOAA estimates range from about 50% to 80% of oxygen production occurs in the ocean). Fleming observed Penicillium's effect in September 1928; Florey and Chain developed penicillin as a drug around 1940. Alice Ball (1892 to 1916) developed an injectable chaulmoogra oil extract in 1915 at the College of Hawaii; Arthur Dean published it without credit; the University of Hawaii formally recognized her work in 2000. CDC recommends scrubbing hands for at least 20 seconds. Size estimates: bacteria about 1 to 5 µm; human hair about 17 to 180 µm wide; Paramecium about 50 to 300 µm. Bread mold test: sealed bags only; discard unopened.

Answer key: interactive lesson

Reconnect

  1. What is the name for a tool that identifies organisms through a series of paired, opposite choices?
    Answer: a dichotomous key
    Dicho = in two.
  2. How many couplets does a key need to identify 10 different organisms?
    Answer: 9 couplets
    Always one fewer couplet than organisms: 10 − 1 = 9.
  3. A leaf edge with small jagged points is called ___ (the opposite of smooth or entire).
    Answer: toothed
    The margin is toothed.
  4. Why should a good key avoid color as a trait?
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Color changes with season, age or light, so the same organism could key out two ways
    Model answer: Color changes with the season, the leaf's age and the light, so the same tree could be identified two different ways.

Learn 1: Meet the Microbes

  1. A bacterium is about 2 micrometers long. How many could line up end to end across a sand grain 1 millimeter (1,000 µm) wide?
    Answer: 500 bacteria
    1,000 ÷ 2 = 500.
  2. Bacteria and protists are both single-celled, but only protists have a ___.
    Answer: nucleus
    Bacteria have no nucleus; protists do.
  3. What group of microorganism is the red tide organism Karenia brevis?
    Answer: a protist
    It is a single-celled organism with a nucleus.
  4. Bacteria live in hot springs, polar ice, deep oceans and inside your body. Why might being tiny and single-celled help them live in so many places?
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Small size lets them spread easily (air, water, on surfaces) and need few resources; They reproduce quickly, so populations adapt to many conditions
    Model answer: Because they are tiny, bacteria are carried easily by air, water and animals, and they need very little food or space. They also reproduce very fast, so some can survive almost anywhere.

Learn 2: Helpful Microbes

  1. A fallen oak log in a Florida hammock turns soft and crumbly over a few years as fungi and bacteria break it down. What role are those fungi and bacteria playing?
    Answer: decomposers
    Decomposers break down dead matter and return nutrients to the soil.
  2. Which scientist noticed in 1928 that a mold killed the bacteria around it, leading to penicillin?
    Answer: Alexander Fleming
    The mold was Penicillium.
  3. Imagine all decomposers on Earth disappeared. Describe two things that would happen to a Florida forest over the next few years.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Dead leaves, logs and animals would pile up instead of breaking down; Soil would run out of nutrients, so new plants could not grow well (the whole food web suffers)
    Model answer: Dead leaves, fallen branches and dead animals would pile up and never rot away. The nutrients locked in them would not return to the soil, so new plants would grow poorly, and the animals that eat those plants would suffer too.
  4. Fleming's discovery started with an accident: a mold got into his dish. Why do you think he noticed it when someone else might have thrown the dish away? What does this say about how science works?
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: He observed carefully and asked why the bacteria near the mold had died; Science depends on curiosity and careful observation, not only planned experiments (chance favors a prepared mind)
    Model answer: Fleming looked carefully and asked why there were no bacteria around the mold instead of just cleaning the dish. Science often moves forward when someone notices something unexpected and is curious enough to investigate it.

Learn 3: Harmful Microbes and How We Fight Them

  1. Antibiotics can kill bacteria. What kind of pathogen do they not work on, such as the ones that cause colds and flu?
    Answer: viruses
    Viruses are not cells, so antibiotics cannot attack them.
  2. Match each disease to the type of pathogen that causes it.
    Answer: bacteria; protist (parasite); fungus
    Strep is bacterial; malaria is caused by a protist parasite; athlete's foot is a fungus.
  3. Explain in your own words how overusing antibiotics can create antibiotic-resistant bacteria.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: A few bacteria naturally survive the antibiotic; Those survivors multiply, so the new population is mostly resistant and harder to treat
    Model answer: When antibiotics are used, most bacteria die, but a few that happen to be tougher survive. Those survivors multiply, so soon most of the bacteria are the tough kind, and the antibiotic no longer works against them.
  4. Alice Ball's work was published by someone else without credit. Why does it matter that scientists today recognize who really made a discovery?
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Fairness and honesty: people deserve credit for their work; Recognition shows that scientists come from all backgrounds and inspires young people; accurate history
    Model answer: It matters because it is fair and honest to give people credit for their work. Recognizing Alice Ball also shows that great scientists come from every background, which can inspire students who might not otherwise see themselves as scientists.

Learn 4: Testing Microbes Safely: A Fair Test

  1. A different student tests whether bread kept in the refrigerator grows less mold than bread kept on the counter. What is the independent variable in her test?
    Answer: where the bread is stored (its temperature)
    She changes only where the bread is stored; the mold is what she measures.
  2. Why do you need the control slice that is touched only with clean tongs?
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: It shows how much mold grows without hands, from spores already in the air or bread; Gives a baseline to compare the other slices against
    Model answer: The control shows how much mold grows from spores that were already in the air or on the bread, without any hands. Then I can tell whether the hands added more.
  3. On Day 5, mold covers 18 of the 24 squares of a grid drawn on the bag over one slice. What percent of the slice is covered?
    Answer: 75 %
    18/24 = 3/4 = 75%.

Hands-On

  1. Make a data table with the day and the number of squares (out of 24) showing mold for each bag. Convert your last day's counts to percents.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Table with days and counts for all three bags; Last-day counts converted to percents correctly; Observations or sketches described
    Model answer: Day 1: 0, 0, 0. Day 3: control 0, unwashed 2, washed 0. Day 5: control 1, unwashed 9, washed 2. Day 7: control 3, unwashed 18, washed 5. Day 7 percents: control 12.5%, unwashed 75%, washed about 20.8%. The unwashed slice grew fuzzy green and black spots first.
  2. Write a conclusion. What did your evidence show about hand-washing? Name one way your test could be improved to make it more reliable.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: A claim supported by the data; Compares with the control; A sensible improvement (more slices per group, repeat the test, same person rubbing the same way, measure area more precisely)
    Model answer: My evidence shows that hand-washing reduced mold growth: on Day 7 the unwashed slice was 75% covered but the washed slice only about 21%, close to the control's 12.5%. To make it more reliable, I would use three slices for each group and repeat the test, because one slice could be unusual.

Think Deeper

  1. Some people think of all microbes as "germs." Are microorganisms more helpful or more harmful to life on Earth? Take a position and support it with at least three examples from the lesson.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Clear position (helpful is the stronger, evidence-based answer); At least three accurate examples; Acknowledges harmful microbes; Logical conclusion
    Model answer: Microorganisms are far more helpful than harmful. Decomposers recycle dead matter so forests can keep growing, bacteria in roots add nitrogen that crops need, and ocean phytoplankton make roughly half of Earth's oxygen. Gut bacteria help us digest food, and a mold gave us penicillin. Some microbes do cause harm, like the bacteria behind strep throat or the toxins of a red tide, and we have to protect ourselves with hand-washing and medicine. But without microbes, life on Earth could not exist, while without pathogens it would simply be healthier.
  2. A friend has a cold and asks a doctor for antibiotics "just in case." Explain why the doctor says no, using what you know about viruses, bacteria and resistance.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Colds are caused by viruses, which antibiotics cannot kill; Taking unneeded antibiotics can help resistant bacteria spread; A helpful alternative (rest, fluids, hand-washing; see a doctor if it gets worse)
    Model answer: Colds are caused by viruses, and antibiotics only work on bacteria, so they would not help. Taking antibiotics when they aren't needed also gives bacteria in the body a chance to become resistant, so the medicine might not work when it is really needed. The friend should rest, drink fluids and wash hands, and see the doctor again if they get worse.

Exit Check

  1. What kind of pathogen causes colds and flu?
    Answer: viruses
    That is why antibiotics do not help a cold.
  2. How many micrometers are in 3 millimeters?
    Answer: 3000 µm
    3 × 1,000 = 3,000 µm.
  3. What is the name for a medicine that kills bacteria, such as penicillin?
    Answer: an antibiotic
    Penicillin came from a mold.
  4. Give one way microorganisms help people or ecosystems.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: An accurate example (decomposing, nitrogen in soil, oxygen, digestion, food, medicine)
    Model answer: Decomposer bacteria and fungi break down dead plants and return nutrients to the soil.

Answer key: printable page

  1. Name the four main groups of microorganisms and give one example of each.
    Answer: Bacteria (Salmonella); archaea (hot-spring microbes); protists (amoeba, Karenia brevis); microscopic fungi (yeast, bread mold).
  2. Why are viruses not usually considered living?
    Answer: They are not cells and can only copy themselves inside a living cell.
  3. Give three ways microbes help people or ecosystems.
    Answer: Decomposing; adding nitrogen to soil; making oxygen; digestion; bread, yogurt and cheese; penicillin.
  4. Why don't antibiotics work on colds?
    Answer: Colds are caused by viruses; antibiotics only work on bacteria.
  5. How many 4-micrometer bacteria fit across 2 millimeters?
    Answer: 2,000 ÷ 4 = 500.
  6. Name the independent, dependent and controlled variables in the bread test.
    Answer: Independent: how the hands were cleaned. Dependent: percent of bread covered with mold. Controlled: bread type, bags, water, place, temperature.
