Unit 3: The Diversity of Life · Week 15 · Life Science · Teacher Plan

Mid-Year Review and Field Guide Project

Lesson length: 60 minutes live, plus the after-class interactive lesson

Objectives

  • Explain how the four Unit 3 ideas connect: characteristics of life, classification, keys and microorganisms.
  • Connect classification to cell structure from Unit 2 (nucleus, cell wall, chloroplasts).
  • Plan and produce a field guide with accurate classification, scientific names, observations and a dichotomous key.
  • Observe living things safely and record evidence.
  • Show mastery of Unit 3 on the checkpoint.

Standards

  • SC.6.L.15.1: Analyze and describe how and why organisms are classified according to shared characteristics with emphasis on the Linnaean system combined with the concept of Domains.
  • SC.6.L.14.3: Recognize and explore how cells of all organisms undergo similar processes to maintain homeostasis, including extracting energy from food, getting rid of waste, and reproducing.
  • SC.6.L.14.6: Compare and contrast types of infectious agents that may infect the human body, including viruses, bacteria, fungi, and parasites.
  • SC.6.N.2.2: Explain that scientific knowledge is durable because it is open to change as new evidence or interpretations are encountered.
  • SC.6.N.1.1: Define a problem from the sixth grade curriculum, use appropriate reference materials to support scientific understanding, plan and carry out scientific investigation of various types, such as systematic observations or experiments, identify variables, collect and organize data, interpret data in charts, tables, and graphics, analyze information, make predictions, and defend conclusions.

Materials

  • The interactive lesson
  • Notebook, pencils, colored pencils, a centimeter ruler
  • A phone or camera; optional magnifying glass, binoculars and field guide
  • The Unit 3 project rubric
  • The Unit 3 checkpoint (in the lesson)

Vocabulary

  • field guide: A book that helps people identify living things in nature, with pictures, descriptions and keys.
  • naturalist: A person who studies plants and animals by observing them in nature.
  • prokaryote: A single-celled organism with no nucleus (bacteria and archaea).
  • eukaryote: An organism whose cells have a nucleus (protists, fungi, plants and animals).
  • community science: Science in which members of the public collect data, such as photographing plants and animals for researchers.

Key questions to ask

  • Is it alive? How do you know?
  • Where does it belong, and what evidence tells you?
  • Could someone else use your key?
  • Is it native or invasive?

Common mistakes to watch for

  • All microorganisms are germs.
  • Fungi are plants.
  • Classification never changes.
  • Ringworm is caused by a worm.

Support (if the student is struggling)

  • Allow 6 organisms instead of 8, with 4 observed in person.
  • Provide a field guide page template.
  • Let the student photograph instead of sketch.

Challenge (if the student is ready for more)

  • Upload observations to a community science app with a parent's permission.
  • Add a page comparing a native species with an invasive look-alike.

Background for the teacher

Mark Catesby (1683 to 1749) published The Natural History of Carolina, Florida and the Bahama Islands in parts from 1729 to 1747; he acknowledged help from Native Americans and enslaved Africans. Anolis carolinensis, Danaus plexippus, Serenoa repens, Trametes versicolor and Sabal palmetto are common Florida examples. Fungal cell walls contain chitin. Answer key: interactive lesson

Reconnect

  1. Antibiotics kill bacteria. What kind of pathogen do they not work against?
    Answer: viruses
    Viruses are not cells.
  2. How many micrometers are in 2 millimeters?
    Answer: 2000 µm
    2 × 1,000 = 2,000 µm.
  3. Which scientist's 1928 observation of a mold led to penicillin?
    Answer: Alexander Fleming
    The mold was Penicillium.
  4. What did your bread test show about hand-washing? Give your evidence in one or two sentences.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: A claim about hand-washing; Evidence from the student's own data (mold percents or squares compared with the control)
    Model answer: Hand-washing reduced mold: by the last day the unwashed slice was 75% covered and the washed slice about 21%, close to the control's 12.5%.

Learn 1: Connecting the Big Ideas of Unit 3

  1. In the scientific name Quercus virginiana (the southern live oak), which word is the species name?
    Answer: virginiana
    The genus comes first (Quercus); the species name second.
  2. Use the characteristics of life to explain why a virus is not placed in any kingdom.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Viruses are not made of cells and cannot use energy or reproduce on their own; Classification kingdoms are for living things
    Model answer: Kingdoms group living things, and viruses fail key characteristics of life: they are not cells and cannot use energy or reproduce on their own, only inside a host cell.
  3. Order the levels of classification from broadest to most specific.
    Answer: kingdom; class
    Domain, kingdom, phylum, class, order, family, genus, species.

Learn 2: Mid-Year Review: Cells and Classification

  1. Under a microscope, a cell has a nucleus, a cell wall and chloroplasts. Which kingdom does it most likely belong to?
    Answer: Plants
    Only plant (and some protist) cells have chloroplasts; together with a cell wall and nucleus, this points to a plant.
  2. Bacteria and archaea have no nucleus. What are organisms whose cells have no nucleus called?
    Answer: prokaryotes
    Pro = before; karyon = nucleus.
  3. For a long time, mushrooms were classified as plants. Using cell evidence, explain why scientists now place fungi in their own kingdom.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Fungi have no chloroplasts and cannot photosynthesize; they absorb food; Their cell walls are made of a different material (chitin) and DNA shows they are closer to animals (taught in Week 12)
    Model answer: Fungi cannot make their own food because they have no chloroplasts; they absorb food from what they grow on. DNA evidence also shows they are more closely related to animals than to plants, so scientists gave them their own kingdom.

Learn 3: Planning Your Field Guide

  1. How should a scientific name be written? Type the correct version: "Gopherus Polyphemus" or "Gopherus polyphemus".
    Answer: Gopherus polyphemus (genus capitalized, species lowercase, italicized when printed)
    Only the genus is capitalized; the species name is lowercase.
  2. Your field guide has 8 organisms. How many couplets will its dichotomous key need?
    Answer: 7 couplets
    One fewer couplet than organisms.
  3. For your field guide, why is "has a white stripe on each side of its body" a better identifying trait than "is cute"?
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: The stripe is observable and the same for every observer; "Cute" is an opinion and varies by person
    Model answer: Anyone can see a white stripe, so everyone will identify it the same way. "Cute" is an opinion, and people would disagree.

Hands-On

  1. List your organisms with common name, scientific name, kingdom, and one observation of each (where, when, what it was doing).
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: At least 5 organisms observed in person; Correct common and scientific names, written correctly; Correct kingdom for each; Specific, dated observations
    Model answer: 1. Green anole, Anolis carolinensis, Animalia: on the porch rail at 4 p.m., Nov 20, puffing out its pink throat fan. 2. Cabbage palm, Sabal palmetto, Plantae: by the driveway, fan leaves with a curved midrib. 3. Monarch butterfly, Danaus plexippus, Animalia: on milkweed, 10 a.m. 4. Saw palmetto, Serenoa repens, Plantae: along the fence. 5. Turkey tail fungus, Trametes versicolor, Fungi: on a fallen oak log.
  2. Write your draft dichotomous key, and describe one couplet you changed after testing it.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: One fewer couplet than organisms; True opposites with observable traits; Describes a revision based on the test
    Model answer: 1a. Moves on its own ........ go to 2 1b. Does not move on its own ........ go to 3 2a. Has wings ........ monarch butterfly 2b. No wings; has scales ........ green anole 3a. Grows from wood; no leaves ........ turkey tail fungus 3b. Has leaves ........ go to 4 4a. Leaf has a curved midrib ........ cabbage palm 4b. Leaf has no curved midrib ........ saw palmetto I changed couplet 1 from "is an animal / is not an animal" because my tester didn't know whether to call a fungus an animal.

Think Deeper

  1. Scientists once divided all life into two kingdoms, plants and animals. Today they use three domains and six kingdoms. Explain what evidence changed their minds and why changing a classification is a strength of science, not a weakness.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Evidence: microscopes revealed microorganisms; cell structure (nucleus or not); DNA and Woese's work on archaea; Science changes when new evidence appears; durable but open to change (SC.6.N.2.2); Clear, organized explanation
    Model answer: Microscopes revealed microorganisms that were neither plants nor animals, and studying cells showed that bacteria have no nucleus. Later, Carl Woese's study of genetic material showed that archaea are a separate domain. Scientists changed the system because the evidence demanded it. That is a strength: science is trustworthy because it corrects itself when better evidence appears, instead of holding onto old ideas.

Checkpoint: Part A: Characteristics of living things

  1. A tomato plant bends toward a sunny window. Which characteristic of life does this show?
    Answer: response to the environment (stimuli)
    Plants respond to light.
  2. Your body sweats on a hot day to keep its temperature steady. What is keeping a stable internal balance called?
    Answer: homeostasis
    Sweating cools the body.
  3. Fire grows, uses oxygen and can spread (reproduce). Explain why fire is still not alive, using at least two characteristics of life.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric (4 points toward the checkpoint).
    Rubric: Fire is not made of cells; It does not maintain homeostasis, have DNA, grow and develop in an organized way, or adapt over generations
    Model answer: Fire is not made of cells, which every living thing is. It also does not maintain homeostasis or pass traits to offspring that adapt over generations, so it is not alive.

Checkpoint: Part B: Classification and scientific names

  1. Which is the most specific level of classification?
    Answer: species
    Domain is broadest; species is most specific.
  2. In Puma concolor, which word is the genus?
    Answer: Puma
    Genus first, capitalized.
  3. Which domain includes all organisms whose cells have a nucleus?
    Answer: Eukarya
    Bacteria and Archaea are prokaryotes.
  4. Explain why scientists everywhere use one scientific name for each species.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric (3 points toward the checkpoint).
    Rubric: Common names differ by place and language; One worldwide name prevents confusion
    Model answer: Common names change from place to place and language to language, so one scientific name lets scientists everywhere know exactly which species they mean.

Checkpoint: Part C: Dichotomous keys

  1. Key to four Florida animals 1a. Has feathers ........ go to 2 1b. Has no feathers ........ go to 3 2a. Long legs; wades in water ........ great blue heron 2b. Short legs; perches in trees ........ northern cardinal 3a. Has a hard shell ........ gopher tortoise 3b. Has no shell; has fur ........ Florida panther Use the key. An animal has no feathers and has a hard shell. What is it?
    Answer: gopher tortoise
    1b → 3a.
  2. Use the same key. A bird with short legs is perched in an oak tree. What is it?
    Answer: northern cardinal
    1a → 2b.
  3. Explain what is wrong with this couplet and rewrite it: "1a. Animal is brown. / 1b. Animal is large."
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric (4 points toward the checkpoint).
    Rubric: Not opposites (an animal could be both or neither); color and "large" without a measurement are weak traits; A correct rewrite with opposite, observable traits
    Model answer: The statements are not opposites: an animal could be brown and large, or neither. Color and "large" are also unreliable. Better: "1a. Animal has four legs. / 1b. Animal does not have four legs."

Checkpoint: Part D: Microorganisms

  1. What type of microorganism has a single cell with no nucleus?
    Answer: bacteria (or archaea)
    Prokaryotes have no nucleus.
  2. What group of organisms breaks down dead plants and animals and returns nutrients to the soil?
    Answer: decomposers
    Many bacteria and fungi are decomposers.
  3. Match each disease to its type of pathogen.
    Answer: virus; bacteria; fungus
    Ringworm is a fungal skin infection, not a worm.
  4. Explain why doctors warn against taking antibiotics you do not need.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric (4 points toward the checkpoint).
    Rubric: Antibiotics don't work on viruses; Overuse lets surviving bacteria multiply, creating antibiotic-resistant bacteria
    Model answer: Antibiotics don't help viral illnesses like colds, and overusing them lets the few bacteria that survive multiply, creating resistant bacteria that are hard to treat.

Answer key: printable page

  1. List the seven characteristics of living things.
    Answer: Made of cells; use energy; grow and develop; respond to the environment; reproduce; maintain homeostasis; adapt over generations.
  2. Write the eight levels of classification in order and a memory sentence.
    Answer: Domain, kingdom, phylum, class, order, family, genus, species; Dear King Philip Came Over For Good Soup.
  3. Compare prokaryotes and eukaryotes and give an example of each.
    Answer: Prokaryotes have no nucleus (bacteria); eukaryotes have a nucleus (plants, animals, fungi, protists).
  4. Write a two-couplet key for a pine needle, an oak leaf and a palm frond.
    Answer: Model: 1a. Needle-shaped ... pine; 1b. Flat ... go to 2; 2a. Fan-shaped with folds ... palm; 2b. Not fan-shaped ... oak.
  5. Give two helpful and two harmful roles of microorganisms.
    Answer: Helpful: decomposing, oxygen, digestion, food, medicine. Harmful: strep throat, food poisoning, malaria, red tide.
  6. Why does classification sometimes change?
    Answer: New evidence (microscopes, cell structure, DNA) shows new relationships, and science updates its ideas.
