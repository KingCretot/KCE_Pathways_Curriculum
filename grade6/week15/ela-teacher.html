Unit 3: The Power of Poetry · Week 15 · ELA · Teacher Plan

Mid-Year Review and Poetry Analysis Project

Lesson length: 60 minutes live, plus the after-class interactive lesson

Objectives

  • Identify figurative language and sound devices and explain their effect.
  • Identify the forms studied in Unit 3 and explain how form shapes meaning.
  • Write a poetry analysis with a clear claim, quotations and explanation (T-E-E-C).
  • Recite a poem from memory with clear voice, pacing and expression.
  • Use the Greek and Latin roots from Unit 3.

Standards

  • ELA.6.R.1.4: Describe the impact of various poetic forms on meaning and style.
  • ELA.6.R.3.1: Explain how figurative language contributes to tone and meaning in text(s).
  • ELA.6.R.3.3: Compare and contrast how authors from different time periods address the same or related topics.
  • ELA.6.C.2.1: Present information orally, in a logical sequence, using nonverbal cues, appropriate volume, clear pronunciation, and appropriate pacing.
  • ELA.6.V.1.2: Apply knowledge of Greek and Latin roots and affixes to determine meanings of words and phrases in grade-level content.
  • ELA.K12.EE.1.1: Cite evidence to explain and justify reasoning.

Materials

  • The interactive lesson
  • The Unit 3 poems
  • Index cards, paper and pencil or a computer
  • A phone or tablet to record the recitation
  • The Unit 3 project rubric
  • The Unit 3 checkpoint (in the lesson)

Vocabulary

  • thesis (claim): The main point an analysis argues, in one or two sentences.
  • evidence: Words quoted from the poem that support the claim.
  • recitation: Speaking a poem from memory for an audience.
  • tone: The speaker's attitude, shown through word choice and sound.
  • rondeau: A French form with two rhymes and a short refrain taken from the opening words.

Key questions to ask

  • How does the poet create meaning, not just what does the poem say?
  • Which quotation best proves your point?
  • How does the form fit the feeling?
  • Where do the punctuation and meaning tell you to pause?

Common mistakes to watch for

  • Analysis means retelling the poem.
  • Every line ending needs a pause when reciting.
  • Long quotations are better evidence than short, precise ones.
  • Only one interpretation of a poem is correct (strong interpretations are supported by evidence).

Support (if the student is struggling)

  • Offer a thesis frame: "In ___, ___ uses ___ and ___ to show ___."
  • Allow 10 to 14 memorized lines if the chosen poem is longer.
  • Pre-highlight three possible quotations.

Challenge (if the student is ready for more)

  • Write the analysis on two poems in conversation.
  • Recite "We Wear the Mask" in addition to your chosen poem.

Background for the teacher

"We Wear the Mask" first appeared in Majors and Minors (1895) and is reprinted in Lyrics of Lowly Life (1896); the text here is from The Complete Poems of Paul Laurence Dunbar (1913; Project Gutenberg 18338). It is often described as a variation on the French rondeau: 15 lines, two rhymes, and a short refrain taken from the opening words. Poetry Out Loud is a national recitation contest for high school students run by the National Endowment for the Arts and the Poetry Foundation with state arts agencies. Checkpoint total: 57 points (Part A 18, Part B 15, Part C 10, Part D 7); score written items with their rubrics.

How the lesson runs

During the live lesson, the student shares their screen and answers the Reconnect questions and the Learn checks live while you teach. The Hands-On activity, Think Deeper and Exit Check are completed after class.

Scoring

The interactive lesson scores itself: full points on the first try, half points on the second try, then the answer and explanation appear. Mastery is 85. Written responses arrive in your results email with the score; score them with the rubric below before the student moves on.

Checkpoint scoring

Checkpoint questions are scored separately from the lesson. The student gets one try per question and the answers are not shown, so the checkpoint measures independent mastery and a retake stays fair. Your results email shows the auto-scored checkpoint points, a list of each item marked correct or MISSED, and the points available for the written items. Score the written items with their rubrics, then: checkpoint score = (auto points + rubric points) / total possible × 100. Mastery is 85. Below 85: teach a targeted review lesson on the parts missed (A to D), then the student retakes the checkpoint. Record both attempts in the Weekly Score Log.

Answer key: interactive lesson

Reconnect

  1. How many lines does a villanelle have?
    Answer: 19 lines
    Five tercets and a quatrain.
  2. What is the term for a brief reference to a well-known person, event or work?
    Answer: allusion
    From lud, play.
  3. Which American poet's "I Hear America Singing" does Hughes answer with "I, too, sing America"?
    Answer: Walt Whitman
    Whitman's 1860 poem lists America's workers.
  4. Why did Georgia Douglas Johnson call the cage's bars "sheltering"?
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: The limits on the woman's life also protect her, so she is safe but not free
    Model answer: The things that limit her also keep her safe, so she is protected and trapped at the same time.

Learn 1: Review: Figurative Language and Sound

  1. "The old truck coughed and groaned up the hill." Which figurative device is this?
    Answer: personification
    A truck cannot really cough or groan; it is given human actions.
  2. "Sizzle, crackle, pop went the bacon." Which sound device do sizzle, crackle and pop show?
    Answer: onomatopoeia
    The words imitate the sounds.
  3. "The classroom was a beehive before the bell." Simile or metaphor?
    Answer: metaphor
    It compares without like or as.
  4. Choose one device from the sentences above and explain its effect: what does it help a reader see, hear or feel?
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Names a device correctly; Explains the effect, not just the name
    Model answer: The beehive metaphor makes me picture a classroom full of buzzing, busy students moving everywhere, so I feel the noise and energy before the bell.

Learn 2: Review: How Form Shapes Meaning

  1. A poem has 14 lines, three quatrains and a final rhyming couplet. What form is it?
    Answer: a sonnet
    abab cdcd efef gg.
  2. Mark the rhyme scheme of these lines. Type the four letters with no spaces. "The rain came down on the gravel road, / The wagon creaked beneath its load, / We hurried home before the night / Could swallow up the last of light."
    Answer: aabb
    road/load (a), night/light (b).
  3. Choose one poem from Unit 3 and explain how its form fits its meaning.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Names a poem and its form correctly; Explains a clear link between form and meaning with a detail from the poem
    Model answer: "The Tired Worker" is a sonnet. Its strict 14 lines feel like the worker's fixed daily routine, and the turn at line 11, "But what steals out the gray clouds red like wine?", is the moment dawn arrives and his short rest ends.

Learn 3: Writing a Poetry Analysis

  1. Write a thesis (one or two sentences) for an analysis of a poem you might choose for your project. Name the poem, the poet and two techniques.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Names a Unit 3 poem and poet; Makes a claim about meaning; Names two techniques (devices or form)
    Model answer: In "The Negro Speaks of Rivers," Langston Hughes uses a collective speaker and a repeated refrain to show that Black history is ancient, deep and unbroken.
  2. When you quote two lines of a poem inside a sentence, what mark shows where one line ends and the next begins? (Type the name of the mark.)
    Answer: a slash ( / )
    Example: "I've known rivers: / Ancient, dusky rivers."
  3. A student writes: "In the poem, the worker is tired, and then the sun comes up, and he has to go back to the city." Why is this a retelling instead of analysis? Rewrite it as analysis.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Explains it only summarizes what happens, without explaining how or why; A rewrite that names a technique and its effect, with a quotation
    Model answer: It only tells what happens. Analysis explains how the poet creates meaning. Rewrite: McKay uses the volta at line 11, "But what steals out the gray clouds red like wine?", to show the worker's hope for rest being crushed as dawn arrives too soon.

Learn 4: Reciting a Poem

  1. In "Epilogue," where would you pause and slow down when reciting "Tomorrow, / I'll sit at the table / When company comes"? Explain why.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: A sensible pause (after "Tomorrow," at the comma); Connects the pause to meaning (it marks the turn to hope or confidence about the future)
    Model answer: I would pause after "Tomorrow," because the comma and the word itself mark the turn from how things are now to the future Hughes is sure will come. The pause makes that hope stand out.
  2. When reciting, should you pause at the end of every line, or where the punctuation and meaning tell you to? Type line or punctuation.
    Answer: where the punctuation and meaning tell you to
    Many lines run on into the next without a pause.

Hands-On

  1. Paste or type your full analysis (400 to 600 words) with your MLA citation.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Clear thesis about how the poem creates meaning; Three body paragraphs with accurate quotations; Explains effects (not retelling); Form and at least two devices analyzed; Conclusion and correct MLA citation
    Model answer: (Model opening) Langston Hughes was barely an adult when he wrote "The Negro Speaks of Rivers," yet the poem's speaker sounds thousands of years old. In this free-verse poem, Hughes uses a collective speaker, parallel structure and a repeated refrain to show that Black history is ancient, deep and unbroken. ... Works Cited: Hughes, Langston. "The Negro Speaks of Rivers." The Weary Blues, Alfred A. Knopf, 1926, p. 51.
  2. After recording, describe one thing you improved between your first and final recitation, and one thing you would still work on.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: A specific improvement (volume, pacing, pauses, eye contact, accuracy); A specific next goal
    Model answer: In my first recording I rushed and paused at the end of every line. In the final one I paused only at punctuation, which made the refrain sound stronger. I would still work on looking up at my audience instead of at the floor.

Think Deeper

  1. Which poem from Unit 3 affected you most, and why? Use one quotation and name one technique the poet used to create that effect.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Names a poem and poet; Explains a personal response; An accurate quotation; Names a technique and connects it to the effect
    Model answer: "The House on the Hill" affected me most. The refrain "They are all gone away" kept coming back, and by the end it felt heavy, the way missing someone keeps returning to your mind. Robinson used the villanelle's repeating refrains to make me feel that loss instead of just reading about it.

Checkpoint: Part A: Read a new poem

  1. We Wear the Mask Paul Laurence Dunbar (1895; text from The Complete Poems of Paul Laurence Dunbar , 1913) We wear the mask that grins and lies, It hides our cheeks and shades our eyes,— This debt we pay to human guile; With torn and bleeding hearts we smile, And mouth with myriad subtleties.   Why should the world be over-wise, In counting all our tears and sighs? Nay, let them only see us, while     We wear the mask.   We smile, but, O great Christ, our cries To thee from tortured souls arise. We sing, but oh the clay is vile Beneath our feet, and long the mile; But let the world dream otherwise,     We wear the mask! guile: trickery. myriad: countless. subtleties: small, clever details. over-wise: too knowing. nay: no. vile: disgusting, foul. What does the mask stand for in this poem? Explain the metaphor.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric (4 points toward the checkpoint).
    Rubric: The mask is a metaphor for hiding true pain behind a smiling or calm face; Connects to the people speaking ("we"): suffering they keep from the world
    Model answer: The mask is a metaphor for the smiling face people show the world to hide their real pain. The speakers, a "we" who suffer, grin and smile while their hearts are "torn and bleeding," so no one sees how they really feel.
  2. Type the refrain of the poem, the short line that repeats.
    Answer: We wear the mask
    It closes stanzas 2 and 3.
  3. The poem uses only two rhyme sounds. One is the sound in lies, eyes and wise. Type a word from the poem that rhymes with smile.
    Answer: guile, while, vile or mile
    guile, smile, while, vile, mile.
  4. The refrain ends with a period in stanza 2 and an exclamation point in stanza 3. How does that change the tone at the end of the poem?
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric (4 points toward the checkpoint).
    Rubric: Notices the shift from period to exclamation point; Explains a tone change (from quiet acceptance to stronger emotion: defiance, anguish or insistence)
    Model answer: With a period, the refrain sounds quiet and resigned. With an exclamation point at the end, it sounds forceful, almost a cry, as if the speakers are insisting, with pain and pride, that they will keep hiding their suffering.
  5. Dunbar also wrote "Sympathy," with its caged bird (Week 11). Name one way "We Wear the Mask" and "Sympathy" are in conversation.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric (4 points toward the checkpoint).
    Rubric: A connection: both show hidden suffering; the bird's song and the mask's smile both hide pain; both speak for people whose freedom is limited
    Model answer: Both poems show suffering hidden behind something that looks pleasant: the caged bird's song is really a prayer, and the mask's grin hides torn and bleeding hearts.

Checkpoint: Part B: Devices and forms

  1. "The lighthouse winked at the passing ships." Name the device.
    Answer: personification
    A lighthouse cannot wink.
  2. "Her laugh was as bright as a brass bell." Simile or metaphor?
    Answer: simile
    It uses as.
  3. "Silver stars slid slowly south." Which sound device repeats the s sound at the start of words?
    Answer: alliteration
    Repeated beginning consonant sounds.
  4. What is the name of a three-line Japanese poem with 17 sound units?
    Answer: haiku
    5-7-5 in Japanese.
  5. What is the name for the turn in a sonnet?
    Answer: volta
    Italian for turn.

Checkpoint: Part C: Poems and their times

  1. What was the name of the movement of about 6 million Black Southerners to cities in the North and West between about 1910 and 1970?
    Answer: the Great Migration
    It helped make the Harlem Renaissance possible.
  2. "The Negro Speaks of Rivers" has no regular rhyme scheme or meter. What kind of poetry is it?
    Answer: free verse
    It is held together by repetition and parallel structure.
  3. Explain how one poem from Unit 3 answers or echoes an earlier poem or text. Name both and give one specific detail.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric (4 points toward the checkpoint).
    Rubric: Names a correct pair (Hughes/Whitman; Johnson/Dunbar; Fauset/Truth; any well-supported pair); A specific, accurate detail showing the connection
    Model answer: Hughes's "Epilogue" answers Whitman's "I Hear America Singing." Whitman lists many singing Americans but leaves out Black Americans, so Hughes begins "I, too, sing America" and ends "I, too, am America."

Checkpoint: Part D: Roots from Unit 3

  1. Write the meaning of each root.
    Answer: sound; free; turn; move
    phon = sound; liber = free; vers = turn; migr = move.
  2. Sym means together and path means feeling. What word means feeling together with someone in their sorrow?
    Answer: sympathy
    sym + path: Dunbar's poem title.

Answer key: printable page

  1. Define simile, metaphor, personification and symbol, with one example of each.
    Answer: Model: simile, a comparison using like or as ("like a flower"); metaphor, a comparison without like or as ("the classroom was a beehive"); personification, human traits for non-human things ("the truck coughed"); symbol, a concrete thing standing for an idea (stars for hope).
  2. Describe the sonnet and the villanelle. Name a Unit 3 poem in each form.
    Answer: Sonnet: 14 lines, abab cdcd efef gg, volta (Sonnet 18 or "The Tired Worker"). Villanelle: 19 lines, two refrains, two rhymes ("The House on the Hill").
  3. Write a thesis for an analysis of any Unit 3 poem.
    Answer: Model: In "Oriflamme," Jessie Fauset uses an epigraph and parallel stanzas to show that hope passes from one generation to the next.
  4. Explain the difference between retelling and analysis.
    Answer: Retelling says what happens; analysis explains how the poet creates meaning and why it matters.
  5. Give the meaning of each root and one word using it: metr, voc, dia, nat.
    Answer: metr = measure (meter); voc = voice, call (vocal); dia = across, through (dialogue); nat = born (native).
  6. Name two ways to memorize a poem.
    Answer: Learn one stanza at a time; say it aloud while walking; write it from memory; use first-word cue cards.
