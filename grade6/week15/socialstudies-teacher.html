Unit 3: Ancient Asia · Week 15 · World and American History · Teacher Plan

Mid-Year Review and Ancient Asia Project

Lesson length: 60 minutes live, plus the after-class interactive lesson

Objectives

  • Compare the economic, political, social and religious features of Kemet, Mesopotamia, the Indus Valley and ancient China.
  • Compare early writing systems and explain what each lets historians know.
  • Explain how the Silk Roads and sea roads connected Asia, East Africa and the Mediterranean.
  • Separate historical fact from fiction and cite evidence in a creative project.
  • Show mastery of Unit 3 on the checkpoint.

Standards

  • SS.6.W.2.4: Compare the economic, political, social, and religious institutions of ancient river civilizations.
  • SS.6.W.4.10: Explain the significance of the silk roads and maritime routes across the Indian Ocean to the movement of goods and ideas among Asia, East Africa, and the Mediterranean Basin.
  • SS.6.W.1.3: Interpret primary and secondary sources.
  • SS.6.W.1.6: Describe how history transmits culture and heritage and provides models of human character.
  • SS.6.G.2.6: Explain the concept of cultural diffusion, and identify the influences of different ancient cultures on one another.

Materials

  • The interactive lesson
  • Large paper, colored pencils, a world map or globe, index cards
  • Optional: string and tape
  • The Unit 3 project rubric
  • The Unit 3 checkpoint (in the lesson)

Vocabulary

  • civilization: A complex society with cities, government, specialized jobs, social classes, religion and often writing.
  • historical fiction: A made-up story set in a real time and place, built on accurate historical facts.
  • primary source: A source created at the time being studied, such as an inscription or a merchant's guidebook.
  • secondary source: A later source that explains or interprets the past, such as a textbook or museum website.
  • anachronism: Something placed in the wrong time period, such as a compass in Hammurabi's Babylon.

Key questions to ask

  • What did these civilizations share, and what made each unique?
  • What can writing tell us, and what can objects tell us?
  • Is this detail true to 100 CE?
  • Which sources prove it?

Common mistakes to watch for

  • All ancient civilizations were ruled the same way.
  • The Indus script has been deciphered.
  • Historical fiction can change the facts.
  • Africa was cut off from Asian trade.

Support (if the student is struggling)

  • Provide a journal entry frame: date and place, what I saw, what was traded, a danger, what I learned.
  • Allow four entries instead of five.
  • Give a list of approved sources.

Challenge (if the student is ready for more)

  • Write one entry from the point of view of a second traveler on the sea route.
  • Add a short glossary of terms your traveler would know.

Background for the teacher

Approximate starting dates in the comparison chart: unified Kemet about 3100 BCE; Mesopotamian cities such as Uruk about 3500 BCE; mature Harappan cities about 2600 BCE; Shang dynasty about 1600 BCE. The latest known datable cuneiform tablet is an astronomical almanac from Uruk dated 79/80 CE (Babylon's latest texts date to about 75 CE), so a traveler in 100 CE might hear of the old writing but not see it in daily use. Magnetic compasses for navigation are documented in China in the 11th century CE; paper money appeared in China in the 11th century (Song). Han wuzhu coins had square holes. Checkpoint total: 39 points (Part A 10, Part B 9, Part C 10, Part D 10; the Part D source item is included); score written items with their rubrics.

Answer key: interactive lesson

Reconnect

  1. Which Han officer's journeys west, beginning about 138 BCE, helped open the Silk Roads?
    Answer: Zhang Qian
    His reports led to Han missions and trade.
  2. What seasonal winds carried ships across the Indian Ocean?
    Answer: the monsoon
    Southwest in summer, northeast in winter.
  3. Which religion spread from India to China along the Silk Roads?
    Answer: Buddhism
    The Mogao Caves at Dunhuang are evidence.
  4. What does the Periplus tell us about Aksum's place in world trade?
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Ivory came to Aksum and was shipped from Adulis; Aksum traded widely; its ruler knew Greek literature
    Model answer: Ivory from beyond the Nile came to Aksum and was shipped from Adulis, and its ruler knew Greek literature, so Aksum was a major, connected trading power.

Learn 1: Comparing River Civilizations

  1. Use the comparison chart.
    Answer: Indus Valley; ancient China
    The Indus script is unread; the Zhou introduced the Mandate of Heaven.
  2. Choose two of the four civilizations. Give one similarity and one difference in how they were governed.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: An accurate similarity (rulers with great power, written records, officials); An accurate difference (divine pharaoh vs. Mandate that could be lost; law code vs. unknown rulers)
    Model answer: Kemet and China both had powerful rulers who claimed support from the gods or Heaven. But the pharaoh was seen as divine himself, while a Chinese king could lose the Mandate of Heaven if he ruled badly.
  3. Read the Analects passage. According to Confucius, what is the weakness of ruling only by laws and punishments, and what works better?
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Laws and punishments make people avoid punishment without feeling shame; leading by virtue and proper behavior makes people good
    Model answer: Confucius says people ruled only by laws and punishments just try not to get caught and feel no shame. If rulers lead by virtue and good manners, people feel shame when they do wrong and become good.
  4. Which king of Babylon set up a stone pillar with 282 laws around 1754 BCE?
    Answer: Hammurabi
    Week 11.
  5. Why did all four civilizations begin on river floodplains? Explain using geography.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Rivers brought water and fertile soil (silt or loess) for farming; Food surpluses allowed cities, specialized jobs and trade; rivers were also routes
    Model answer: River floods left fertile soil and provided water for crops, so farmers grew extra food. That surplus let people live in cities and take other jobs, and the rivers were highways for trade.

Learn 2: Writing and Records Across Africa and Asia

  1. What object helped scholars decipher Egyptian hieroglyphs in 1822, because it carried the same text in three scripts?
    Answer: the Rosetta Stone
    It included Greek, which scholars could read.
  2. Which writing system was pressed into clay with a reed, making wedge-shaped marks?
    Answer: cuneiform
    Cuneus is Latin for wedge.
  3. Chinese characters have been used for over 3,000 years, but cuneiform and the Indus script are no longer used. How does that difference affect what historians can learn about each civilization?
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Continuous use means ancient Chinese texts can be read and linked to later records; Cuneiform had to be deciphered; the Indus script cannot be read, so historians rely on archaeology for the Indus Valley
    Model answer: Because Chinese writing never stopped being used, scholars can read oracle bones and trace ideas across 3,000 years. Cuneiform had to be deciphered before it could be read, and the Indus script still cannot be read at all, so for the Indus Valley historians depend almost entirely on objects and city ruins.

Learn 3: Planning Your Ancient Asia Project: A Silk Roads Travel Journal

  1. What is the term for a detail placed in the wrong time period, such as a magnetic compass on a Silk Roads caravan in 100 CE?
    Answer: anachronism
    Ana (against) + chron (time).
  2. A student's journal says, "In Samarkand I bought silk, then paid for it with paper money." Which part is a historical error, and how would you fix it?
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Paper money is the anachronism (it came centuries later); A fix: paid with silver or bronze coins, or traded goods
    Model answer: Paper money did not exist in 100 CE, so that is an anachronism. The traveler should pay with silver or bronze coins or trade goods like horses or cloth.
  3. Is the Periplus of the Erythraean Sea a primary source or a secondary source for first-century trade?
    Answer: a primary source
    A merchant wrote it at the time.

Hands-On

  1. Describe your traveler, route and the six stops on your map. For each stop, write one real fact you will use.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: A believable traveler for 100 CE; A route with at least six real stops in order; An accurate fact for each stop
    Model answer: Traveler: Nanai, 14, apprentice to a Sogdian merchant, around 100 CE. Route: Chang'an → Dunhuang (oasis at the edge of the Taklamakan) → Kashgar (market below the Pamirs) → Samarkand (Sogdian home city) → Merv (Parthian city) → near the ruins of Babylon (old Mesopotamian capital, where cuneiform tablets were still being written in temples until about 75 CE). Facts: Chang'an was the Han capital; Dunhuang's oasis gave caravans water; the Pamir passes west of Kashgar are among the highest on the route.
  2. Paste your best journal entry and its Historian's Note line explaining which details are real.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: A vivid first-person entry set in 100 CE; At least three accurate historical details; No anachronisms; A Historian's Note separating fact from fiction
    Model answer: Entry 3, Kashgar, late spring. Our camels groaned as we climbed toward the Pamirs. Master Wakhshu bargained for jade at the market while I counted bronze coins with square holes from Chang'an. A monk from Gandhara told me of a teacher called the Buddha... Historian's Note: Kashgar's market, jade trading, Han square-holed coins and Buddhist monks traveling east are real; Nanai and Wakhshu are invented.

Think Deeper

  1. Which achievement from the civilizations of Unit 3 (Mesopotamia, the Indus Valley, China, or the Silk Roads) has most shaped the world you live in today? Support your choice with evidence and compare it with one other achievement.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: A clear choice; Accurate evidence about the achievement and its legacy; A comparison with another achievement; Logical reasoning
    Model answer: Writing, which began in Mesopotamia with cuneiform, has shaped my world the most. Without writing there would be no laws, history books or science, and Hammurabi's code shows how writing let a whole society share the same rules. Paper from Han China comes close, because it made writing cheap and spread across the Silk Roads, but paper only mattered because writing already existed.

Checkpoint: Part A: Mesopotamia

  1. What was the name of the stepped temple tower built at the center of Sumerian cities such as Ur?
    Answer: a ziggurat
    Week 11.
  2. Who was the priestess of Ur, often called the world's first known author by name?
    Answer: Enheduanna
    Week 11. Scholars still debate how many of the hymns credited to her she wrote herself.
  3. Hammurabi had his 282 laws carved on a tall stone pillar and set up where people could see them. Why would a king want laws written down and displayed in public? Give two reasons.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric (4 points toward the checkpoint).
    Rubric: Everyone (and every judge) could know the same rules; Shows the king's power and claim to justice, or makes the laws harder to change or ignore
    Model answer: Written laws in a public place let everyone, including judges, know the same rules, so punishments did not depend only on one person's mood. The pillar also showed Hammurabi's power and his claim to be a just king.

Checkpoint: Part B: The Indus Valley

  1. Indus weights doubled in size: each weight in the series was twice the one before it. If the smallest weight is 1 unit, how many units is the fifth weight?
    Answer: 16 units
    1, 2, 4, 8, 16.
  2. Indus bricks were made in the same proportions (about 1 : 2 : 4) in cities hundreds of miles apart, and weights followed one system. What do these standards suggest about how the Indus Valley was organized? Give two ideas.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric (3 points toward the checkpoint).
    Rubric: Shared rules or a shared authority across many cities (planners, councils or rulers); Organized trade or cooperation: standard weights let traders measure fairly; skilled planners and builders
    Model answer: Matching bricks in faraway cities suggest the cities followed shared rules set by some kind of authority, even though we do not know who ruled. Standard weights suggest organized trade, so buyers and sellers everywhere could measure goods fairly.
  3. After about 1900 BCE, people slowly left the large Indus cities. Where did most of them go?
    Answer: to smaller villages
    Archaeologists find people moving to smaller villages, not signs of conquest (Week 12).

Checkpoint: Part C: Ancient China

  1. Under the Mandate of Heaven, what kind of event could people take as a sign that a dynasty had lost Heaven's support? Name one.
    Answer: floods, famine or rebellions
    Week 13: floods, famines and rebellions were seen as signs that a dynasty had lost the Mandate.
  2. Match each teaching to its thinker: Confucius, Laozi or Han Feizi.
    Answer: Han Feizi; Confucius; Laozi
    Legalism, Confucianism, Daoism.
  3. Name one achievement and one cost of Qin Shi Huang's rule.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric (4 points toward the checkpoint).
    Rubric: Achievement: unification, standardized writing, money, weights, axles, early Great Wall, roads; Cost: forced labor, harsh punishments, book burning, short dynasty
    Model answer: He united China and standardized writing and money, but hundreds of thousands of people were forced to labor on his projects.

Checkpoint: Part D: The Silk Roads and sea roads

  1. Silk passed from Chinese to Sogdian to Parthian merchants before reaching Rome. What is this kind of trade called?
    Answer: relay trade
    Each merchant added costs and profit.
  2. What term means the spread of ideas, beliefs and inventions from one culture to another?
    Answer: cultural diffusion
    Buddhism spreading to China is an example.
  3. Primary source: Periplus of the Erythraean Sea (first century CE; translated by W. H. Schoff, 1912) "...there is Adulis, a port established by law, lying at the inner end of a bay that runs in toward the south." A port "established by law" was officially recognized for trade. What does this suggest about Aksum's government and its place in Red Sea trade? Use one detail from Week 9 or Week 14.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric (4 points toward the checkpoint).
    Rubric: Suggests an organized government that controlled and regulated trade; Uses a detail: Aksum's coins, ivory exports, trade with Rome, India and Arabia, or Zoscales knowing Greek
    Model answer: It suggests Aksum had an organized government that officially controlled trade at its port. That fits what we learned in Week 14: ivory from beyond the Nile was brought to Aksum and shipped from Adulis, and the ruler Zoscales knew Greek literature, so Aksum's leaders ran a port that traded with Rome, India and Arabia.

Answer key: printable page

  1. Name the river or rivers and one achievement of Kemet, Mesopotamia, the Indus Valley and ancient China.
    Answer: Nile: pyramids; Tigris and Euphrates: cuneiform, law code; Indus: planned cities and drains; Yellow and Yangtze: bronze, oracle bones, Confucian ideas.
  2. Compare cuneiform and the Indus script.
    Answer: Cuneiform: wedge marks in clay, deciphered; Indus script: short inscriptions on seals, still unread.
  3. Explain the Mandate of Heaven and the dynastic cycle.
    Answer: Heaven gives a just ruler the right to rule and takes it away from an unjust one; dynasties rise, prosper, decline, face rebellion and are replaced.
  4. How did relay trade and the monsoon shape ancient trade?
    Answer: Goods passed through many merchants, raising prices; monsoon winds let ships cross the Indian Ocean each season.
  5. What is an anachronism? Give an example for 100 CE.
    Answer: A detail in the wrong time period; for example, a magnetic compass or paper money on the Silk Roads in 100 CE.
  6. Give one example of cultural diffusion and its evidence.
    Answer: Buddhism spread from India to China; the Mogao Caves at Dunhuang.
