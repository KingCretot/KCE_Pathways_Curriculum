Unit 5: Ecosystems · Week 24 · Life Science · Teacher Plan

How Organisms Interact

Lesson length: 60 minutes live, plus the after-class interactive lesson

Objectives

  • Describe and give examples of predation, competition, mutualism, commensalism and parasitism.
  • Classify relationships by who benefits, who is harmed and who is unaffected.
  • Interpret a predator–prey graph and explain why the populations cycle.
  • Explain how limiting factors and competition, including from invasive species, affect populations.
  • Model a predator–prey relationship and analyze the data.

Standards

  • SC.7.L.17.2: Compare and contrast the relationships among organisms such as mutualism, predation, parasitism, competition, and commensalism.
  • SC.7.L.17.3: Describe and investigate various limiting factors in the local ecosystem and their impact on native populations, including food, shelter, water, space, disease, parasitism, predation, and nesting sites.
  • SC.6.N.3.4: Identify the role of models in the context of the sixth grade science benchmarks.
  • NGSS MS-LS2-2: Construct an explanation that predicts patterns of interactions among organisms across multiple ecosystems.

Materials

  • The interactive lesson
  • About 200 small and 40 larger paper squares in two colors, tape, paper or graph paper, colored pencils
  • Printable practice page

Vocabulary

  • predation: A relationship in which one organism (the predator) hunts and eats another (the prey).
  • competition: A relationship in which organisms struggle for the same limited resource, such as food, water, space or light.
  • symbiosis: A close, long-term relationship between two different species.
  • mutualism: A symbiosis in which both species benefit.
  • commensalism: A symbiosis in which one species benefits and the other is neither helped nor harmed.
  • parasitism: A symbiosis in which one species (the parasite) benefits and the other (the host) is harmed.
  • limiting factor: Anything that limits the size of a population, such as food, space, water or predators.
  • carrying capacity: The largest population an environment can support over time.

Key questions to ask

  • Who benefits, who is harmed, who is unaffected?
  • What limits this population?
  • Why does the predator peak come later?
  • How do people change these relationships?

Common mistakes to watch for

  • Predators wipe out their prey.
  • Symbiosis always means both species benefit.
  • Competition always involves fighting.
  • Parasites usually kill their hosts quickly (most don't; a dead host is bad for a parasite).

Support (if the student is struggling)

  • Use a +, −, 0 chart to classify relationships.
  • Act out the cleaning station and the tick on a deer.
  • Provide a partially filled data table for the simulation.

Challenge (if the student is ready for more)

  • Research the Isle Royale wolves and moose and compare their graph with the lynx and hares.
  • Design an experiment to test whether remoras help or harm sharks.

Background for the teacher

The lynx–hare cycle comes from Hudson's Bay Company fur records (roughly 1845 to 1935), analyzed by Charles Elton and others; trapping effort affects pelt counts, so the data reflect populations only approximately. Hare cycles are driven by both predation and food supply. The green and brown anole study is Stuart and colleagues (2014, Science): on islands in Mosquito Lagoon, green anoles perched higher within months of brown anole introduction, and toe pads enlarged within about 15 years (about 20 generations). Wolves were reintroduced to Yellowstone in 1995 and 1996; the extent of the resulting ripple effects is still debated by ecologists. Florida places these benchmarks in Grade 7 (SC.7.L.17.2 and 17.3); KCE teaches them here per the Grade 6 scope.

Sources

  • Stuart, Y. E., and others, "Rapid evolution of a native species following invasion by a congener," Science 346 (2014).
  • Elton, C., and M. Nicholson, "The ten-year cycle in numbers of the lynx in Canada," Journal of Animal Ecology (1942).
  • NOAA, Coral Reef Conservation Program: What is coral bleaching?
  • National Park Service, Yellowstone: Wolf Restoration.

Answer key: interactive lesson

Reconnect

  1. Which process do plants use to take carbon dioxide out of the air?
    Answer: photosynthesis
    Producers start every food web.
  2. What is the name of the underground limestone layer that feeds Florida's springs?
    Answer: the Floridan aquifer
    Spring ecosystems depend on it.
  3. When a predator eats its prey, does energy cycle back or flow on and get lost as heat?
    Answer: it flows on, and most is lost as heat
    Matter cycles; energy flows.
  4. A lynx eats a snowshoe hare. Trace one carbon atom from a willow twig, through the hare and the lynx, and back into the air.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Willow (photosynthesis) to hare (eating) to lynx (eating); Returns to the air through respiration or decomposition
    Model answer: The willow made sugar from carbon dioxide; the hare ate the twig, the lynx ate the hare, and the lynx breathed the carbon out as carbon dioxide (or decomposers released it after the lynx died).

Learn 1: Predation and the Predator–Prey Cycle

  1. In the graph, which population peaks first, the hare or the lynx?
    Answer: the hare
    Predators increase after their food supply does.
  2. If hare populations peaked in 1886, 1896 and 1906, in about what year would you predict the next peak?
    Answer: 1916
    The cycle is about 10 years.
  3. Explain why the lynx population peaks a year or two after the hare population, not at the same time.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Lynx need time to reproduce and raise young after food becomes plentiful; By the time lynx numbers peak, hares are already declining
    Model answer: When hares become plentiful, the lynx have more food, but it takes time for them to have kittens and for those kittens to grow. By the time the lynx population peaks, the hares are already declining because there are so many predators and less food.

Learn 2: Competition and Limiting Factors

  1. Two species of birds eat the same seeds in a park. What type of relationship is this?
    Answer: competition
    Both want the same limited resource.
  2. Explain how competition with brown anoles changed where green anoles live and how their bodies changed. Why is this considered evidence of evolution?
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Green anoles moved higher in trees; Toe pads got larger and stickier over generations; Evolution: the population's traits changed over generations because those traits helped survival
    Model answer: Brown anoles took over the low perches, so green anoles moved higher into the trees. Over about 20 generations, green anoles with bigger, stickier toe pads survived better up high, so the population's toe pads got larger. A change in a population's traits over generations is evolution.
  3. Think of a population you can observe at home or nearby (squirrels in your yard, ants by your door, fish in a tank, plants in a garden bed). Name two limiting factors for it and predict what would happen if one were removed.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: A real local population; Two reasonable limiting factors; A prediction (grows until another factor limits it)
    Model answer: The squirrels in our yard are limited by how many acorns the oaks drop and by the hawks that hunt them. If the hawks disappeared, the squirrel population would grow until food or nesting space became the new limit.

Learn 3: Symbiosis: Mutualism, Commensalism and Parasitism

  1. Classify each relationship.
    Answer: commensalism; parasitism; mutualism
    +/0; +/−; +/+.
  2. When ocean water gets too warm, corals expel the algae living inside them and turn white, which is called bleaching. Use the idea of mutualism to explain why bleached coral may starve and die.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: The algae provided food to the coral by photosynthesis; Without the partner, the coral loses most of its food (the mutualism breaks down)
    Model answer: Coral and algae are partners in mutualism: the algae make food by photosynthesis and share it with the coral. When the coral expels the algae, it loses most of its food supply, so if the water stays too warm for too long, the coral can starve and die.
  3. Explain why the remora and shark relationship is hard to classify. What evidence would a scientist need to decide?
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Remora clearly benefits; the question is whether the shark is helped, harmed or unaffected; Evidence: whether remoras remove parasites, slow the shark down or have no effect, measured with observations or data
    Model answer: The remora clearly benefits from free rides and scraps, but it's unclear whether the shark gains anything. A scientist would need evidence such as observations of remoras eating the shark's parasites, or measurements of whether sharks with remoras swim more slowly or stay healthier.

Learn 4: People and Relationships in Ecosystems

  1. Explain the relationship between Florida citrus growers and honeybees. What type is it, and what would happen to orange harvests if local bee populations crashed?
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Mutualism: bees get nectar and pollen (and hives); growers get pollination and fruit; Fewer bees means fewer flowers pollinated, so fewer oranges
    Model answer: It's mutualism: the bees get nectar, pollen and safe hives, and the growers get their trees pollinated. If bee populations crashed, many orange blossoms wouldn't be pollinated, so trees would produce fewer oranges.

Hands-On

  1. Describe your graph. Did the hare and lynx populations rise and fall? Did the lynx peaks come after the hare peaks? Give numbers from your data.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Describes the pattern with numbers; Compares the timing of the peaks; Honest about anything that didn't match the real cycle
    Model answer: Hares grew from 3 to 48 by generation 6, while lynx stayed at 1 or 2. Then lynx jumped to 7 in generation 8, catching so many hares that hares dropped to 9 by generation 10. Lynx crashed to 1 in generation 11, and hares started climbing again. The lynx peak came two generations after the hare peak, like the real data.
  2. Name two ways this model is different from a real Yukon forest. How might adding one real factor (like limited winter plants for hares) change your results?
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Two real differences (no plant food limit, no other predators or prey, random tossing, no weather or disease, small area); A reasonable prediction about adding a factor
    Model answer: Real lynx search with their senses instead of landing randomly, and real hares are limited by winter food, not just by lynx. If I limited hares to 60 because of winter plants, hare peaks would be lower and the crash would come sooner, even before the lynx numbers rose much.

Think Deeper

  1. In 1995 and 1996, wolves were brought back to Yellowstone National Park after being missing for about 70 years. Elk numbers dropped and elk stopped grazing so heavily along rivers. Predict two ripple effects on other organisms, using at least two relationship types from this week.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Two plausible ripple effects; At least two relationship types named and used correctly (predation, competition, mutualism, commensalism); Logical chain of cause and effect
    Model answer: Wolves are predators of elk, so with fewer elk browsing, willows and aspens along rivers could regrow. Beavers, which compete with elk for willows, would have more food and build more dams, creating ponds for fish and frogs. Ravens and eagles feed on scraps from wolf kills, a commensal relationship, so their food supply would grow too.
  2. Explain why invasive species like the brown anole or the Burmese python can harm native species so much, using what you learned about relationships, limiting factors and evolution.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Native species didn't evolve defenses or strategies against the newcomer; Invasives may lack their own predators or competitors (no limiting factors), so populations explode; They compete with or prey on natives, reducing native populations
    Model answer: Native species never evolved alongside the invader, so they have no defenses against a new predator or strategies to compete with it. The invader often has no predators or diseases in its new home, so few limiting factors keep its population down. It can quickly outcompete or eat native species, as brown anoles pushed green anoles up the trees and pythons cut mammal numbers in the Everglades.

Exit Check

  1. A relationship in which both species benefit is called ___.
    Answer: mutualism
    Like the goby and the grouper.
  2. Heartworms living in a dog: which type of symbiosis?
    Answer: parasitism
    The worm benefits; the dog is harmed.
  3. The largest population an environment can support over time is its ___.
    Answer: carrying capacity
    Limiting factors set it.
  4. In one or two sentences, explain why predator and prey populations rise and fall in cycles.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: More prey lets predators increase; more predators reduce prey; then predators decline, and prey recover
    Model answer: When prey are plentiful, predators increase and eat more prey, so prey decline; then predators starve and decline, letting prey recover.

Answer key: printable page

  1. Define and give one example of each: predation, competition, mutualism, commensalism, parasitism.
    Answer: Lynx and hare; two birds for seeds; goby and grouper; Spanish moss on oak; tick on deer.
  2. Why does the lynx population peak after the hare population?
    Answer: Lynx need time to reproduce after food increases; by their peak, hares are already declining.
  3. How did brown anoles affect green anoles in Florida?
    Answer: Competition pushed green anoles higher in trees, and their toe pads grew larger over generations.
  4. Name three limiting factors and define carrying capacity.
    Answer: Food, water, space, predators, disease; the largest population an environment can support over time.
  5. Explain coral bleaching using mutualism.
    Answer: Warm water makes coral expel its food-making algae partners, so the coral may starve.
  6. Describe one way people break or support relationships in ecosystems.
    Answer: Invasive species or warmer seas break them; keeping bees or sustainable harvesting supports them.
