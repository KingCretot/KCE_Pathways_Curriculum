Grade 6 · Week 24 · Parent Guide

Week 24: Inequalities, Author's Craft, How Organisms Interact and the Indian Ocean World

Math writes, tests and graphs inequalities and uses them to stay within a budget. ELA finishes The Call of the Wild (Chapters VI and VII) and studies London's craft: word choice, narrator, foreshadowing and how he portrays the Yeehats. Science sorts the relationships between organisms, from cleaning fish on Florida reefs to the lynx and hare cycle of the Yukon. Social Studies sails the monsoon winds to the Swahili Coast and visits Great Zimbabwe.

How scoring works: each lesson scores itself. Your child gets two tries per question: full points on the first try, half points on the second, then the answer and explanation appear. A score of 85 or higher means the lesson is mastered. Written answers go to Jonathan, who scores them with a rubric.

Time this week: About 60 minutes of live lessons per subject, plus a hands-on activity, Think Deeper questions and an Exit Check for each. ELA also needs time to read Chapters VI and VII (about an hour and a half). The science simulation takes about 30 minutes plus graphing.

Gather this week: Painter's tape or chalk, 11 sheets of paper and a marker, two paper plates (one plain, one colored in), a family member, a grocery store flyer, a store's website or a recent receipt, paper and pencil, and optionally a calculator for checking (Math); painter's tape or string about 8 feet long, 15 index cards and a marker, The Call of the Wild (Chapters VI and VII), a flashlight, a family member as audience, and paper and pencil (ELA); 150 paper squares about 1 inch across and 40 squares about 4 inches across in a different color, cut from scrap paper (about 20 minutes of cutting), scissors, tape to mark a 2-foot by 2-foot area on a table or rug, paper and pencil or graph paper, and colored pencils (Science); about 100 small wooden blocks, dominoes, unconnected LEGO bricks or sugar cubes, a ruler, 12 index cards and a marker, about 20 small household items or paper squares to stand for trade goods, a family member, and paper and pencil (Social Studies).

Heads up: Novel content: Chapter VII is the most violent chapter. The Yeehats kill John Thornton, his partners and the camp dogs, and Buck kills several Yeehats; London describes the attack in graphic detail. Please preview it and decide whether to read it aloud, summarize it or skip the attack passage. The lesson also discusses London's stereotyped portrayal of the Yeehats, an Indigenous group he describes without names or a point of view; your child will compare it with the real history of the Klondike's First Nations from Week 21. In the retelling activity, your child tells the ending from a Yeehat family's point of view using only what the novel says and ordinary feelings, with no invented ceremonies, clothing, accents or costumes. The ELA retelling is done with the lights off by flashlight; clear the floor first and keep a lamp nearby. Pick up tape and wipe off chalk so no one slips, work with the paper squares on a table or rug, and keep paper squares, blocks, sugar cubes and small trade items away from young children (choking hazard) and pets.

ELA: Author's Craft

Your child analyzes how London's diction and figurative language create tone, how the third-person narrator knows more than Buck and shifts to a legend at the end, how foreshadowing builds toward Buck joining the wolves, and how the book comes full circle. They also read as a critic, examining London's portrayal of the Yeehats and comparing it with the real Tr'ondëk Hwëch'in, Tagish and Tlingit history from Week 21.

Ask at dinner: "Why do you think London chose that word instead of a plainer one?"

Try it together: Watch your child's flashlight retelling of the ending, then the version told from a Yeehat family's point of view (based only on the novel and ordinary feelings), and talk about how each made you feel.

Signs it's clicking: They can admire London's writing and still explain what he got wrong. Signs they need help: They summarize the plot instead of analyzing craft. Ask, "How did London make you feel that?"

Math: Inequalities

Your child reads and writes inequalities with <, >, ≤ and ≥, tests values to see whether they are solutions, graphs inequalities with open and closed circles, and solves budget problems, learning when to round down (spending limits) and when to round up (savings goals).

Ask at dinner: "Is the boundary number included, and how do you know?"

Try it together: Do the $50 grocery challenge together with a real store flyer.

Signs it's clicking: They say "at most 11" when 11.4 notebooks fit the budget. Signs they need help: They mix up "more than" and "at least." Ask whether the number itself counts.

Life Science: How Organisms Interact

Your child classifies predation, competition, mutualism, commensalism and parasitism, reads the famous lynx and snowshoe hare cycle, learns how invasive brown anoles pushed Florida's green anoles higher into the trees, why tangelo growers need bees, explains coral bleaching as a broken partnership, and models predators and prey with paper squares.

Ask at dinner: "In this relationship, who benefits, who is harmed and who isn't affected?"

Try it together: Look for relationships in your yard or a park, such as Spanish moss on an oak or bees on flowers, and classify them together.

Signs it's clicking: They explain why the lynx peak comes after the hare peak. Signs they need help: They think symbiosis always means both benefit. Remind them of ticks and heartworms.

World and American History: The Swahili Coast, Great Zimbabwe and the Indian Ocean

Your child learns how monsoon winds powered trade between East Africa and Arabia, India and China, explores the Swahili city-states and their Bantu-based language and culture, compares Ibn Battuta's 1331 description of Kilwa with what archaeologists found, studies Great Zimbabwe's mortarless stone walls and gold trade, and learns how colonial myths denying that Africans built it were disproved.

Ask at dinner: "Why would someone want people to believe Africans didn't build Great Zimbabwe?"

Try it together: Try the block-wall challenge together and see whose wall stands tallest without glue.

Signs it's clicking: They trace gold from Great Zimbabwe to China step by step. Signs they need help: They think Africa was isolated before Europeans arrived. Point to the Chinese porcelain found at Great Zimbabwe.
