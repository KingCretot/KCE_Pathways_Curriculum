Unit 5: Expressions and Equations · Week 24 · Math · Teacher Plan

Inequalities

Lesson length: 60 minutes live, plus the after-class interactive lesson

Objectives

  • Read and write inequalities using <, >, ≤ and ≥.
  • Determine whether given values make an inequality true or false.
  • Graph an inequality on a number line using open and closed circles.
  • Write inequalities to represent real-world limits, including budgets.
  • Solve one-step inequalities in budget situations and explain what the solutions mean.

Standards

  • MA.6.AR.2.1: Given an equation or inequality and a specified set of integer values, determine which values make the equation or inequality true or false.
  • MA.6.AR.2.3: Write and solve one-step equations in one variable within a mathematical or real-world context using multiplication and division, where all terms and solutions are integers.
  • MA.6.AR.2.4: Determine the unknown decimal or fraction in an equation involving any of the four operations, relating three numbers, with the unknown in any position.
  • Financial literacy: budgeting and comparing spending to income (Jump$tart Spending and Saving).

Materials

  • The interactive lesson
  • Painter's tape or chalk, 11 sheets of paper, a marker, 2 paper plates
  • A grocery flyer, website or receipt; optional calculator
  • Printable practice page

Vocabulary

  • inequality: A mathematical sentence that compares two expressions using , ≤, ≥ or ≠.
  • solution of an inequality: Any value of the variable that makes the inequality true; there are often infinitely many.
  • at least: Greater than or equal to (≥).
  • at most: Less than or equal to (≤).
  • open circle: A circle on a number line showing the number is not included ( ).
  • budget: A plan for how much money you can spend; the spending limit.

Key questions to ask

  • Is the boundary number included?
  • Which way do the solutions go?
  • Does your answer make sense with whole items?
  • Should you round up or down here?

Common mistakes to watch for

  • "At least" means less than.
  • An inequality has only one solution.
  • Open and closed circles mean the same thing.
  • Always round to the nearest whole number in budget problems.

Support (if the student is struggling)

  • Provide a phrase-to-symbol chart.
  • Use the human number line for every graph.
  • Test two values (one solution, one not) before graphing.

Challenge (if the student is ready for more)

  • Graph the compound situation "at least 48 inches and less than 76 inches" for a ride.
  • Explain what happens to 4 > 2 when you multiply both sides by −1, and why (a Grade 7 preview).

Background for the teacher

Florida B.E.S.T. Grade 6 asks students to test values in inequalities (MA.6.AR.2.1); writing, solving and graphing one-step inequalities is formally Grade 7 (MA.7.AR.2.1), so this week treats solving as an extension through budget contexts, with only positive coefficients. Prices are practice figures; the grocery challenge uses real local prices.

Sources

  • Florida Department of Education, B.E.S.T. Standards for Mathematics (2020).
  • Jump$tart Coalition, National Standards for Personal Financial Education (2021).

How the lesson runs

During the live lesson, the student shares their screen and answers the Reconnect questions and the Learn checks live while you teach. The Hands-On activity, Think Deeper and Exit Check are completed after class.

Scoring

The interactive lesson scores itself: full points on the first try, half points on the second try, then the answer and explanation appear. Mastery is 85. Written responses arrive in your results email with the score; score them with the rubric below before the student moves on.

Answer key: interactive lesson

Reconnect

  1. Solve: 6p = 48
    Answer: 8
    Divide both sides by 6.
  2. Solve: m − 12.5 = 30
    Answer: 42.5
    Add 12.5 to both sides.
  3. Five identical books cost $43.75. What does one book cost?
    Answer: 8.75 dollars
    5b = 43.75, so b = $8.75.
  4. Maya needs to earn exactly $135. Last week you solved 9n = 135. But in real life, would Maya be upset if she earned more than $135? What kind of math sentence fits "at least $135" better than an equation?
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Notes that more than $135 is fine; Suggests an inequality
    Model answer: No, earning more would be fine. "At least $135" means $135 or more, so an inequality like 9n ≥ 135 fits better than an equation.

Learn 1: Reading and Writing Inequalities

  1. Write an inequality: "You must be at least 13 years old." Use a for age.
    Answer: a ≥ 13
    At least means 13 or older.
  2. Write an inequality: "A carry-on bag can weigh no more than 22 pounds." Use w.
    Answer: w ≤ 22
    No more than means 22 or less.
  3. Explain the difference between "more than 10 students" and "at least 10 students." Write an inequality for each.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Explains whether 10 is included in each; Correct inequality for each
    Model answer: "More than 10" doesn't include 10, so it's s > 10 (11, 12, and so on). "At least 10" includes 10, so it's s ≥ 10.

Learn 2: Testing Solutions

  1. Test the values 6, 7, 7.5 and 8 in 4n ≥ 30. Which is the smallest of them that makes it true?
    Answer: 7.5
    4(7.5) = 30, and 30 ≥ 30 is true; 4(7) = 28 is too small.
  2. Is −2 a solution of x + 5 > 2? Type yes or no.
    Answer: yes
    −2 + 5 = 3, and 3 > 2.
  3. An equation like x + 4 = 10 has one solution. Explain why x + 4 < 10 has infinitely many, and name four of them, including one negative and one decimal.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Explains why there are infinitely many solutions; Four correct solutions, including a negative and a decimal
    Model answer: Any number less than 6 makes x + 4 < 10 true, and there are infinitely many numbers below 6, including fractions and decimals. Four examples: 5, 0, −3 and 2.5.

Learn 3: Graphing on a Number Line

  1. Describe the graph of y ≥ −1.
    Answer: closed; right
    ≥ includes −1, and greater numbers are to the right.
  2. A number line has an open circle at 50 and shading to the left. Write the inequality, then describe a real situation it could represent.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Correct inequality with the correct symbol; A real situation where the boundary is not included
    Model answer: x < 50. It could mean a speed limit sign that says you must drive under 50 miles per hour in a construction zone.

Learn 4: Money: Staying Within a Budget

  1. Art kits cost $6.25 each and the co-op has $50. What is the greatest number of kits it can buy?
    Answer: 8 kits
    6.25k ≤ 50, so k ≤ 8.
  2. Jordan has $75 for a bike repair. The part costs $28. What is the most he can spend on labor?
    Answer: 47 dollars
    28 + L ≤ 75, so L ≤ 47.
  3. A family wants to save at least $500 for a trip and can save $45 a week. What is the fewest number of weeks needed?
    Answer: 12 weeks
    45w ≥ 500, w ≥ 11.1, so 12 weeks (11 weeks gives only $495).
  4. In one problem the answer 11.43 became 11, and in another 13.5 became 14. Explain the rule for which way to round in budget and goal problems.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: States when to round down, with a reason; States when to round up, with a reason; Mentions whole items
    Model answer: When you have a spending limit, round down, because rounding up would put you over budget. When you must reach a goal, round up, because rounding down would leave you short. Either way, you can only buy or sell whole items.

Hands-On

  1. Write three inequalities you acted out, the type of circle you used and the solutions you stood on. Which one was trickiest and why?
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Three inequalities with the correct circles; Correct whole-number solutions; Reflects on the trickiest
    Model answer: x ≥ −2: closed circle at −2, stood on −2, −1, 0, 1, 2, 3, 4, 5. x < 1: open circle at 1, stood on 0, −1 down to −5. x > −4: open circle, stood on −3 through 5. The trickiest was x < 1, because I wanted to stand on 1, but the open circle means 1 doesn't count.
  2. Show your grocery plan: items, prices, total, your inequality and how much you had left. How many extra items could you buy with the leftover money? Show the inequality.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Itemized list with real prices; Total and a budget inequality; Leftover found; Extra-item inequality solved and rounded correctly
    Model answer: Chicken $11.48, rice $3.29, broccoli $4.50, tortillas $3.19, beans $2.58, cheese $4.99, salsa $3.49, milk $3.89. Total $37.41, and 37.41 ≤ 50 is true, so I'm within budget. Left: 50 − 37.41 = $12.59. Fruit cups at $1.29: 1.29f ≤ 12.59, f ≤ 9.76, so at most 9 fruit cups.

Think Deeper

  1. Your family's phone plan costs $35 a month plus $5 for each GB of data over the limit, and the family budget for the phone is no more than $60 a month. Write an inequality, solve it, and explain what your answer means. Then give one piece of advice to someone who keeps going over.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Correct inequality and solution; Explains the meaning in context; Practical advice about staying within budget
    Model answer: 35 + 5g ≤ 60. Subtract 35: 5g ≤ 25. Divide by 5: g ≤ 5. That means we can use at most 5 GB over the limit each month and still stay within $60. My advice is to use Wi-Fi at home and check data usage weekly so there are no surprises.
  2. Explain how solving 4x ≤ 20 is like solving 4x = 20, and how the solutions are different. Then describe a real situation where each makes more sense.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Compares the solving steps; Compares the solutions; A situation for each
    Model answer: Both are solved by dividing both sides by 4. But 4x = 20 has one solution, x = 5, while 4x ≤ 20 has every number 5 or less. An equation fits buying exactly 5 tickets at $4 when the total was $20; an inequality fits having up to $20 to spend on $4 tickets.

Exit Check

  1. Write an inequality: "The temperature stayed below 32°F." Use t.
    Answer: t < 32
    Below 32 doesn't include 32.
  2. Which kind of circle do you draw for x ≥ 4: open or closed?
    Answer: closed
    ≥ includes 4.
  3. Tickets cost $9 each and you have $60. What is the greatest number of tickets you can buy?
    Answer: 6 tickets
    9t ≤ 60, t ≤ 6.67, so 6.
  4. Why is a budget better described by an inequality than an equation?
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Explains what a budget limit means; Explains why one exact amount does not fit
    Model answer: A budget is a limit, so you can spend any amount up to it, not just one exact number.

Answer key: printable page

  1. Write an inequality for each: (a) at least 18 people (b) less than $25 (c) no more than 3 hours
    Answer: (a) p ≥ 18 (b) c < 25 (c) h ≤ 3
  2. Which values make 5x − 2 > 18 true: 3, 4, 4.5, 6?
    Answer: 4.5 and 6 (5x − 2 = 20.5 and 28); 4 gives 18, which is not greater than 18.
  3. Draw number lines for x < −2 and x ≥ 0.
    Answer: Open circle at −2 shaded left; closed circle at 0 shaded right.
  4. Pencils cost $0.85 each. You have $10. Write and solve an inequality for the greatest number you can buy.
    Answer: 0.85p ≤ 10; p ≤ 11.76; at most 11 pencils.
  5. You need at least $240 for a tablet and earn $18 a week mowing. Write and solve an inequality for the fewest weeks.
    Answer: 18w ≥ 240; w ≥ 13.33; 14 weeks.
  6. Explain when to round up and when to round down in budget problems.
    Answer: Round down for a spending limit; round up to reach a goal.
