Unit 5: Novel Study · Week 24 · ELA · Teacher Plan

Author's Craft

Lesson length: 60 minutes live, plus the after-class interactive lesson

Objectives

  • Analyze how London's diction and figurative language create tone.
  • Explain how the narrator's point of view, and its shift at the end, shapes the story.
  • Identify foreshadowing and explain how the novel's structure comes full circle.
  • Evaluate London's portrayal of the Yeehats using what you know about the Klondike's real First Nations.
  • Use the roots prim, spir and imper to understand challenging words.

Standards

  • ELA.6.R.3.1: Explain how figurative language contributes to tone and meaning in text(s).
  • ELA.6.R.1.3: Explain the influence of multiple narrators and/or shifts in point of view in a literary text.
  • ELA.6.R.1.2: Analyze the development of stated or implied theme(s) throughout a literary text.
  • ELA.6.V.1.2: Apply knowledge of Greek and Latin roots and affixes to determine meanings of words and phrases in grade-level content.
  • ELA.K12.EE.1.1: Cite evidence to explain and justify reasoning.

Materials

  • The interactive lesson
  • The Call of the Wild, Chapters VI and VII
  • Painter's tape or string, 15 index cards, a marker, a flashlight
  • Printable practice page

Vocabulary

  • diction: An author's choice of words.
  • connotation: The feelings or ideas a word suggests beyond its dictionary meaning.
  • tone: The author's attitude toward a subject, shown through word choice and details.
  • narrator: The voice that tells a story.
  • foreshadowing: Hints or clues about what will happen later in a story.
  • stereotype: An oversimplified, often unfair idea about a whole group of people.
  • prim / spir / imper: Latin roots meaning first (primitive, primordial), breathe (spirit, inspire) and command (imperious, imperative).

Key questions to ask

  • Why this word and not another?
  • Whose eyes are we seeing through, and whose are missing?
  • What clues did London plant earlier?
  • Can we admire a book and still criticize it?

Common mistakes to watch for

  • Tone is the same as mood (tone is the author's attitude; mood is the reader's feeling).
  • Third person means the narrator knows nothing about thoughts.
  • Criticizing a classic means you shouldn't read it.
  • The Yeehats are a real First Nation.

Support (if the student is struggling)

  • Provide a connotation chart with sample words.
  • Read the last three paragraphs aloud together before the retelling.
  • Offer sentence frames: "London's word ___ suggests ___ because ___."

Challenge (if the student is ready for more)

  • Compare London's portrayal of the Yeehats with an Indigenous-authored story or account of the Yukon.
  • Rewrite one Chapter VI paragraph in first person from Buck's point of view and explain what changes.

Background for the teacher

Chapter VII is violent: the Yeehats kill Thornton, his partners Hans and Pete, and the dogs at camp, and Buck kills several Yeehats; London describes the attack in graphic detail. The lesson quotes only short phrases. The Yeehats appear to be London's invention and do not correspond to a documented First Nation; their portrayal reflects stereotypes common in early twentieth-century fiction. Week 21 taught the Klondike's First Nations (Tr'ondëk Hwëch'in, Tagish, Tlingit). All quotations are checked against Project Gutenberg eBook #215. Families may preview Chapter VII and summarize the attack instead of reading it.

Sources

  • London, Jack. The Call of the Wild (1903), Project Gutenberg eBook #215.
  • Tr'ondëk Hwëch'in, Tr'ondëk-Klondike World Heritage Site (UNESCO, 2023).
  • Florida Department of Education, B.E.S.T. Standards for English Language Arts (2020).

How the lesson runs

During the live lesson, the student shares their screen and answers the Reconnect questions and the Learn checks live while you teach. The Hands-On activity, Think Deeper and Exit Check are completed after class.

Scoring

Answer key: interactive lesson

Reconnect

  1. What symbol keeps pulling Buck back toward people in Chapter VI: the club, the fire or the trail?
    Answer: the fire
    Love for Thornton "drew him back to the fire again."
  2. What do we call a character whose differences highlight another character, like Hal compared with François?
    Answer: a foil
    Thornton is a foil too: the ideal master.
  3. Write one theme statement about The Call of the Wild as a complete sentence (not one word).
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: A complete sentence; States an insight about life, not just a topic
    Model answer: When the old rules no longer work, survival depends on adapting, even if it means losing part of who you were.
  4. Last week, John Thornton saved Buck from Hal. Predict how Thornton, as a foil to Hal, will change Buck's story.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Contrasts Thornton with Hal; A reasonable prediction
    Model answer: Because Thornton is kind where Hal was cruel, I think Buck will love him and stay near people for a while, even as the wild keeps calling.

Learn 1: Diction, Connotation and Tone

  1. The feelings a word suggests beyond its dictionary meaning are its ___.
    Answer: connotation
    Denotation is the dictionary meaning.
  2. London calls Buck "a live hurricane of fury." What does the metaphor suggest about Buck that a plain sentence like "Buck attacked angrily" does not? Connect it to a theme.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Explains what a hurricane suggests; Connects to a theme
    Model answer: A hurricane is a force of nature that destroys everything in its path and can't be reasoned with. The metaphor shows that Buck is no longer a pet thinking like a civilized dog but part of the wild itself, which fits the theme that the wild has reclaimed him.
  3. Rewrite this plain sentence twice, once with a tender tone and once with a menacing tone, by changing only the diction: "The dog walked toward the man in the camp."
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: A tender version with warm connotations; A menacing version with threatening connotations; The basic action stays the same
    Model answer: Tender: The loyal dog padded softly toward his beloved master by the glowing fire. Menacing: The wolfish beast stalked silently toward the stranger crouched in the shadowy camp.

Learn 2: The Narrator and Point of View

  1. Is The Call of the Wild told in first person or third person?
    Answer: third person
    A narrator outside Buck tells his story.
  2. The first line says Buck "did not read the newspapers." What does this show about the narrator, and how does it create suspense?
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Explains what the line shows about the narrator; Explains how it creates suspense
    Model answer: It shows the narrator knows more than Buck does, including the gold rush and the demand for strong dogs. It creates suspense because readers know trouble is coming while Buck has no idea.
  3. Why might London end the novel by shifting from Buck's thoughts to the legend of the Ghost Dog? Give one effect of the shift.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Describes the shift; An effect
    Model answer: Once Buck fully joins the wild, we can no longer see into his mind, just as people can't understand a wild wolf. Telling his story as a legend makes him seem mythic and mysterious, which shows how completely he has left the human world.

Learn 3: Foreshadowing and Structure

  1. Put these steps of Buck's pull toward the wild in story order.
    Answer: Buck first feels the call but returns to the fire → Buck runs with the wild brother for a day → Buck stays away hunting for days at a time → Buck joins the wolf pack after Thornton's death
    Each step goes farther, which is foreshadowing.
  2. Explain how the novel comes full circle. Compare Buck in Chapter I with Buck at the end, using one similarity and one difference.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: A similarity; A difference
    Model answer: In both chapters Buck is a ruler: first the king of Judge Miller's place, then the leader of a wolf pack. But in Chapter I he is a pampered pet in sunny California living by human rules, and at the end he is a wild hunter in the Yukon living by instinct.

Learn 4: Reading as a Critic: How London Portrays the Yeehats

  1. List three things London leaves out about the Yeehats that he gives to other characters such as Thornton or Hal.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Three specific omissions
    Model answer: He never gives them names, never explains why they attack, and never shows their thoughts or daily life, while Thornton and Hal get names, motives and personalities.
  2. How could a portrayal like the Yeehats in a famous, widely read novel affect how readers saw real Indigenous people in 1903 and today? Use what you learned in Week 21.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Explains the stereotype; Contrasts with real history from Week 21; Explains a real effect
    Model answer: The Yeehats are nameless, violent and frightened of an "Evil Spirit," so readers might picture all Indigenous people that way. In reality, the Klondike was the Tr'ondëk Hwëch'in homeland, Keish helped find the gold and Tlingit packers made the stampede possible. A stereotype like this hides that history and the harm the rush caused, and can make readers dismiss real people even today.

Hands-On

  1. Which three words were hardest to place on the tone line, where did you put them, and why? What did the line show you about how London's tone changes from Chapter VI to Chapter VII?
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Three hard words with placements and reasons; A conclusion about the change in tone across the chapters
    Model answer: "Luring" was hard because it sounds both exciting and dangerous, so I put it just past the middle. "Camp" is neutral by itself. "Wild brother" sounds friendly but also wild, so I placed it near tender. Most Chapter VI words ended up on the tender side and most Chapter VII words on the menacing side, which shows London's tone darkening as Buck leaves people behind.
  2. How did your Yeehat-perspective retelling change the story? What did you add that London left out, and how did it change how your audience felt about the ending?
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Describes the perspective retelling; Names what was added; Reflects on the audience's response
    Model answer: I told it from the point of view of a grandmother whose family hunts moose in the fall. I added her name, her worry about the strangers digging for gold and her grief for the hunters who never came home. My audience said the Ghost Dog seemed frightening instead of glorious, and they felt sorry for the family instead of just cheering for Buck.

Think Deeper

  1. Choose two craft tools from this week (diction, figurative language, point of view, foreshadowing, structure). Explain how each helps London develop one theme of the novel. Quote the text at least twice.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Two craft tools named and explained; At least two accurate quotations; Each tool clearly linked to the same theme; Organized paragraphs with a clear claim
    Model answer: London uses diction and point of view to develop the theme that the wild can reclaim even a civilized creature. First, his diction shifts from tender to violent. In Chapter VI, Buck feels "love that was feverish and burning," but in Chapter VII he becomes "a live hurricane of fury." The words move from warmth to destruction, showing Buck's civilized side giving way to the wild. Second, the point of view shifts at the end. After "And here may well end the story of Buck," we no longer see inside Buck's mind; he becomes a legend that "runs at the head of the pack." Because we can no longer understand him as a human would, the shift shows he has fully joined the wild. Together these choices make the theme felt, not just stated.
  2. Some schools and families still read The Call of the Wild; others choose different books because of its violence and its portrayal of the Yeehats. Should homeschool families still read it? Make a claim, give two reasons and answer one counterclaim, as you learned in Unit 4.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: A clear claim; Two reasons supported with examples from the novel or this week; A counterclaim answered with a rebuttal
    Model answer: Homeschool families should still read The Call of the Wild, as long as they discuss its problems. First, it teaches craft: London's diction, foreshadowing and shifting point of view are models for any writer. Second, reading it critically, as we did with the Yeehats, teaches students to question stereotypes instead of absorbing them. Some argue the violence and the portrayal of Indigenous people make it harmful. That concern is real, but avoiding the book wouldn't teach students how to recognize those problems; reading it with guidance, alongside the true history of the Tr'ondëk Hwëch'in, does.

Exit Check

  1. An author's word choice is called ___.
    Answer: diction
    Diction creates tone.
  2. Hints about what will happen later in a story are called ___.
    Answer: foreshadowing
    Like the call growing louder.
  3. The Latin root in primitive and primordial means ___.
    Answer: first
    Primitive: like the first, earliest ways.
  4. In one or two sentences, explain how a reader can admire a book's craft and still criticize it.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Separates skill in writing from the ideas or portrayals in the book; Gives an example
    Model answer: You can admire how London's words make you feel Buck's love and rage while still pointing out that his Yeehats are an unfair stereotype.

Answer key: printable page

  1. Explain the difference between denotation and connotation, with an example.
    Answer: Denotation is the dictionary meaning; connotation is the feeling (home versus house).
  2. How does London's diction change between Chapter VI and Chapter VII? Quote one word or phrase from each.
    Answer: From tender ("a cheering word," "adoration") to violent ("frenzy," "hurricane of fury").
  3. What is the narrator's point of view, and how does it shift at the end?
    Answer: Third person, mostly inside Buck; at the end it steps back and tells Buck's story as a Yeehat legend.
  4. Give one example of foreshadowing in Chapters VI and VII.
    Answer: The call grows louder and Buck stays away longer, hinting he will join the wild when Thornton is gone.
  5. Describe London's portrayal of the Yeehats and one problem with it.
    Answer: Nameless attackers with no motives or point of view, called "Indians"; it stereotypes Indigenous peoples and erases real First Nations like the Tr'ondëk Hwëch'in.
  6. Use the roots prim, spir and imper to define primordial, inspire and imperious.
    Answer: Primordial: existing from the first; inspire: breathe in, fill with an idea; imperious: commanding.
