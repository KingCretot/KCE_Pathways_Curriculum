Unit 5: Empires and Global Trade · Week 24 · World and American History · Teacher Plan

The Swahili Coast, Great Zimbabwe and the Indian Ocean

Lesson length: 60 minutes live, plus the after-class interactive lesson

Objectives

  • Explain how the monsoon winds shaped Indian Ocean trade.
  • Describe the Swahili city-states, their culture and their trade.
  • Analyze a primary source about Kilwa and compare it with archaeological evidence.
  • Describe Great Zimbabwe, who built it and how its gold linked it to the coast.
  • Evaluate how colonial-era myths about Great Zimbabwe denied African achievement, and how evidence corrected them.

Standards

  • SS.6.G.2.1: Explain how major physical characteristics, natural resources, climate, and absolute and relative locations have influenced settlement, interactions, and the economies of ancient civilizations of the world.
  • SS.6.E.3.4: Describe the relationship among civilizations that engage in trade, including the benefits and drawbacks of voluntary trade.
  • SS.6.W.1.3: Interpret primary and secondary sources.
  • SS.6.W.1.4: Describe the methods of historical inquiry and how history relates to the other social sciences.
  • SS.6.W.1.5: Describe the roles of historians and recognize varying historical interpretations (historiography).

Materials

  • The interactive lesson
  • About 60 blocks, dominoes or sugar cubes, a ruler, cardboard
  • Index cards, a marker, about 20 small items for trade goods
  • Printable practice page

Vocabulary

  • monsoon: A seasonal wind that changes direction, bringing wet and dry seasons; in the Indian Ocean, it reverses about every six months.
  • city-state: An independent city that governs itself and the land around it.
  • Swahili: The Bantu-based language and culture of East Africa's coast, with many words borrowed from Arabic and other languages; from an Arabic word meaning coasts.
  • dhow: A sailing ship with triangular sails used across the Indian Ocean.
  • Shona: A Bantu-speaking people of present-day Zimbabwe whose ancestors built Great Zimbabwe.
  • archaeologist: A scientist who studies the past through objects, buildings and other remains.

Key questions to ask

  • How did geography (wind, coast, gold) shape these societies?
  • What do written sources and archaeology each tell us?
  • Who benefited from the Great Zimbabwe myth?
  • How was East Africa connected to the rest of the world?

Common mistakes to watch for

  • The Swahili coast was founded by Arabs (it was African, with Arab and Persian influence).
  • Great Zimbabwe was built by outsiders.
  • Africa was isolated before Europeans arrived.
  • Swahili is an Arabic dialect (it is a Bantu language).

Support (if the student is struggling)

  • Trace the gold route on the map with a finger before reading.
  • Provide a two-column chart: written sources and archaeology.
  • Pre-teach monsoon, dhow and city-state.

Challenge (if the student is ready for more)

  • Research the giraffe that East African envoys brought to China's Ming court in the 1410s and how Chinese officials described it.
  • Learn ten Swahili words and find which come from Arabic.

Background for the teacher

Dates: Swahili towns grew from about the 800s; Ibn Battuta visited Mogadishu, Mombasa and Kilwa in 1331 (some scholars argue 1329 or 1330); the quotation is from H. A. R. Gibb, Ibn Battuta: Travels in Asia and Africa 1325-1354 (1929), checked against the scanned text. Kilwa's Great Mosque dates from about the 1000s with later expansion; Husuni Kubwa was built in the early 1300s. Portuguese forces under Francisco de Almeida took Kilwa and sacked Mombasa in 1505. Great Zimbabwe was occupied from about 1100 to 1450; Great Enclosure walls reach about 11 m. Karl Mauch visited in 1871; David Randall-MacIver reported in 1906 (Medieval Rhodesia) on his 1905 work; Gertrude Caton-Thompson excavated in 1929 (The Zimbabwe Culture, 1931). Archaeologists including Peter Garlake and Paul Sinclair reported pressure from Rhodesian authorities in the 1970s. UNESCO inscription 1986. SS.6.W.1.4 should be checked against CPALMS. Medieval East Africa is outside Florida's Grade 6 NGSSS world history range; the standards above cover the skills taught.

Answer key: interactive lesson

Reconnect

  1. What was the capital of the Songhai Empire?
    Answer: Gao
    On the Niger River.
  2. In what year did Morocco defeat Songhai at Tondibi?
    Answer: 1591
    Moroccan guns won the battle.
  3. Which Timbuktu scholar wrote that skin color was no reason to enslave anyone?
    Answer: Ahmad Baba
    He wrote it in 1615.
  4. Camels carried goods across the Sahara to Timbuktu. Predict one advantage and one challenge of carrying goods by sea instead.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: An advantage; A challenge
    Model answer: A ship can carry far more cargo than a camel caravan, but sailors depend on the winds and risk storms and shipwrecks.

Learn 1: The Swahili City-States

  1. The word Swahili comes from an Arabic word meaning ___.
    Answer: coasts
    The culture of the coast.
  2. The Swahili language is Bantu at its core but borrowed many Arabic words. What does this tell historians about the people of the coast and their contacts?
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Explains what the Bantu core shows; Explains what the Arabic words show
    Model answer: It shows the coastal people were African, speaking a Bantu language, but they had centuries of close contact with Arabic-speaking traders and Muslim scholars, so they borrowed words for trade, religion and daily life.
  3. Swahili merchants were go-betweens between inland Africa and overseas traders. Compare their role with the role of Timbuktu's merchants in the trans-Saharan trade.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: One similarity with Timbuktu's merchants; One difference
    Model answer: Like Timbuktu's merchants, Swahili traders connected the gold of inland Africa with buyers far away and grew rich from it. But they used dhows and monsoon winds on the ocean instead of camels in the desert, and their partners were in Arabia, India and China instead of North Africa.

Learn 2: Kilwa: A Traveler and the Archaeologists

  1. Which material were Kilwa's Great Mosque and Husuni Kubwa palace built from?
    Answer: coral stone
    Archaeologists excavated their ruins.
  2. Ibn Battuta's account (in Gibb's translation) says Kilwa's buildings were of wood, but archaeologists found great stone buildings. Give two possible explanations, and explain why historians use both kinds of evidence.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Two possible explanations; Why historians use both kinds of evidence
    Model answer: Most of the houses Ibn Battuta saw may really have been wood, with only a few great buildings of stone, or the words may have changed in copying and translation. Historians use both because his account tells us about people like the generous sultan, while archaeology shows what was actually built, and each helps check the other.
  3. Ibn Battuta visited Kilwa in 1331 and Mali in 1352. How many years apart were his visits?
    Answer: 21 years
    1352 − 1331 = 21.

Learn 3: Great Zimbabwe: Houses of Stone

  1. Which people's ancestors built Great Zimbabwe?
    Answer: the Shona
    A Bantu-speaking people of Zimbabwe.
  2. The walls of Great Zimbabwe were built without mortar and still stand after about 700 years. What does this tell you about the builders' skills and their society?
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: What it shows about the builders' skills; What it shows about their society
    Model answer: It shows the builders were skilled engineers who could shape and stack stones so precisely that gravity and balance hold them up. Building walls that big also took organized labor and leaders who could plan, gather resources and feed many workers for years, which means the society was wealthy and well run.
  3. Explain the chain that connected a gold miner near Great Zimbabwe to a merchant in China. Name at least three places or steps.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: At least three correct steps; Shows goods traveling in both directions
    Model answer: Gold mined on the plateau was controlled by Great Zimbabwe's rulers, carried to the port of Sofala, and shipped up the coast to Kilwa. Swahili and Arab merchants then sailed it on the monsoon winds to Arabia and India, where it could reach Chinese traders, and Chinese porcelain traveled all the way back to Great Zimbabwe.

Learn 4: A Lie About the Past, and the Evidence That Exposed It

  1. Caton-Thompson confirmed African builders in 1929. The country took the name Zimbabwe in 1980. How many years later was that?
    Answer: 51 years
    1980 − 1929 = 51.
  2. Why did some colonizers and the Rhodesian government insist that Africans could not have built Great Zimbabwe, even after archaeologists proved they did? What does this teach about how history can be misused?
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Explains the motive; A lesson
    Model answer: Admitting that Africans built a great stone city would have undercut the claim that colonizers were bringing civilization to Africa, so they kept the myth to justify their rule. It teaches that people in power can twist history to serve themselves, so we should always ask who benefits from a story and check it against evidence.
  3. Why do you think the new nation chose the name Zimbabwe in 1980?
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Connects the name to the site's meaning; Considers what name it replaced and why that mattered
    Model answer: Choosing Zimbabwe honored a great African achievement and replaced Rhodesia, a name that celebrated a British colonizer, Cecil Rhodes. It showed pride in the country's own history.

Hands-On

  1. Compare your three walls. Which stood tallest and strongest, and why? Connect your results to how Great Zimbabwe's builders designed the Great Enclosure.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Results for all three walls with measurements; Explains staggering, tapering and curving; Connects to Great Zimbabwe's design
    Model answer: The lined-up wall fell at 9 layers. The staggered, tapered wall reached 15 layers and didn't fall when fanned, and the curved wall was the steadiest. Staggering spreads weight across blocks, tapering lowers the center of gravity, and curves resist tipping, which helps explain why Great Zimbabwe's curving, tapered walls still stand without mortar.
  2. Describe your trading game. When did you have to wait for the wind, and what did that teach you about planning a real trading voyage?
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Describes turns and trades; Notes when you waited for the wind; Connects to how real merchants planned voyages
    Model answer: I sailed to Africa in November with cloth and beads and traded for gold and ivory, but I had to wait in port until April before the wind would take me home. Real merchants had to plan around the seasons and often stayed for months, which is one reason Arab and Persian traders settled and married into coastal communities.

Think Deeper

  1. Compare trans-Saharan trade (Weeks 21 to 23) with Indian Ocean trade (this week). Give two similarities and two differences, with specific examples.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Two similarities; Two differences; Specific examples
    Model answer: Both trade networks carried African gold to distant markets and spread Islam, and both created wealthy trading cities, like Timbuktu and Kilwa. But trans-Saharan trade used camel caravans across the desert to North Africa and was built on the gold-for-salt exchange, while Indian Ocean trade used dhows on monsoon winds to reach Arabia, India and China, bringing back cloth and porcelain.
  2. Weeks 22 to 24 have shown several examples of outsiders misjudging African history: the claim that West Africa had no written history, Ibn Battuta's view of Kilwa and the myth of Great Zimbabwe. What tools do historians use to correct such mistakes? Give at least three, with examples.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: At least three tools; Accurate examples from Weeks 22 to 24
    Model answer: First, archaeology: digs at Great Zimbabwe and Kilwa proved African builders and stone architecture. Second, African sources: Timbuktu's manuscripts and the griots' Epic of Sundiata show Africans recording their own history. Third, comparing sources and asking who benefits: the Great Zimbabwe myth collapsed once historians noticed it served colonial rule and didn't match the evidence.

Exit Check

  1. What seasonal winds carried ships across the Indian Ocean?
    Answer: the monsoon winds
    They reverse about every six months.
  2. Name one Swahili city-state.
    Answer: Kilwa (also Mombasa, Malindi, Mogadishu, Zanzibar, Lamu)
    Independent trading cities on the coast.
  3. What modern country takes its name from Great Zimbabwe?
    Answer: Zimbabwe
    It took the name at independence in 1980.
  4. In one or two sentences, explain why Great Zimbabwe matters to how we understand African history.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Explains who built it and what that shows; Explains what its story shows about evidence and myth
    Model answer: It proves that Africans built a powerful, trading stone city, and the myths denying it show how colonizers tried to erase African achievement.

Answer key: printable page

  1. How did the monsoon winds make Indian Ocean trade possible?
    Answer: They blow toward Africa from November to March and away from April to September, so ships sailed with the season.
  2. Describe Swahili culture in three details.
    Answer: Bantu language with Arabic words; mostly Muslim; coral-stone towns; trading city-states.
  3. What did Ibn Battuta say about Kilwa, and what did archaeologists find?
    Answer: A fine, solidly built town of wood; archaeologists found a coral-stone Great Mosque and palace.
  4. Who built Great Zimbabwe, and what made it powerful?
    Answer: Ancestors of the Shona; cattle and control of gold traded to the coast.
  5. What myth did colonizers spread about Great Zimbabwe, and how was it disproved?
    Answer: That outsiders built it; Randall-MacIver (1905) and Caton-Thompson (1929) showed Africans built it.
  6. Name two goods East Africa exported and two it imported.
    Answer: Gold, ivory (exported); cotton cloth, Chinese porcelain (imported).
