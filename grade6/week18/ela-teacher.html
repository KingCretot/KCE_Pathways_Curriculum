Unit 4: Making the Case (Argument) · Week 18 · ELA · Teacher Plan

Counterclaims and Rebuttals

Lesson length: 60 minutes live, plus the after-class interactive lesson

Objectives

  • Identify counterclaims and rebuttals in an argument.
  • State an opposing view fairly, without twisting it into a weaker version (a straw man).
  • Write rebuttals that concede what is true and answer with reasons and evidence.
  • Analyze how Thomas Paine answered counterclaims in Common Sense, including his use of analogy.
  • Use transitions and the roots ced/cess and vert/vers in argument writing.

Standards

  • ELA.6.C.1.3: Write and support a claim using logical reasoning, relevant evidence from sources, elaboration, and a logical organizational structure with varied transitions.
  • ELA.6.R.2.4: Track the development of an argument, identifying the types of reasoning used.
  • ELA.6.C.2.1: Present information orally, in a logical sequence, using nonverbal cues, appropriate volume, clear pronunciation, and appropriate pacing.
  • ELA.6.V.1.2: Apply knowledge of Greek and Latin roots and affixes to determine meanings of words and phrases in grade-level content.
  • ELA.K12.EE.1.1: Cite evidence to explain and justify reasoning.

Materials

  • The interactive lesson
  • Two signs (FOR and AGAINST), tape, a soft ball or rolled socks
  • A timer, index cards, pencil
  • Printable practice page

Vocabulary

  • counterclaim: An opposing view that an argument answers.
  • rebuttal: A response that shows why a counterclaim is wrong or weaker.
  • concede: To admit that part of the other side's point is true.
  • straw man: A fallacy: twisting the other side's argument into a weaker version that is easy to knock down.
  • analogy: A comparison that explains an idea by showing how it is like something familiar.
  • pamphlet: A short printed booklet, often arguing for a cause.
  • Loyalist: A colonist who stayed loyal to Great Britain during the American Revolution.

Key questions to ask

  • What is the strongest thing the other side could say?
  • Have I stated it fairly?
  • What can I concede, and what do I answer?
  • Freedom or rights for whom?

Common mistakes to watch for

  • Mentioning the other side weakens your argument.
  • A rebuttal must prove the other side completely wrong.
  • Conceding means giving up.
  • The American Revolution meant liberty for everyone.

Support (if the student is struggling)

  • Provide the four-step counterclaim frame.
  • Read Paine's sentences one at a time with the glossary.
  • Allow oral rebuttals before written ones.

Challenge (if the student is ready for more)

  • Read Paine's answer to the counterclaim that America is too weak to fight Britain, later in the same section.
  • Research James Armistead Lafayette, an enslaved spy for the Patriots.

Background for the teacher

Common Sense was published January 10, 1776. Sales claims vary widely (Paine claimed 120,000 copies in three months; modern historians suggest a smaller but still very large number of copies circulated, through many printings and newspaper reprints). The excerpt follows the Project Gutenberg text, keeping "connexion" and "expence" with a gloss. Enslaved people made up roughly 20 percent of the colonial population in 1776 (about 500,000 of 2.5 million). Dunmore's Proclamation was issued November 7, 1775; estimates of Black Loyalists who reached British lines run into the tens of thousands across the war. About 5,000 Black men served in the Continental forces (National Park Service). The 1775 essay "African Slavery in America" is traditionally attributed to Paine, though authorship is debated. Pennsylvania's Act for the Gradual Abolition of Slavery passed March 1, 1780, while Paine was clerk of the Pennsylvania Assembly. The Project Play statistic is summarized from the Aspen Institute's State of Play reports. Florida requires a personal financial literacy course for high school students entering grade 9 in 2023-24 and later.

Answer key: interactive lesson

Reconnect

  1. "Everyone in my co-op is switching to that math program, so it must be the best." Name the fallacy.
    Answer: bandwagon
    Popularity doesn't prove quality.
  2. Which appeal relies on the speaker's experience or trustworthiness?
    Answer: credibility (ethos)
    From Aristotle's three appeals.
  3. What kind of question did Douglass ask when he said, "Would you have me argue that man is entitled to liberty?"
    Answer: a rhetorical question
    A question that answers itself.
  4. "Our garden grew better after we got a cat, so cats help gardens." Name the weak reasoning and give a more likely cause.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: False cause; A more likely cause (more rain, better soil, more watering, season)
    Model answer: That's false cause. The garden probably grew better because of more rain or because we watered it more, not because of the cat.

Learn 1: The Counterclaim Move

  1. When a writer admits that part of the other side's point is true, the writer ___ that point.
    Answer: concedes
    con + cede: to yield.
  2. "People against the new library just hate reading." Which fallacy twists the other side into a weaker version?
    Answer: a straw man
    Most opponents probably worry about cost or location.
  3. Use the roots.
    Answer: versus; adversary
    vers means turn: turned against.
  4. Rewrite this straw-man counterclaim so it states the other side fairly: "Some people think kids shouldn't have pets because they hate animals."
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: A fair, realistic opposing reason (cost, time, allergies, responsibility); Uses a counterclaim phrase
    Model answer: Some parents argue that kids shouldn't have pets because pets cost money and need daily care that busy families may not have time for.

Learn 2: Paine Answers His Opponents

  1. In sentence 5, what counterclaim does Paine answer? Answer in a few words.
    Answer: that Britain has protected the colonies
    "But she has protected us, say some."
  2. Explain Paine's milk-and-meat analogy in your own words. How does it answer the counterclaim in sentence 1?
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: A baby needs milk but grows to need other food; what helped at one stage isn't right forever; America, like a growing child, has outgrown its need for Britain, so past success doesn't prove the connection is still needed
    Model answer: A baby grows on milk, but no one says it should drink only milk forever; as it grows it needs other food. Paine means that Britain may have helped America when it was young, but that doesn't mean America needs Britain forever. Past success doesn't prove the connection is still right.
  3. In sentence 6, what does Paine concede, and how does he still rebut the counterclaim?
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Concedes Britain did defend the colonies; Rebuts: Britain did it for its own trade and power (it would defend Turkey for the same reason), not for America's sake
    Model answer: Paine admits that Britain did defend the colonies. But he argues it did so for its own trade and power, so much so that it would have defended Turkey for the same reason. So the protection wasn't a favor that Americans owed Britain for.

Learn 3: Whose Independence?

  1. Which British governor of Virginia promised freedom in 1775 to enslaved people who joined British forces?
    Answer: Lord Dunmore
    Dunmore's Proclamation, November 1775.
  2. Write a counterclaim that an enslaved person in 1776 might have made to Paine's argument that independence would bring liberty, and a rebuttal Paine might have offered.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: A realistic counterclaim: independence brings liberty to white colonists while slavery continues; the British offered freedom; A rebuttal Paine might give (the principles of liberty could later be extended to all; he opposed slavery); Fair to both sides
    Model answer: Counterclaim: "You talk about liberty, but independence will keep me enslaved, while the British have promised me freedom if I join them." Rebuttal: "It is true that liberty has not reached you. However, a nation founded on the idea that all people have natural rights gives you a principle to demand your freedom, and I will argue for ending slavery."

Learn 4: Writing Your Counterclaim Paragraph

  1. In the model, which transition word begins the rebuttal?
    Answer: However
    It signals the turn from the counterclaim to the answer.
  2. Choose a claim you believe (or use: "Every Florida child should learn to swim") and write a full counterclaim paragraph with all four parts.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: A fair counterclaim; A concession; A rebuttal with a reason and evidence or an example; A sentence connecting back to the claim; Transitions
    Model answer: Some families argue that swim lessons are too expensive. It is true that private lessons can cost a lot. However, many YMCAs and city pools offer free or low-cost group lessons, and the American Red Cross trains instructors in communities across the state. A few weeks of lessons costs far less than the danger of a child who can't swim in a state full of water. That is why every Florida child should learn to swim.

Hands-On

  1. Write your question, the strongest counterclaim you heard, and your best rebuttal using "It is true that ... However ..." What was it like to argue the side you disagree with?
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Question and a fair counterclaim; A rebuttal with a concession and a reason; Reflection on arguing the other side
    Model answer: Question: should screen time be the same on weekends? Strongest counterclaim (my mom): "Weekends are for family time, and screens pull us apart." My rebuttal: "It is true that we should spend time together. However, we could watch a movie or play a video game as a family, which counts as both." Arguing the other side was hard, but it helped me understand why my mom cares about this.

Think Deeper

  1. Is Paine's milk-and-meat analogy a strong rebuttal or a weak one? Use what you learned last week about reasoning, and quote the text.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: A clear judgment; Accurate quotation; Explains how the analogy works and where it might break down (a country is not a child; growing up doesn't automatically mean separating); Uses vocabulary from Weeks 16 to 18
    Model answer: Paine's analogy is persuasive but not airtight. Saying that "because a child has thrived upon milk" it should never "have meat" makes the opposing argument sound silly, and it's easy for ordinary readers to picture. It works as a rebuttal because it shows that past success doesn't prove something is right forever. But an analogy is not evidence: a country isn't a child, and growing up doesn't mean a child must leave its family entirely. Paine's stronger rebuttal comes next, when he argues that America would have flourished even more on its own, though he offers that as an opinion without evidence.
  2. Write a short argument (two paragraphs) on this question: "Should Florida require every middle school student to take a financial literacy course?" Paragraph 1: claim, reason and evidence. Paragraph 2: a fair counterclaim, concession and rebuttal.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: A clear claim; A reason with evidence or an example; A fair counterclaim with a concession; A rebuttal with a reason; Transitions; no fallacies
    Model answer: Florida should require every middle school student to take a financial literacy course, because money decisions start early. Many students earn money from chores, pet sitting or a small business, and they already make choices about saving and spending. Learning about budgets, interest and scams in middle school gives them good habits before they get their first job or bank card. Some people argue that the school day is already too full and that families should teach money skills at home. It is true that parents are important teachers. However, not every family has the time or knowledge to teach budgeting, and a course makes sure every student gets the same tools. Florida already requires a personal finance course in high school, and starting in middle school would build on it.

Exit Check

  1. What is the name for a response that answers a counterclaim?
    Answer: a rebuttal
    Counterclaim, concede, rebut.
  2. What fallacy twists an opposing argument into a weaker version?
    Answer: a straw man
    Easy to knock down, but unfair.
  3. Paine compared America to a child who outgrows milk. What is this kind of comparison called?
    Answer: an analogy
    It explains an idea using something familiar.
  4. Why does including a fair counterclaim make an argument more convincing?
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Shows the writer is fair and informed (credibility); Answers the reader's doubts
    Model answer: It shows the writer understands the other side and isn't hiding from it, and the rebuttal answers the reader's doubts before they grow.

Answer key: printable page

  1. Define counterclaim, concede and rebuttal.
    Answer: Counterclaim: an opposing view. Concede: admit part of it is true. Rebuttal: an answer showing why it is wrong or weaker.
  2. What is a straw man? Give an example and fix it.
    Answer: Twisting the other side into a weaker version. Example: "Opponents just want kids bored" fixed to "Opponents worry about the cost."
  3. Explain the counterclaim Paine answers in sentence 1 and his analogy.
    Answer: Counterclaim: America did well under Britain, so it needs Britain. Analogy: a child who thrived on milk must not stay on milk forever.
  4. Why did many enslaved Black Americans join the British during the Revolution?
    Answer: Lord Dunmore and later British leaders promised freedom to those who escaped and joined them.
  5. Write a counterclaim paragraph for the claim "Our town should build a skate park."
    Answer: Model: Some residents argue that a skate park is dangerous. It is true that skaters can fall. However, a park with smooth ramps and a helmet rule is safer than skating on streets and in parking lots, where cars are a danger. A skate park would keep young people safer.
  6. Use the roots: what do concede, reverse and adversary have to do with their roots?
    Answer: Concede: ced, yield; reverse: vers, turn back; adversary: vers, turned against.
