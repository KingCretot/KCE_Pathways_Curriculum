Unit 4: The Human Body · Week 18 · Life Science · Teacher Plan

The Circulatory and Respiratory Systems

Lesson length: 60 minutes live, plus the after-class interactive lesson

Objectives

  • Describe the parts and jobs of the circulatory system: the heart, arteries, veins, capillaries and blood.
  • Trace the double loop of blood through the heart, lungs and body.
  • Describe the path of air through the respiratory system and gas exchange in the alveoli.
  • Explain how the circulatory, respiratory, muscular and nervous systems interact during exercise to maintain homeostasis.
  • Collect and graph heart-rate and breathing-rate data, and describe the work of scientists such as Charles Drew.

Standards

  • SC.6.L.14.5: Identify and investigate the general functions of the major systems of the human body (digestive, respiratory, circulatory, reproductive, excretory, immune, nervous, and musculoskeletal) and describe ways these systems interact with each other to maintain homeostasis.
  • SC.6.N.1.1: Define a problem from the sixth grade curriculum, use appropriate reference materials to support scientific understanding, plan and carry out scientific investigation of various types, such as systematic observations or experiments, identify variables, collect and organize data, interpret data in charts, tables, and graphics, analyze information, make predictions, and defend conclusions.
  • SC.6.N.3.4: Identify the role of models in the context of the sixth grade science benchmarks.
  • SC.6.N.2.3: Recognize that scientists who make contributions to scientific knowledge come from all kinds of backgrounds and possess varied talents, interests, and goals.

Materials

  • The interactive lesson
  • A timer, graph paper, pencil
  • An empty clear plastic bottle, 2 balloons, a rubber band, tape, scissors (adult use)
  • Printable practice page

Vocabulary

  • artery: A thick-walled blood vessel that carries blood away from the heart.
  • vein: A blood vessel that carries blood back to the heart; it has valves to keep blood flowing one way.
  • capillary: The tiniest blood vessel, with walls one cell thick, where materials pass between blood and cells.
  • plasma: The yellowish liquid part of blood, mostly water, that carries cells, nutrients and wastes.
  • alveoli: Tiny air sacs in the lungs where oxygen enters the blood and carbon dioxide leaves it.
  • diaphragm: A dome-shaped muscle under the lungs that contracts to pull air in.
  • gas exchange: The swap of oxygen and carbon dioxide between air, blood and cells.

Key questions to ask

  • Which way is the blood going, and is it oxygen-rich or oxygen-poor?
  • How does this structure fit its job?
  • What changes during exercise, and why?
  • Which systems are working together?

Common mistakes to watch for

  • Blood in veins is blue.
  • The heart is on the far left of the chest (it sits near the center, tilted left).
  • We breathe in oxygen and breathe out only carbon dioxide (exhaled air still contains oxygen).
  • Lungs move air by themselves (the diaphragm does the work).

Support (if the student is struggling)

  • Color-code the double-loop diagram as you trace it.
  • Practice finding the pulse together.
  • Provide a data table template.

Challenge (if the student is ready for more)

  • Research blood types and why type O negative is called the universal donor.
  • Compare resting heart rates of different animals (a hummingbird, a human, an elephant) and look for a pattern with body size.

Background for the teacher

Charles R. Drew (1904-1950) earned a doctorate from Columbia University in 1940 with a thesis on banked blood, directed the Blood for Britain project (1940) and was the first director of the American Red Cross blood bank (1941). He left the program in April 1941, the same month the Red Cross adopted the military's policy of separating blood by donor race, and later spoke out publicly against it. Daniel Hale Williams founded Provident Hospital and Training School in Chicago in 1891 and on July 10, 1893, repaired the pericardium of James Cornish, who had been stabbed. Heart rate: the American Heart Association and NIH give about 60 to 100 bpm as a typical resting range for adults and children 10 and older. About 300 to 500 million alveoli with a total surface of about 50 to 75 square meters; a singles tennis court is about 196 square meters. A resting circulation time of about one minute is commonly cited. Kenyan runners: altitude adaptation (higher red cell mass) is one of several proposed factors; training, culture and other factors also matter.

Answer key: interactive lesson

Reconnect

  1. Which part of a bone makes red blood cells?
    Answer: red marrow
    Week 17.
  2. Muscles work in opposing pairs because a muscle can only ___.
    Answer: pull (contract)
    It can't push.
  3. Which type of muscle tissue, studied in Week 16, makes up the heart?
    Answer: cardiac muscle
    It works nonstop for a lifetime.
  4. Your leg muscles work hard when you run up stairs. What two things must the blood bring them, and what waste must it carry away?
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Brings oxygen and food (glucose, nutrients); Carries away carbon dioxide (or other wastes)
    Model answer: The blood must bring oxygen and sugar for energy, and carry away carbon dioxide.

Learn 1: Heart, Vessels and Blood

  1. Name the blood vessel.
    Answer: artery; vein; capillary
    A for away: arteries.
  2. A heart beats about 70 times a minute. About how many times does it beat in one day? (There are 1,440 minutes in a day.)
    Answer: 100800 beats
    70 × 1,440 = 100,800, about 100,000 beats a day.
  3. Why is it important that capillary walls are only one cell thick? Connect structure to function.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Thin walls let oxygen, nutrients and wastes pass through easily; Exchange with body cells is the capillaries' job
    Model answer: Capillaries are where blood trades materials with body cells. Walls only one cell thick let oxygen and nutrients pass out to cells and wastes pass in easily.

Learn 2: The Double Loop

  1. Put the trip of a drop of blood in order, starting in the right side of the heart.
    Answer: right side of the heart → lungs → left side of the heart → aorta → capillaries in the body → veins
    Right heart → lungs → left heart → aorta → body capillaries → veins → right heart.
  2. Which side of the heart pumps blood to the lungs: right or left?
    Answer: the right side
    The left side pumps to the body.
  3. The left ventricle's wall is much thicker than the right ventricle's. Explain why, using the two loops.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: The left ventricle pumps blood to the whole body, a long distance needing high pressure; The right ventricle pumps only to the nearby lungs
    Model answer: The left ventricle pumps blood through the whole body, from head to toes, so it needs thick, strong muscle to create high pressure. The right ventricle only sends blood a short way to the lungs.

Learn 3: Breathing and Gas Exchange

  1. Which muscle contracts and moves down to pull air into the lungs?
    Answer: the diaphragm
    It is a skeletal muscle you can also control on purpose.
  2. In which tiny air sacs does oxygen enter the blood?
    Answer: alveoli
    Wrapped in capillaries.
  3. Why is it helpful that the lungs contain hundreds of millions of tiny alveoli instead of one big air bag? Connect structure to function.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Many tiny sacs create a huge surface area; More surface means more oxygen and carbon dioxide can be exchanged at once
    Model answer: Hundreds of millions of tiny alveoli create a huge surface, about a third of a tennis court. More surface touching capillaries means much more oxygen can move into the blood at once than one big bag could manage.

Learn 4: Systems Working Together

  1. What change in your blood tells your body to breathe faster during exercise, and why is that a good signal to use?
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Rising carbon dioxide in the blood; It increases when muscles work harder, so it shows how much oxygen they are using
    Model answer: It detects rising carbon dioxide in the blood. That's a good signal because muscles make more carbon dioxide the harder they work, so it shows exactly when they need more oxygen.
  2. Mia counts 22 heartbeats in 15 seconds. What is her heart rate in beats per minute?
    Answer: 88 beats per minute
    22 × 4 = 88 beats per minute.
  3. Which Black surgeon founded Provident Hospital in Chicago and repaired the sac around a patient's heart in 1893?
    Answer: Dr. Daniel Hale Williams
    One of the first heart surgeries of its kind.

Hands-On

  1. Record your resting, after-exercise and recovery heart rates and breathing rates. Describe the pattern on your graph and explain it using the circulatory, respiratory and muscular systems.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Data at rest, after exercise and during recovery; Describes the pattern: a rise, then a gradual fall; Explains: muscles need more oxygen and make more CO2, so heart and breathing rates rise; they return to normal (homeostasis)
    Model answer: Resting: 76 beats per minute and 16 breaths per minute. After jumping jacks: 132 and 34. After 1 minute: 104; 2 minutes: 92; 3 minutes: 80. My graph shoots up and then curves back down. My muscles needed more oxygen and made more carbon dioxide, so my heart pumped faster and I breathed faster. When I rested, my body returned to its normal balance.
  2. What happened to the inside balloon when you pulled the bottom balloon down, and when you pushed it up? Which body part does each balloon stand for, and how is the model unlike real lungs?
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Pulling down: balloon inflates; pushing up: it deflates; Inside balloon = lung, bottom balloon = diaphragm, bottle = chest; A limit (one balloon vs. two lungs; no alveoli or blood; ribs don't move)
    Model answer: When I pulled the bottom balloon down, the inside balloon filled with air; when I pushed up, it emptied. The inside balloon is a lung, the bottom balloon is the diaphragm and the bottle is the chest. Unlike real lungs, there is only one balloon, it has no alveoli or blood vessels, and the bottle can't expand the way ribs do.

Think Deeper

  1. At high altitudes, like the mountains of Ethiopia or Colorado, each breath contains less oxygen. Visitors feel out of breath. Predict how a visitor's heart rate and breathing rate change, and explain why. Then explain why runners from high-altitude places like Kenya's Rift Valley may have an advantage in races at sea level.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Visitors' heart rate and breathing rate rise to deliver enough oxygen; Explains the cause: less oxygen per breath; Advantage: bodies that live at altitude often make more red blood cells, carrying more oxygen at sea level; Uses system interaction
    Model answer: A visitor's breathing rate and heart rate will rise, because each breath brings in less oxygen, so the body must breathe more and pump more blood to deliver the same amount to cells. People who live and train at high altitude, like many runners from Kenya's Rift Valley, often have bodies that make more red blood cells. When they race at sea level, where the air has more oxygen, their blood can carry extra oxygen to their muscles, which helps them run hard for longer.
  2. Charles Drew said there was no scientific reason to separate blood by the donor's race. Using what you know about blood (its parts and jobs), explain why he was right, and why it matters that scientists speak up when policies ignore evidence.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Blood is made of the same parts (plasma, red and white cells, platelets) in all people; what matters for transfusion is blood type, not race; Speaking up matters: policies based on prejudice instead of evidence can harm people and waste lifesaving resources; Clear reasoning
    Model answer: Everyone's blood has the same parts, plasma, red blood cells, white blood cells and platelets, doing the same jobs. What matters for a safe transfusion is blood type (A, B, AB or O, and Rh), and every blood type is found in people of every race. So separating blood by race had no scientific basis. It matters that scientists speak up, because a rule based on prejudice instead of evidence could waste blood and cost lives, and Drew used his expertise to tell the truth.

Exit Check

  1. Which blood vessels carry blood away from the heart?
    Answer: arteries
    A for away.
  2. Where in the lungs does gas exchange take place?
    Answer: in the alveoli
    Tiny air sacs wrapped in capillaries.
  3. You count 21 beats in 15 seconds. What is your heart rate in beats per minute?
    Answer: 84 beats per minute
    21 × 4 = 84.
  4. Explain how the circulatory and respiratory systems work together to supply your cells with oxygen.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Respiratory system brings oxygen into the alveoli; blood in capillaries picks it up; Circulatory system pumps the oxygen-rich blood to body cells (and carries CO2 back)
    Model answer: The lungs bring oxygen into the alveoli, where blood in the capillaries picks it up. Then the heart pumps that oxygen-rich blood to every cell and brings carbon dioxide back to be breathed out.

Answer key: printable page

  1. Compare arteries, veins and capillaries.
    Answer: Arteries carry blood away from the heart under high pressure; veins return it with valves; capillaries are one cell thick for exchange.
  2. Trace a drop of blood from the right side of the heart back to the right side.
    Answer: Right heart → lungs (pick up O2) → left heart → aorta → body capillaries (drop off O2) → veins → right heart.
  3. Name the four parts of blood and the job of each.
    Answer: Plasma (carries materials), red blood cells (oxygen), white blood cells (fight infection), platelets (clotting).
  4. Explain how the diaphragm makes you inhale.
    Answer: It contracts and moves down, making the chest larger, so air pressure drops and air rushes in.
  5. You count 26 beats in 15 seconds. Find your heart rate.
    Answer: 26 × 4 = 104 beats per minute.
  6. What did Charles Drew contribute to medicine?
    Answer: Methods for processing and storing blood plasma; led Blood for Britain; first director of the American Red Cross blood bank; opposed segregating blood by race.
