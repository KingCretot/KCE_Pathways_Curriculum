Grade 6 · Week 18 · Parent Guide

Week 18: Adding and Subtracting Integers, Counterclaims, Heart and Lungs, and Rome

Math calculates with negative numbers and tracks a loan and a credit card below zero. ELA learns to answer the other side of an argument, using Thomas Paine's Common Sense. Science follows blood and air through the circulatory and respiratory systems, with a heart-rate lab and a model lung. Social Studies explores the Roman Republic, Cincinnatus and the rise of the empire.

How scoring works: each lesson scores itself. Your child gets two tries per question: full points on the first try, half points on the second, then the answer and explanation appear. A score of 85 or higher means the lesson is mastered. Written answers go to Jonathan, who scores them with a rubric.

Time this week: About 60 minutes of live lessons per subject, plus a hands-on activity, Think Deeper questions and an Exit Check for each.

Gather this week: A deck of playing cards (remove face cards) or 20 index cards numbered 1 to 10 twice, two colors of counters (beans, coins or paper squares, about 20 of each), painter's tape or chalk for a number line from −20 to 20 (or paper), a family member, and paper and pencil (Math); two sheets of paper labeled FOR and AGAINST, a soft ball or rolled-up socks, a timer, a family member or two, and index cards and a pencil (ELA); a timer or stopwatch, graph paper and a pencil, an empty clear plastic bottle (about 16 to 20 ounces), 2 balloons, a rubber band, scissors (used by an adult) and tape (Science); index cards labeled CONSUL, CONSUL, SENATOR, TRIBUNE and CITIZEN, 2 or more family members, a clear plastic cup or jar, small gravel or pebbles, sand, larger flat stones or crackers, soil, and paper and pencil (Social Studies).

Heads up: Science includes 1 minute of jumping jacks: your child should stop and tell you if they feel dizzy, short of breath or in pain, and should skip the exercise if a doctor has advised against hard exercise. An adult cuts the bottle. Check for latex allergies before using balloons, and keep balloons and pieces away from young children and pets. ELA's ball toss should be gentle and underhand, away from lamps and windows. Social Studies' road model: throw the materials outdoors or in the trash, not down the sink, keep gravel, pebbles and crackers away from young children and pets, and don't eat the crackers.

ELA: Counterclaims and Rebuttals

Your child learns to state an opposing view fairly, concede what is true and rebut with reasons and evidence, spots the straw-man fallacy, analyzes how Thomas Paine answered his opponents in Common Sense (1776), considers how enslaved people and Native nations saw the Revolution, and writes a counterclaim paragraph.

Ask at dinner: "What's the strongest thing the other side could say, and how would you answer it?"

Try it together: Play Rebuttal Ping-Pong on a real family question, then switch sides.

Signs it's clicking: They begin a rebuttal with "It is true that ... However ..." Signs they need help: They twist the other side into something silly. Ask, "Would someone who disagrees with you really say that?"

Math: Adding and Subtracting Integers

Your child adds integers with counters and number lines, learns the rules for same and different signs, subtracts by adding the opposite, finds temperature and elevation changes, and tracks a loan and a credit card balance below zero, including why interest makes debt grow.

Ask at dinner: "Are there more positives or more negatives? What is the opposite of the number you're subtracting?"

Try it together: Play the integer card game: black cards positive, red cards negative.

Signs it's clicking: They explain why 10 − (−4) = 14 with a story about canceling a debt. Signs they need help: They write −9 + 4 = −13. Have them model it with counters and remove zero pairs.

Life Science: Circulatory and Respiratory Systems

Your child learns the parts of the heart, blood vessels and blood, traces blood's double loop through the lungs and body, follows air to the alveoli, and measures how heart rate and breathing respond to exercise. They also learn about Dr. Charles Drew's work on blood storage and Dr. Daniel Hale Williams's early heart surgery.

Ask at dinner: "Why does your heart beat faster when you run?"

Try it together: Take each other's pulse at rest and after exercise and compare.

Signs it's clicking: They trace a drop of blood from the right side of the heart to the lungs and around the body. Signs they need help: They think blood in veins is blue. All blood is red; diagrams use blue to show oxygen-poor blood.

World and American History: Rome: Republic to Empire

Your child connects Rome's location to its growth, studies the Republic's consuls, Senate, assemblies and tribunes, learns how plebeians won rights and the Twelve Tables, reads Livy on Cincinnatus and connects him to George Washington, and explains how Julius Caesar and Augustus turned the Republic into an empire.

Ask at dinner: "How did the Romans try to keep anyone from becoming a king, and why did it fail?"

Try it together: Run the family senate with a tribune's veto.

Signs it's clicking: They name features of the U.S. government borrowed from Rome. Signs they need help: They call Julius Caesar the first emperor. Augustus was; Caesar was dictator for life.
