Unit 4: The Mediterranean World · Week 18 · World and American History · Teacher Plan

Rome: Republic to Empire

Lesson length: 60 minutes live, plus the after-class interactive lesson

Objectives

  • Explain how Rome's location helped it grow.
  • Describe the government of the Roman Republic (consuls, Senate, assemblies, tribunes) and the struggle between patricians and plebeians.
  • Explain the importance of the Twelve Tables and the idea of the rule of law.
  • Explain how and why the Republic became an empire under Julius Caesar and Augustus.
  • Analyze Livy's account of Cincinnatus and trace Roman influences on the United States.

Standards

  • SS.6.W.3.10: Describe the government of the Roman Republic and its contribution to the development of democracy (Senate, Consuls, Tribunes, Assemblies, Twelve Tables).
  • SS.6.W.3.11: Explain the transition from Roman Republic to empire and Imperial Rome, and compare Roman life and culture under each one.
  • SS.6.W.3.12: Explain the causes for the growth and longevity of the Roman Empire.
  • SS.6.C.1.2: Identify how the government of the Roman Republic contributed to the development of democratic principles (separation of powers, rule of law, representative government, civic duty).
  • SS.6.G.2.4: Explain how the geographical location of ancient civilizations contributed to the culture and politics of those societies.
  • SS.6.W.1.3: Interpret primary and secondary sources.

Materials

  • The interactive lesson
  • Role cards (consul, senator, tribune, citizen)
  • A clear cup, soil, large stones, gravel, sand, flat stones or crackers
  • Paper and pencil
  • Printable practice page

Vocabulary

  • republic: A government in which citizens elect leaders to make decisions for them.
  • patrician: A member of Rome's wealthy, noble families.
  • plebeian: An ordinary Roman citizen: a farmer, worker or merchant.
  • consul: One of two elected leaders who ran the Roman Republic for one year.
  • veto: Latin for "I forbid": the power to block a decision.
  • dictator: In the Roman Republic, an emergency leader given full power for up to six months.
  • Pax Romana: "Roman Peace": about 200 years of relative peace in the empire, beginning with Augustus.

Key questions to ask

  • How did Rome keep one person from becoming king, and why did it eventually fail?
  • Who had a voice in the Republic, and who didn't?
  • What was Livy's purpose in telling this story?
  • What did the United States borrow from Rome?

Common mistakes to watch for

  • Rome was always an empire.
  • A Roman dictator was the same as a modern dictator.
  • Julius Caesar was the first emperor.
  • All Romans were citizens who could vote.

Support (if the student is struggling)

  • Use a three-part organizer: kings, republic, empire.
  • Read the Livy excerpt one sentence at a time with the glossary.
  • Pre-fill the timeline dates.

Challenge (if the student is ready for more)

  • Read Livy 3.26-29 for the full Cincinnatus story and compare it with how Washington's resignation was reported.
  • Research how a Roman aqueduct kept water flowing downhill for many miles.

Background for the teacher

Dates: traditional founding 753 BCE; Republic 509 BCE; First Secession of the Plebs 494 BCE; Twelve Tables about 451-450 BCE; Cincinnatus's dictatorship 458 BCE (Livy 3.26-29); Caesar crosses the Rubicon January 49 BCE; assassinated March 15, 44 BCE; Octavian receives the name Augustus January 16, 27 BCE. Roman roads: estimates of about 50,000 miles (80,000 km) of paved main roads at the empire's height. The Society of the Cincinnati was founded in 1783 by Continental Army officers; Cincinnati, Ohio, was named in 1790 by Arthur St. Clair, a member. The Livy excerpt follows Spillan's 1853 translation (Project Gutenberg), keeping "honour" with a gloss; Spillan spells the name Quintius.

Answer key: interactive lesson

Reconnect

  1. Athens had a direct democracy. What is the name for a government where citizens elect officials to make laws for them?
    Answer: a representative democracy (a republic)
    Today's lesson: the Roman Republic.
  2. Early Rome was also a city-state. What Greek word for a city-state did you learn last week?
    Answer: polis
    Week 17.
  3. Which city on Egypt's Mediterranean coast in North Africa, founded by Alexander the Great, became a center of learning for Egyptian, Greek and Jewish scholars?
    Answer: Alexandria
    It later became part of the Roman Empire.
  4. Who was left out of Athenian democracy? Predict whether the Roman Republic will include them.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Women, enslaved people, foreigners; A reasonable prediction
    Model answer: Women, enslaved people and foreigners couldn't vote in Athens. I predict Rome also left out women and enslaved people.

Learn 1: From Seven Hills to a Republic

  1. On which river was Rome built?
    Answer: the Tiber
    About 15 miles inland.
  2. Give two ways Rome's location helped it grow. Use geography words.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Two valid advantages (river for water and trade; inland and safe from sea raids; hills for defense; fertile plains for farming; central location in the Mediterranean)
    Model answer: The Tiber River gave Romans fresh water and a route for trade, and being about 15 miles inland protected them from pirates. The fertile plains around Rome grew enough food for a big population.
  3. Rome became a republic in 509 BCE. Augustus became Rome's first emperor in 27 BCE. How many years did the Republic last?
    Answer: 482 years
    509 − 27 = 482 years.

Learn 2: How the Republic Worked

  1. What were the officials elected by plebeians who could block unfair laws called?
    Answer: tribunes
    Created after the plebeians walked out in 494 BCE.
  2. Why did Rome have two consuls instead of one, each elected for only one year?
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: To prevent one person from gaining too much power and becoming a king; Each could veto the other; short terms limited power
    Model answer: The Romans didn't want another king. With two consuls, each could veto the other, and electing them for only one year meant no one held power long enough to take over.
  3. Before the Twelve Tables, laws were not written down, and patrician judges decided what the law was. Explain why writing laws down and displaying them helped plebeians.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Everyone could know the laws; Judges couldn't change the rules to favor the powerful (rule of law)
    Model answer: When the laws were written and posted, everyone could read what they were. Patrician judges couldn't invent rules to favor themselves, so the same laws applied to plebeians and patricians.

Learn 3: Cincinnatus and Civic Virtue

  1. Cincinnatus was given power for six months but gave it up on the sixteenth day. About how many days of power did he give up? Use 30 days per month.
    Answer: 164 days
    6 × 30 = 180 days; 180 − 16 = 164 days.
  2. Livy opens by saying the story is worth hearing for people who think honor and virtue exist only where there is great wealth. What lesson does he want readers to take from Cincinnatus, and how do the details of the four-acre farm and the hut support it?
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Lesson: true honor and virtue come from character and service, not wealth; Details show Cincinnatus was a simple, poor farmer, yet he was "the sole hope of the Roman people"
    Model answer: Livy wants readers to see that honor comes from character and service, not money. Cincinnatus farmed only four acres and kept his toga in a hut, yet he was the person Rome trusted most, which proves you don't need riches to be great.
  3. Why would Americans in the 1780s and 1790s compare George Washington to Cincinnatus? What does this show about what they feared and valued?
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Washington, like Cincinnatus, gave up power when he could have kept it; They feared a king or dictator and valued leaders who serve and then step down
    Model answer: Washington gave up command of the army and later the presidency, just as Cincinnatus gave up being dictator. Americans had just fought a king, so they feared anyone grabbing too much power, and they valued leaders who served the country and then stepped aside.

Learn 4: From Republic to Empire

  1. Put these events in order.
    Answer: Rome becomes a republic → Twelve Tables written → Caesar crosses the Rubicon → Caesar is assassinated → Augustus becomes emperor
    509, about 450, 49, 44 and 27 BCE.
  2. Which title did the Senate give Octavian in 27 BCE?
    Answer: Augustus
    It means honored or revered one.
  3. Name two causes that led the Roman Republic to become an empire.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Two valid causes (gap between rich and poor; armies loyal to generals; civil wars; Caesar's seizure of power; Augustus taking control while keeping the Senate)
    Model answer: Generals like Caesar had armies loyal to them instead of to Rome, so they could seize power. And the growing gap between rich landowners and poor farmers led to unrest and civil wars that weakened the Republic.

Hands-On

  1. Describe your two laws, who used a veto and why, and the final results. Which part of the Roman government had the most power in your simulation, and is that what the Romans intended?
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Two laws and outcomes; Describes vetoes by the tribune and a consul; Reflects on the balance of power and the Romans' goal of preventing one-person rule
    Model answer: Law 1, dessert only on weekends: the senator advised yes, citizens voted yes, but the tribune (my sister) vetoed it because it was unfair to kids. Law 2, everyone reads 30 minutes a day: one consul vetoed the other's proposal to make it an hour. The vetoes had the most power because they could stop anything. That's what the Romans wanted: no single person could force a law through.
  2. Sketch and label your road layers. Where did the water go? Why were well-built roads important for holding an empire together? Compare Roman roads with the Persian Royal Road from Week 16.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Labeled layers; Observation about drainage; Roads moved armies, messengers, trade and officials quickly (connect to the Persian Royal Road)
    Model answer: From bottom: soil, big stones, gravel, sand, flat crackers. The water ran off the curved top and soaked down through the gravel instead of making mud. Roads let armies, messengers and traders move quickly, like the Persian Royal Road, so the emperor could control faraway provinces.

Think Deeper

  1. The framers of the U.S. Constitution studied the Roman Republic. Identify at least three features of the U.S. government that resemble Rome's, and one important way the United States tried to avoid what happened to Rome.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Three accurate parallels (Senate; elected leaders with limited terms; veto power; written laws/rule of law; checks and balances; separation of powers); One way to avoid Rome's fate (civilian control of the military, term limits, peaceful transfer of power, a written constitution that is hard to change); Clear explanation
    Model answer: The United States has a Senate, like Rome. The president can veto laws, like Roman consuls and tribunes, and power is divided among branches that check each other, just as Rome divided power among consuls, the Senate and assemblies. Our laws are written down for all to see, like the Twelve Tables. To avoid Rome's fate, the Constitution makes the president the commander in chief, so the army answers to an elected civilian leader instead of to a general like Caesar, and presidents leave office peacefully when their terms end.
  2. Augustus ended a century of civil wars and began 200 years of peace, but he also ended the Republic. Was Augustus good or bad for Rome? Take a position, support it with evidence, and acknowledge the other view.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: A clear position; Evidence from the lesson (civil wars ended, Pax Romana, roads, trade; or end of elected government, one-man rule); Acknowledges the other view
    Model answer: Augustus was good for ordinary Romans in the short run but bad for the Republic. He ended the civil wars that had killed thousands, and the Pax Romana brought peace, roads and trade. But Romans lost the right to truly choose their leaders: the Senate became mostly for show. People who valued peace above all would praise him; those who valued self-government, like Cincinnatus's admirers, would see him as the end of Rome's greatest idea.

Exit Check

  1. What was the name for an ordinary Roman citizen, such as a farmer or worker?
    Answer: a plebeian
    Patricians were the wealthy nobles.
  2. What was the name of Rome's first written law code, displayed in the Forum?
    Answer: the Twelve Tables
    About 450 BCE.
  3. Who became Rome's first emperor in 27 BCE?
    Answer: Augustus (Octavian)
    Julius Caesar was dictator for life but never emperor.
  4. What does veto mean, and why was it important in the Roman Republic?
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Veto means I forbid: blocking a decision; It prevented any one leader or group from taking too much power
    Model answer: Veto means "I forbid." It let consuls and tribunes block decisions, so no single leader or group could take over.

Answer key: printable page

  1. Describe the four main parts of the Roman Republic's government.
    Answer: Two consuls (elected yearly, could veto each other), the Senate (patricians for life, advised and controlled money), assemblies (male citizens voted), tribunes (elected by plebeians, could veto).
  2. Compare patricians and plebeians, and explain how plebeians gained more rights.
    Answer: Patricians: wealthy nobles; plebeians: ordinary citizens. Plebeians walked out in 494 BCE and won tribunes; the Twelve Tables put laws in writing.
  3. What did Cincinnatus do, and why did Americans admire him?
    Answer: He led Rome as dictator in an emergency and gave up power after 16 days; Americans compared Washington to him for stepping down.
  4. Explain two causes of the change from republic to empire.
    Answer: Armies loyal to generals; civil wars; gap between rich and poor; Caesar's seizure of power; Augustus took control.
  5. Name three lasting Roman contributions.
    Answer: Roads, aqueducts, Latin (and Romance languages), written law, the idea of a republic and a senate.
  6. Who was excluded from political power in the Roman Republic?
    Answer: Women, enslaved people and (at first) plebeians in practice; non-citizens.
