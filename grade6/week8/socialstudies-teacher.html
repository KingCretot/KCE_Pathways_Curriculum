Unit 2: The Nile Valley and Ancient Africa · Week 8 · World and American History · Teacher Plan

Nubia and the Kingdom of Kush

Lesson length: 60 minutes live, plus the after-class interactive lesson

Objectives

  • Locate Nubia, its cataracts, and the sites of Qustul, Kerma and Napata on a map.
  • Describe the A-Group culture and explain the debate over the Qustul incense burner.
  • Explain how the Kingdom of Kerma (the first Kush) grew rich and powerful, and what the Western Deffufa shows.
  • Use the Sobeknakht inscription as evidence of Kush's military power.
  • Describe the changing relationship between Kush and Egypt: trade, war, conquest and independence.

Standards

  • SS.6.W.3.18: Describe the rise and fall of the ancient east African kingdoms of Kush and Axum and Christianity's development in Ethiopia.
  • SS.6.E.3.3: Describe traditional economies (Egypt, Greece, Rome, Kush) and elements of those economies that led to the rise of a merchant class and trading partners.
  • SS.6.G.2.5: Interpret how geographic boundaries invite or limit interaction with other regions and cultures.
  • SS.6.G.2.6: Explain the concept of cultural diffusion, and identify the influences of different ancient cultures on one another.
  • SS.6.G.2.4: Explain how the geographical location of ancient civilizations contributed to the culture and politics of those societies.
  • SS.6.W.1.3: Interpret primary and secondary sources.
  • C3 Framework D2.His.10.6-8: Detect possible limitations in the historical record based on evidence collected from different kinds of historical sources.

Materials

  • The interactive lesson
  • A map of Africa
  • Soil, water, dry grass or straw, molds (ice cube tray or paper cups), a bowl and spoon
  • A ruler and some books
  • Printable practice page

Vocabulary

  • Nubia: The region along the Nile south of Egypt, in today's southern Egypt and northern Sudan.
  • Kush: The name Egyptians used for the kingdoms of Upper Nubia, including Kerma and later Napata and Meroe.
  • Ta-Seti: "Land of the Bow," an Egyptian name for Nubia and for Egypt's southernmost region, honoring Nubian archers.
  • cataract: A rocky stretch of rapids in the Nile. Nubia lies along the six cataracts.
  • deffufa: A Nubian word for a huge mud-brick building; the Western Deffufa was Kerma's great temple.
  • trade route: A path used by traders to carry goods between places.
  • tribute: Payment or goods that a weaker ruler or people must give to a more powerful one.
  • independence: Freedom from control by another country.

Key questions to ask

  • What can archaeology tell us when a people left no writing?
  • Whose point of view does this source show?
  • How did the balance of power between Kush and Egypt change, and why?
  • Does borrowing ideas make a culture less important?

Common mistakes to watch for

  • Thinking Nubia was just a part of Egypt.
  • Believing Kerma was an Egyptian outpost (early researchers thought so; excavation proved otherwise).
  • Assuming royal records tell the whole truth.
  • Thinking that because Nubia lay south (upriver), it was "below" Egypt in importance.

Support (if the student is struggling)

  • Provide a labeled map of Nubia to keep.
  • Give a timeline card of the five main periods.
  • Pair the Sobeknakht story with a simple cause-and-effect chart.

Challenge (if the student is ready for more)

  • Research the black granite statues of Kushite kings found by Charles Bonnet's team at Kerma (Dukki Gel) in 2003.
  • Compare Kerma's royal burial mounds with the pyramids of Giza: what does each show about beliefs and power?

Background for the teacher

The A-Group flourished in Lower Nubia from about 3800 to 2900 BCE. The Qustul incense burner (Cemetery L, tomb 24, Oriental Institute, now ISAC Chicago) was interpreted by Bruce Williams (1980, 1986) as evidence that pharaonic kingship had Nubian roots; many scholars (for example, Wegner 1996) argue that Egyptian royal iconography is older and that A-Group rulers adopted it. Present both views; the debate continues. The Kingdom of Kerma (about 2500 to 1500 BCE; Classic Kerma about 1750 to 1580 BCE) was excavated by George Reisner (1913 to 1916) and Charles Bonnet (from the 1970s into the 2010s); the Western Deffufa's base measures about 52 by 27 m and its surviving height about 18 m. The Sobeknakht II inscription at Elkab (tomb 66/T10) was revealed during cleaning in 2003 and published by W. V. Davies (Egyptian Archaeology 23, 2003). Thutmose I campaigned in Kush around 1500 BCE; Kerma was destroyed under Thutmose III. The tomb of Huy (TT40) shows Nubian princes bringing tribute to Tutankhamun. The Napatan kingdom arose by the 9th to 8th century BCE at Jebel Barkal.

Answer key: interactive lesson

Reconnect

  1. Week 7: ancient Egyptian belongs to which large language family, shared with Hausa, Somali and Amharic?
    Answer: Afroasiatic
    Afroasiatic. Most of its branches are spoken in Africa, one more sign that Kemet was African.
  2. Week 7 said early Upper Egyptian cultures were closely related to the cultures of Lower Nubia. What does that suggest about how Egypt and Nubia began?
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: They shared roots or grew from related peoples and traditions
    Model answer: It suggests that Egypt and Nubia grew from related peoples who shared traditions like pottery and cattle herding, so they began as neighbors with common roots.
  3. Why did some writers in the 1800s want to cut Egypt out of African history and deny that it was a Black African civilization?
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Racism; to claim Black Africans could not build civilizations; to justify colonialism and slavery
    Model answer: They held racist beliefs that Black Africans could not build great civilizations, and that idea helped them justify colonialism and slavery.
  4. Complete the sentence.
    Answer: Kush
    Week 7 previewed that kings from Kush ruled all of Egypt as the 25th Dynasty.

Learn 1: The A-Group and the Qustul Debate

  1. Explain the two sides of the debate over the Qustul incense burner. What does the object prove, no matter which side is right?
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Williams: Nubian kings used royal symbols as early as or earlier than Egypt; kingship might have begun in Nubia; Others: older Egyptian symbols exist; Qustul rulers borrowed them, or the burner was made in Egypt and imported; Either way, Nubia had powerful kings and wealth about 3200 BCE
    Model answer: Bruce Williams argued the burner shows Nubian kings using royal symbols as early as or earlier than Egypt, so kingship might have started in Nubia. Other archaeologists say older Egyptian symbols exist, so Qustul's rulers borrowed them. Either way, it proves Nubia had powerful kings and great wealth around 3200 BCE.
  2. The Qustul cemeteries now lie under Lake Nasser (called Lake Nubia in Sudan). Besides ancient sites, what did the lake cost Nubian people, and why does that loss matter to historians?
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: More than 100,000 Nubians lost their homes and villages; Flooded sites like Qustul can no longer be studied, so evidence of Nubian history is lost
    Model answer: More than 100,000 Nubians had to leave their villages, and sites like Qustul were flooded. Historians lost evidence they can never dig up again, and Nubian families lost places tied to their own history.

Learn 2: Kerma: The First Kingdom of Kush

  1. Give two reasons Kerma became rich and powerful. Use details from the lesson.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Control of the Nile and trade routes between Egypt and the south; Valuable goods: gold, ivory, ebony, incense, cattle
    Model answer: Kerma controlled a long stretch of the Nile, so goods from central Africa had to pass through it on the way to Egypt. It also had valuable resources like gold, ivory, ebony and cattle to trade.
  2. Kerma left no written records of its own. How do historians learn about a people without writing? Name two kinds of evidence and what each can tell us.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Archaeology: buildings, tombs, pottery show wealth, beliefs, skills; Records written by neighbors (Egyptian texts), with their bias
    Model answer: Archaeology, like the Deffufa, the royal mounds and the pottery, shows Kerma's wealth, religion and skill. Egyptian texts that mention Kush tell about wars and trade, but they show Egypt's point of view, so historians compare them with the archaeology.
  3. The Western Deffufa's base is about 52 meters by 27 meters. About how many square meters does it cover?
    Answer: 1404 square meters
    52 × 27 = 1,404 square meters, about a quarter of an American football field.

Learn 3: When Kush Struck Egypt

  1. Why might Egyptian records have said little about Kush's invasion of Egypt? What does the Sobeknakht inscription teach about the bias of sources?
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Royal records celebrated victories and hid defeats; Sources reflect their makers' point of view; new evidence can change history
    Model answer: Egyptian kings wanted to look powerful, so official records celebrated victories and kept quiet about defeats. The Sobeknakht inscription shows that sources reflect who made them, and that a new discovery can change what historians know.
  2. How many years passed between Kush's attack (about 1575 BCE) and the inscription's discovery in 2003 CE?
    Answer: about 3,577 years (3,578 by simple adding)
    1,575 + 2,003 = 3,578, or 3,577 because there is no year 0.
  3. Look at what happened after Egypt conquered Kush. Give one way Egypt changed Nubia and one way Nubians kept their own identity.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Egypt took gold, collected tribute, raised Nubian princes in Egypt, built temples; Nubians kept their identity and later regained independence (around 1070 BCE)
    Model answer: Egypt took Nubia's gold and raised the sons of Nubian rulers at the Egyptian court. But Nubians kept their own identity, and when Egypt weakened around 1070 BCE, Kush became independent again.

Learn 4: Kush Rises Again: Napata

  1. Why was Jebel Barkal important to both Kushites and Egyptians?
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Both believed Amun lived inside the mountain; Made Napata a holy center that gave Kush's kings religious authority
    Model answer: Both peoples believed the god Amun lived inside Jebel Barkal, so the mountain was sacred. That made Napata a holy center and gave Kush's kings special religious authority.
  2. Kush borrowed Egyptian gods and hieroglyphs but kept Nubian burial customs and kingship. Earlier, Egypt borrowed from Nubia, too. Why do neighboring cultures borrow from each other, and does borrowing mean one culture is less important?
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Contact through trade, marriage, war leads to sharing ideas; Borrowing does not make a culture less important; all cultures borrow and adapt
    Model answer: Neighbors trade, marry and fight, so ideas travel both ways. Borrowing does not make a culture less important. Kush adapted Egyptian ideas in its own way, just as Egypt took Nubian archers, gold and traditions.
  3. Put these events in time order, earliest first.
    Answer: A-Group rulers are buried with royal symbols at Qustul → Kerma becomes the capital of the first Kingdom of Kush → Kush and its allies attack deep into Egypt → Thutmose I conquers Kerma → Kush becomes independent again as Egypt weakens → Kush's kings march north to rule Egypt as the 25th Dynasty
    About 3200, 2500, 1575, 1500, 1070 and 750 BCE.

Hands-On

  1. Record your results in a table: brick type, number of books held, and what happened in the drop test. Which brick was stronger?
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Data table for both brick types; Clear comparison; Identifies the stronger brick
    Model answer: Brick A (no straw): held 3 books, broke into pieces in the drop test. Brick B (with straw): held 6 books, chipped but stayed whole. Brick B was stronger.
  2. Explain why adding straw might make mud brick stronger, and why a dry climate like Nubia's helped the Western Deffufa last so long.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Straw fibers hold the mud together and stop cracks spreading; Little rain means less erosion, so mud brick survives
    Model answer: The straw fibers act like a net that holds the dried mud together, so cracks do not spread as easily. Nubia gets very little rain, so the mud bricks were not washed away, which is why the Deffufa still stands.

Think Deeper

  1. Write a paragraph explaining how the relationship between Kush and Egypt changed over time. Include at least four events or periods, in order, and explain what caused one of the changes.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Four or more accurate events or periods in chronological order; Explains a cause of one change; Shows the relationship went both ways (trade, war, conquest, independence); Clear organization with transitions
    Model answer: The relationship between Kush and Egypt changed many times over 3,000 years. Around 3200 BCE, the A-Group rulers of Qustul traded with Egypt and shared royal symbols. Later, from about 2500 BCE, the Kingdom of Kerma grew rich by controlling trade along the Nile. Around 1575 BCE, while Egypt was weak and divided, Kush's army attacked deep into Egypt, as the Sobeknakht inscription shows. Egypt struck back around 1500 BCE: Thutmose I conquered Kerma, and Egypt ruled Nubia for about 400 years, taking its gold. When Egypt weakened around 1070 BCE, Kush became independent again, and by about 750 BCE its kings marched north to rule Egypt. One cause of these changes was the balance of power: whenever one kingdom was divided or weak, the other grew stronger and expanded.
  2. Many older textbooks mention Nubia only as a place Egypt conquered. Using this week's evidence, write two sentences a better textbook should include about Nubia, and explain why including them matters.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Two accurate, specific sentences (Kerma's power, the Sobeknakht invasion, Qustul kings, independence); Explains why a fuller story matters (accuracy, fairness, African history)
    Model answer: 1. The Kingdom of Kerma ruled the Nile from the First to the Fifth Cataract and around 1575 BCE was strong enough to invade Egypt. 2. Kush regained its independence and later ruled all of Egypt as the 25th Dynasty. Including these facts matters because leaving them out makes African kingdoms seem weaker than they were and tells only Egypt's side of the story.

Exit Check

  1. What does Ta-Seti mean?
    Answer: the Land of the Bow
    Nubian archers were famous for their skill and served in Egyptian armies.
  2. What was the Western Deffufa, and what does it show about Kerma?
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: A huge mud-brick temple at Kerma; Shows wealth, organization and skilled builders
    Model answer: It was Kerma's giant mud-brick temple. It shows that Kerma was a rich, organized city with skilled builders and its own religion.
  3. What did the Sobeknakht inscription, found in 2003, reveal?
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Kush and its allies invaded deep into Egypt (about 1575 to 1550 BCE)
    Model answer: It revealed that an army from Kush and its allies invaded deep into Egypt around 1575 to 1550 BCE.
  4. Which Kushite capital stood beside Jebel Barkal, the "Pure Mountain"?
    Answer: Napata
    Napata, near the Fourth Cataract.
