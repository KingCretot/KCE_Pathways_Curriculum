Unit 2: The Nile Valley and Ancient Africa · Week 8 · Life Science · Teacher Plan

Plant Cells and Photosynthesis

Lesson length: 60 minutes live, plus the after-class interactive lesson

Objectives

  • Identify the cell wall, chloroplasts and central vacuole and explain the job of each.
  • Compare and contrast plant and animal cells.
  • Describe photosynthesis: its inputs, outputs and where it happens.
  • Plan and run a controlled leaf-disk investigation and explain the results with evidence.
  • Explain why photosynthesis matters for food webs and the air we breathe.

Standards

  • SC.6.L.14.4: Compare and contrast the structure and function of major organelles of plant and animal cells, including cell wall, cell membrane, nucleus, cytoplasm, chloroplasts, mitochondria, and vacuoles.
  • SC.8.L.18.1 (preview): Describe and investigate the process of photosynthesis, such as the roles of light, carbon dioxide, water and chlorophyll; production of food; release of oxygen.
  • SC.6.N.1.1: Define a problem from the sixth grade curriculum, use appropriate reference materials to support scientific understanding, plan and carry out scientific investigation of various types, such as systematic observations or experiments, identify variables, collect and organize data, interpret data in charts, tables, and graphics, analyze information, make predictions, and defend conclusions.
  • SC.6.N.2.2: Explain that scientific knowledge is durable because it is open to change as new evidence or interpretations are encountered.
  • NGSS MS-LS1-6: Construct a scientific explanation based on evidence for the role of photosynthesis in the cycling of matter and flow of energy into and out of organisms.

Materials

  • The interactive lesson
  • Fresh spinach, a straw or hole punch, baking soda, dish soap, a needle-free plastic syringe, two clear cups, a bright lamp, a timer
  • Optional: red, green and blue cellophane for the challenge design
  • Optional: a microscope and an onion skin or Elodea leaf
  • Printable practice page

Vocabulary

  • cell wall: A stiff layer outside the cell membrane of a plant cell that supports and protects it.
  • chloroplast: The organelle where photosynthesis happens; it contains the green pigment chlorophyll.
  • chlorophyll: The green pigment in chloroplasts that absorbs light energy.
  • central vacuole: A large sac in a plant cell that stores water and keeps the cell firm.
  • photosynthesis: The process plants, algae and some bacteria use to turn light energy, carbon dioxide and water into glucose and oxygen.
  • glucose: A sugar that stores energy; the food made by photosynthesis.
  • stomata: Tiny openings, mostly on the underside of leaves, that let carbon dioxide in and oxygen and water vapor out.
  • producer: A living thing that makes its own food, such as a plant.

Key questions to ask

  • What would happen to this plant cell without its wall? Without its vacuole?
  • Where does a tree's mass come from?
  • How do we know the oxygen came from photosynthesis?
  • Which variable did we change, and which did we keep the same?

Common mistakes to watch for

  • Plants get their food from the soil.
  • Plants have chloroplasts instead of mitochondria.
  • Plants carry out photosynthesis, and animals carry out respiration (plants respire too).
  • A tree's mass comes from the soil.
  • The cell wall replaces the cell membrane.

Support (if the student is struggling)

  • Provide a fill-in inputs and outputs chart.
  • Count the floating disks together for the first five minutes.

Challenge (if the student is ready for more)

  • Research the Florida seagrass and manatee recovery work since 2021 and explain what reducing pollution does for light levels.

Background for the teacher

Florida Fish and Wildlife Conservation Commission recorded a record 1,101 manatee deaths in 2021, many from starvation linked to seagrass loss in the Indian River Lagoon after repeated algal blooms reduced light. The leaf-disk assay is a standard classroom protocol: sodium bicarbonate supplies dissolved carbon dioxide, soap wets the hydrophobic leaf surface, and oxygen from photosynthesis accumulates in the spongy mesophyll, making disks buoyant; respiration also uses some oxygen, so results reflect net photosynthesis. In Florida, photosynthesis is formally a Grade 8 benchmark (SC.8.L.18.1); it is included here because plant cell organelles (SC.6.L.14.4) cannot be understood without the chloroplast's job. Van Helmont's willow experiment (published 1648): about 164 lb (74 kg) gained versus about 2 oz (57 g) of soil lost. Priestley (1771) and Ingenhousz (Experiments upon Vegetables, 1779).

Answer key: interactive lesson

Reconnect

  1. Which organelle releases energy from food for the cell?
    Answer: mitochondria
    Mitochondria release the energy stored in food (glucose). Today you will learn where that glucose comes from.
  2. Which part controls what enters and leaves every cell?
    Answer: cell membrane
    The cell membrane. Plant cells have one too, inside their cell wall.
  3. In last week's cell-as-a-city model, name the organelle that builds proteins and the one that packages and ships them.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Ribosomes build proteins; Golgi apparatus packages and ships
    Model answer: Ribosomes build proteins, and the Golgi apparatus packages and ships them.
  4. What did Henrietta Lacks's story teach about how scientists must treat people who give cells or tissue?
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Informed consent: ask permission and explain how cells will be used
    Model answer: Scientists must ask for permission, explain how the cells will be used and respect the person and their family.

Learn 1: Three Parts That Make Plant Cells Different

  1. Name each lettered part of the plant cell. Choose from: cell wall, cell membrane, chloroplast, central vacuole, nucleus, mitochondrion.
    Answer: A = cell wall, B = cell membrane, C = chloroplast, D = central vacuole, E = nucleus, F = mitochondrion
    A is the thick outer wall. B is the membrane just inside it. C is a green chloroplast. D is the large central vacuole. E is the nucleus. F is a mitochondrion, the orange bean shape with folds.
  2. A potted basil plant droops on a hot afternoon. An hour after watering, it stands up again. Explain what happened inside its cells.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Without water the central vacuoles shrank and stopped pressing on the cell walls, so the plant wilted; Water refilled the vacuoles, which pushed against the walls and made the cells firm again
    Model answer: Without enough water, the central vacuoles shrank and stopped pushing on the cell walls, so the cells went soft and the plant drooped. After watering, the vacuoles filled up again, pressed against the stiff walls and made the cells firm, so the plant stood up.
  3. A classmate says, "Plants have chloroplasts instead of mitochondria." Correct the statement and explain why plants need both.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Plants have both chloroplasts and mitochondria; Chloroplasts make food (glucose); mitochondria release energy from that food
    Model answer: That is not right: plants have both. Chloroplasts use light to make glucose, and mitochondria release the energy stored in that glucose so the cell can use it, day and night.

Learn 2: Plant and Animal Cells Compared

  1. A scientist looks at an unknown cell. It has a nucleus, mitochondria and a cell membrane, but no cell wall and no chloroplasts. Is it more likely a plant cell or an animal cell? Give two pieces of evidence.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Animal cell; Evidence: no cell wall and no chloroplasts (parts both share do not decide it)
    Model answer: It is more likely an animal cell, because it has no cell wall and no chloroplasts, which plant cells have. The nucleus, mitochondria and membrane do not help decide, because both kinds of cells have them.
  2. Root cells deep in the soil are plant cells, but they have few or no chloroplasts. Why does that make sense?
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Roots get no light underground, so chloroplasts would not help; Roots get food made in the leaves; their job is to take in water and minerals
    Model answer: Chloroplasts need light, and roots are underground in the dark, so chloroplasts would be useless there. Roots get sugar made in the leaves, and their job is to take in water and minerals.
  3. Explain how the cell wall and the central vacuole work together to help a plant stand up without a skeleton.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: The full vacuole pushes outward; The stiff wall pushes back, so the cell stays firm, like an inflated tire or a water balloon in a box
    Model answer: The full vacuole pushes outward like an inflated balloon, and the stiff wall pushes back. Together they make each cell firm, and millions of firm cells hold the plant up.

Learn 3: Photosynthesis: Making Food from Light

  1. Complete the summary of photosynthesis.
    Answer: water; light; glucose; oxygen
    carbon dioxide + water + light energy → glucose + oxygen.
  2. Count the atoms. How many oxygen atoms are on the left side of the equation 6CO 2 + 6H 2 O?
    Answer: 18 atoms
    6CO 2 has 6 × 2 = 12 oxygen atoms and 6H 2 O has 6 × 1 = 6, so 12 + 6 = 18. The right side also has 18: 6 in glucose and 12 in 6O 2 . Atoms are rearranged, never lost.
  3. A seedling grows into a tree weighing thousands of kilograms, but the soil around it barely changes in mass. Where did most of the tree's mass come from? Use what you know about photosynthesis.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Mostly from carbon dioxide in the air (plus water); Photosynthesis builds glucose and then cellulose and wood from those atoms; soil supplies only small amounts of minerals
    Model answer: Most of the mass came from carbon dioxide in the air, along with water. Photosynthesis joins those atoms into glucose, which the tree builds into cellulose and wood. The soil only gives small amounts of minerals.
  4. Return to the manatees. Explain the chain from algae blooms to starving manatees, using the words light, photosynthesis and producer.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Algae clouded the water and blocked light; Seagrass could not carry out photosynthesis and died; Seagrass is the producer manatees depend on, so they starved
    Model answer: Algae blooms clouded the water and blocked the light. Without light, the seagrass could not carry out photosynthesis to make food, so it died. Seagrass is the producer at the base of the manatees' food chain, so with no seagrass the manatees starved.

Learn 4: How Scientists Discovered It

  1. Van Helmont's willow gained about 74 kg while the soil lost only 57 g. What did this evidence disprove, and what did he miss?
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Disproved that plants get their mass mostly from soil; Missed carbon dioxide from the air (he credited water only)
    Model answer: It disproved the idea that plants grow by eating soil. He concluded the mass came from water, but he missed that most of it comes from carbon dioxide in the air.
  2. How did Ingenhousz's discovery add to Priestley's? What variable did Ingenhousz test?
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Priestley showed plants restore air; Ingenhousz showed this needs light and green parts; Variable tested: light (and green versus non-green parts)
    Model answer: Priestley showed that plants restore the air. Ingenhousz tested light and found plants do it only in light and only with their green parts, which showed that light is needed.

Hands-On

  1. Make a data table with the minute and the number of floating disks in Cup A and Cup B. What pattern do you see?
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Complete table for both cups; Clear description of the pattern
    Model answer: Minute 5: A 1, B 0. Minute 10: A 6, B 0. Minute 15: A 10, B 1. Disks in the baking-soda cup floated much faster than disks in plain water.
  2. Write a claim, evidence and reasoning (CER) explaining your results. Name the independent variable, the dependent variable and one variable you kept the same.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Claim: photosynthesis needs carbon dioxide (or light, for the dark trial); Evidence: specific counts from the data table; Reasoning: photosynthesis made oxygen, which collected in the disks and made them float; Independent variable: baking soda (carbon dioxide) or light; dependent: number of floating disks; controlled: same light, leaves, water amount, soap, time
    Model answer: Claim: Spinach disks carry out photosynthesis faster when carbon dioxide is available. Evidence: After 15 minutes, all 10 disks floated in the baking-soda cup, but only 1 floated in plain water. Reasoning: Baking soda adds carbon dioxide to the water. Photosynthesis used it to make oxygen, and the oxygen bubbles filled the disks and made them float. Without carbon dioxide, little photosynthesis happened. The independent variable was baking soda, the dependent variable was the number of floating disks, and I kept the light, the spinach and the amount of water the same.

Think Deeper

  1. Trace the energy in your breakfast back to the Sun. Pick two foods (one from a plant and one from an animal) and explain each chain step by step.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Plant food traced to photosynthesis capturing light; Animal food traced through the animal eating plants back to photosynthesis; Uses producer and glucose or stored energy correctly; Clear step-by-step reasoning
    Model answer: My oatmeal comes from oats. The oat plant used light energy in photosynthesis to make glucose, which it stored as starch in its seeds, and I ate the seeds. My egg came from a chicken. The chicken ate corn and seeds, and those plants made their food through photosynthesis using sunlight. So the energy in the egg passed from the Sun to the plants, which are producers, to the chicken, and then to me.
  2. Design your own leaf-disk experiment to test whether light color (for example red, green or blue cellophane) changes the rate of photosynthesis. Write a question, a hypothesis, your variables and how you would measure the results.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Testable question and hypothesis with reasoning; Independent variable (light color), dependent variable (disks floating over time), controlled variables; Clear measurement plan with repeated trials
    Model answer: Question: Does light color change how fast spinach disks float? Hypothesis: Disks under green light will float slowest, because leaves look green when they reflect green light instead of absorbing it. Independent variable: the color of cellophane over each cup. Dependent variable: the number of floating disks each minute for 15 minutes. Controlled: same lamp and distance, same baking-soda solution, same number of disks, same spinach. I would repeat each color three times and compare the averages.

Exit Check

  1. Name the three parts plant cells have that animal cells lack, with one job for each.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Cell wall: support and protection; Chloroplast: photosynthesis, makes food; Central vacuole: stores water, keeps cell firm
    Model answer: The cell wall supports and protects the cell, chloroplasts make food by photosynthesis, and the central vacuole stores water and keeps the cell firm.
  2. List the inputs and outputs of photosynthesis.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Inputs: carbon dioxide, water, light energy; Outputs: glucose, oxygen
    Model answer: Inputs: carbon dioxide, water and light energy. Outputs: glucose and oxygen.
  3. Which green pigment in chloroplasts absorbs light energy?
    Answer: chlorophyll
    Chlorophyll absorbs light and reflects green, which is why leaves look green.
  4. Predict what would happen to a cup of leaf disks kept completely dark, and explain why.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Few or no disks float; No light means no photosynthesis, so no oxygen is made
    Model answer: Few or none would float, because without light photosynthesis cannot happen, so no oxygen bubbles form in the disks.

Answer key: printable page

  1. Draw a plant cell and label the cell wall, cell membrane, chloroplast, central vacuole, nucleus and a mitochondrion.
    Answer: Wall outside the membrane; green chloroplasts; one large central vacuole; nucleus; mitochondrion.
  2. Name two parts both plant and animal cells have and two parts only plant cells have.
    Answer: Both: nucleus, cell membrane, mitochondria, cytoplasm. Plant only: cell wall, chloroplasts, central vacuole.
  3. Write the word equation for photosynthesis.
    Answer: carbon dioxide + water + light energy → glucose + oxygen
  4. Why do roots have few or no chloroplasts?
    Answer: Roots are in the dark underground, so chloroplasts would not help.
  5. In the leaf-disk activity, why did the disks float?
    Answer: Photosynthesis made oxygen, which collected in the disks.
  6. How did Ingenhousz build on Priestley's work?
    Answer: He showed that plants restore the air only in light and only with their green parts.
