Unit 2: Reading and Writing Informational Texts · Week 8 · ELA · Teacher Plan

Summarizing Without Copying

Lesson length: 60 minutes live, plus the after-class interactive lesson

Objectives

  • Explain what makes a summary objective, brief and accurate.
  • Separate key points from minor details.
  • Paraphrase sentences by changing words and sentence structure, not just swapping synonyms.
  • Spot copied wording and explain why copying without credit is plagiarism.
  • Write a 60- to 80-word objective summary of an informational article.

Standards

  • ELA.6.R.2.2: Analyze the central idea(s), implied or explicit, and its development throughout a text.
  • ELA.6.R.3.2: Paraphrase content from grade-level texts.
  • ELA.6.C.4.1: Conduct research to answer a question, drawing on multiple reliable and valid sources, and refocusing the inquiry when appropriate (preview).
  • ELA.6.V.1.2: Apply knowledge of Greek and Latin roots and affixes to determine meanings of words and phrases in grade-level content.
  • ELA.K12.EE.1.1: Cite evidence to explain and justify reasoning.

Materials

  • The interactive lesson
  • A pencil and highlighter
  • Sticky notes, index cards in three sizes and a timer
  • A short article from home or the library
  • Printable practice page

Vocabulary

  • summary: A short restatement of the central idea and key points of a text, in your own words.
  • paraphrase: To restate one idea or passage in your own words and sentence structure.
  • objective: Based on facts in the text, without your opinions or feelings.
  • key point: An important idea that supports the central idea.
  • minor detail: A small fact, example or description that is interesting but not essential.
  • plagiarism: Presenting someone else's words or ideas as your own without giving credit.
  • roots aero, naut, graph, auto: aero = air (aeronautics), naut = sailor or ship (astronaut), graph = write (biography), auto = self (autograph).

Lesson flow

6 minReconnect
Run the Week 7 Reconnect questions.
4 minHook
Ask the student to summarize a favorite movie in one minute. Notice what they leave out.
12 minRead
The student reads the article twice, marking one key sentence per paragraph.
10 minKey points
Sort key points and minor details aloud. Remove the opinion and the invented fact.
12 minParaphrase
Model the patchwriting example, then the 4-word check. Have the student paraphrase with the text covered.
12 minWrite
The student drafts the summary, then checks it against the article.
4 minAssign
Assign the Summary Shrink Ray and Think Deeper.

Key questions to ask

  • If you removed this detail, would the reader still understand?
  • Is this the author's idea or your opinion?
  • Which words came straight from the text?
  • Could someone who never read it understand your summary?

Common mistakes to watch for

  • Swapping synonyms makes a sentence your own.
  • A summary retells everything in order.
  • Adding your opinion makes a summary better.
  • Plagiarism only means copying whole paragraphs.

Support (if the student is struggling)

  • Provide a summary frame: The article ___ explains how ___. First, ___. Then ___. Finally, ___.
  • Let the student say the summary aloud before writing.
  • Highlight copied 4-word strings for the student to rewrite.

Challenge (if the student is ready for more)

  • Write a 25-word summary and a 100-word summary and compare what changed.
  • Compare the article with a biography of Bessie Coleman from the library: what details does each include or leave out?

Background for the teacher

The article is original, written for this lesson from established facts. Bessie Coleman (January 26, 1892 to April 30, 1926) was born in Atlanta, Texas, attended the Colored Agricultural and Normal University in Langston, Oklahoma, for one term, and moved to Chicago around 1915 to 1916. Encouraged by Robert S. Abbott of the Chicago Defender, she studied French and trained at the Caudron brothers' school at Le Crotoy, receiving her license from the Fédération Aéronautique Internationale on June 15, 1921. Her refusal to perform at a segregated event in Orlando is documented in biographies (Doris L. Rich, Queen Bess, 1993). She died in Jacksonville when a loose wrench jammed the controls of her Curtiss JN-4; her mechanic and publicity agent William Wills also died. William J. Powell founded the Bessie Coleman Aero Club in Los Angeles in 1929. The USPS Black Heritage stamp was issued in 1995. The 4-word check is a teaching heuristic, not a formal rule; teach it as a warning sign. Confirm current B.E.S.T. benchmark codes on CPALMS before printing.

Answer key: interactive lesson

Reconnect

  1. Name the five text structures from last week and one signal word for each.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Sequence, cause and effect, compare and contrast, problem and solution, description, each with an accurate signal word
    Model answer: Sequence (first), cause and effect (because), compare and contrast (however), problem and solution (one solution), description (for example).
  2. "Because the money ran out, she left college and moved to Chicago." Which text structure do these signal words show?
    Answer: cause and effect
    "Because" signals cause and effect: the money running out caused her to leave.
  3. In Week 6 you learned that a central idea is different from a topic. Explain the difference with an example.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Topic: a word or phrase (what it is about); Central idea: a full sentence telling the main point about the topic
    Model answer: A topic is a word or phrase, like "the Great Green Wall." The central idea is a sentence about it, like "The Great Green Wall changed from planting a line of trees to helping farmers restore their own land."

Learn 1: Read the Article

  1. Write the central idea of the article in one sentence. Remember: a central idea is a full sentence, not a topic.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Bessie Coleman overcame racism and sexism (learning French, going to France) to become the first Black woman pilot and inspired others
    Model answer: Bessie Coleman overcame racism and sexism by learning French and training in France to become the first African American woman pilot, and her courage opened the way for other Black pilots.
  2. Most of the article follows one text structure from last week. Which one, and what clues show it?
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Sequence or chronological; Clues: dates and years in order (1892, 1915, 1920, 1921, 1926, 1929, 1995), words like back, then
    Model answer: Sequence, because it tells her life in time order with dates like 1892, 1915, 1921 and 1926.
  3. Use the roots aero, naut, graph and auto to complete the sentences.
    Answer: aeronautics; autograph; biography
    aero (air) + naut (sailing) = sailing the air; auto (self) + graph (write) = writing by oneself; bio (life) + graph (write) = life writing.

Learn 2: Key Points or Minor Details?

  1. Sort these into key points or minor details, and explain one choice: (1) She worked as a manicurist in a barbershop. (2) She learned French and trained in France because U.S. schools refused her. (3) Newspapers called her "Queen Bess." (4) She died in a crash in 1926 before she could open her flight school.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Key: 2 and 4; Minor: 1 and 3; Explains one choice using the central idea
    Model answer: Key points: 2 and 4. Minor details: 1 and 3. Number 2 is key because it explains how she overcame being refused, which is the central idea. The nickname is interesting but not needed to understand her story.
  2. Find the two sentences that do not belong in an objective summary, and explain why: "Bessie Coleman earned her pilot's license in France in 1921. I think she was braver than any pilot today. She refused to perform at a show with separate entrances. She also invented the loop-the-loop."
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: "I think she was braver..." is an opinion; "She invented the loop-the-loop" is not in the text (and is not true)
    Model answer: "I think she was braver than any pilot today" is an opinion, so it is not objective. "She invented the loop-the-loop" is not in the article, and a summary must not add facts that are not in the text.
  3. Paragraph 7 has four details. Write one sentence that sums up the paragraph's key point without listing every detail.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Her dream lived on: others trained Black pilots and honored her after her death (one sentence, not a list)
    Model answer: After her death, Coleman's dream lived on as others trained Black pilots in her name and honored her memory.

Learn 3: Paraphrase, Don't Patch

  1. Original: "She used her fame to fight unfairness." A student wrote: "She used her fame to battle injustice." Is this a true paraphrase? Explain using the 4-word check.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: No, it is patchwriting; "She used her fame to" is 5 words in a row copied; same structure with two synonyms
    Model answer: No. "She used her fame to" is five words in a row from the original, and the sentence has the same structure with only two words swapped. That is patchwriting.
  2. Paraphrase this sentence. Change both the words and the structure: "So Coleman studied French at night, saved her pay and found supporters to help with the cost."
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Same meaning (learned French, saved money, got financial help); Different structure and wording; no 4 words in a row copied
    Model answer: To pay for the trip and prepare for school in France, Coleman took evening French classes, put money aside and got help from backers.
  3. Paraphrase: "She dreamed of opening a flight school so that other Black Americans would not have to leave the country to learn."
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Same meaning; Different structure and wording; no 4 words in a row copied
    Model answer: Coleman hoped to start a school in the United States where Black students could learn to fly without traveling abroad as she had.

Learn 4: Write and Check a Summary

  1. Write a 60- to 80-word summary of the article. Start with the title and central idea. Then cover the text and write the key points in your own words.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Opens with title and central idea; Includes key points in order: refusal in the U.S., learning French and training in France, 1921 license, stunt flying and fighting segregation, 1926 death, legacy; Objective: no opinions or added facts; In own words: no 4 or more words in a row copied; 60 to 80 words
    Model answer: The article "Brave Bessie: The Pilot Who Learned French to Fly" explains how Bessie Coleman overcame racism and sexism to become the first African American woman pilot. Because American schools would not teach her, she learned French and trained in France, earning her license in 1921. Back home, she performed daring air shows and refused to fly before segregated crowds. She died in a 1926 crash, but others later trained Black pilots in her honor.
  2. Check your summary. Name one phrase you rewrote because it was too close to the original, and one detail you chose to leave out. Why did you leave it out?
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Names a specific phrase revised; Names a minor detail left out with a reason tied to the central idea
    Model answer: I first wrote "turned her down because she was Black and a woman," which copied the article, so I changed it to "would not teach her." I left out that she was a manicurist because it does not explain how she became a pilot.

Hands-On

  1. Copy your three summaries (100, 50 and 20 words) here, with the article's title and source.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Three summaries of decreasing length; Each keeps the central idea; In own words; Title and source named
    Model answer: Article: "Florida's Manatees Are Making a Comeback" (local newspaper). 100 words: ... 50 words: ... 20 words: Fewer manatees are dying in Florida because seagrass is being replanted and pollution is being cut, but they still face dangers.
  2. What did you cut each time you shrank your summary? What stayed in all three, and why?
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Names what was cut at each step (examples, numbers, quotes); The central idea stayed because everything else supports it
    Model answer: First I cut the quotes and the exact numbers. Then I cut the examples of each cause. The central idea stayed in all three because every other detail only supports it.

Think Deeper

  1. Explain why copying a summary from a website, even with a few words changed, is unfair to the author and harmful to the student. Give at least two reasons.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Unfair to the author: takes credit for their words and work; Harms the student: does not build understanding or skill; dishonest; real consequences in school and work; Clear explanation
    Model answer: Copying is unfair to the author because it takes credit for words and ideas they worked to create. It also hurts the student: writing a summary is how you prove and build your understanding, so a copied summary teaches you nothing. In school and at work, plagiarism can lead to failing grades or losing trust, and a paraphrase with a few swapped words is still copying.
  2. Bessie Coleman learned French to reach her goal. Write a short paragraph explaining how learning another language opened a door for her, and describe one way a second language could open a door for you.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Accurate: she learned French because French schools would train her, which U.S. schools would not; A thoughtful personal connection; Clear paragraph
    Model answer: American flight schools refused Bessie Coleman, but French schools would train her, so learning French gave her the key to her goal. Without it, she could not have taken classes or passed her tests in France. For me, learning Spanish could let me talk with my neighbors and someday work as a nurse in a bilingual hospital.

Exit Check

  1. In your own words, what three things make a good summary?
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Any three: short, central idea and key points, own words, objective, accurate
    Model answer: It is short, it keeps the central idea and key points, and it is in my own words with no opinions.
  2. Paraphrase: "Crowds gasped as she flew loops and figure eights."
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Same meaning; Different wording and structure, no 4 words in a row copied
    Model answer: Her daring stunts in the air amazed the people watching.
  3. What is the word for presenting someone else's words or ideas as your own?
    Answer: plagiarism
    Plagiarism. Next week: how to give credit with citations.
  4. Name one minor detail from the article and explain why it does not belong in a short summary.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: A real minor detail (13 children, manicurist, Queen Bess, the stamp, flowers dropped on her grave); Reason: the main story makes sense without it
    Model answer: She was one of thirteen children. It is interesting, but the story of how she became a pilot makes sense without it.

Answer key: printable page

  1. What is the central idea of "Brave Bessie"?
    Answer: Bessie Coleman overcame racism and sexism by learning French and training in France to become the first African American woman pilot, opening the way for others.
  2. Name two key points and two minor details from the article.
    Answer: Key: U.S. schools refused her; she earned her license in France in 1921; she died in 1926. Minor: thirteen children; manicurist; Queen Bess.
  3. Paraphrase: "In late 1920, she sailed to France."
    Answer: Coleman crossed the Atlantic by ship near the end of 1920 to train in France.
  4. Explain the difference between paraphrasing and patchwriting.
    Answer: Paraphrasing changes words and structure; patchwriting keeps the author's structure and swaps a few words.
  5. Write a 40-word summary of the article.
    Answer: Answers should include the central idea, key points, own words and no opinions.
  6. Use the roots: what do astronaut and autobiography mean, part by part?
    Answer: astro (star) + naut (sailor) = star sailor; auto (self) + bio (life) + graph (write) = writing about one's own life.
