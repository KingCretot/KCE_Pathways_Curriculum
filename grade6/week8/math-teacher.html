Unit 2: Fractions and Ratios · Week 8 · Math · Teacher Plan

Ratios and Ratio Language

Lesson length: 60 minutes live, plus the after-class interactive lesson

Objectives

  • Write a ratio three ways (a to b, a:b, a/b) and describe it in words.
  • Tell the difference between part-to-part and part-to-whole ratios.
  • Find equivalent ratios and write ratios in simplest form.
  • Use a tape diagram to solve ratio problems.
  • Compare two deals by scaling them to the same quantity.

Standards

  • MA.6.AR.4.1: Given a real-world context, write and interpret ratios to show the relationship between two quantities using appropriate notation: a/b, a to b, or a:b where b ≠ 0.
  • MA.6.AR.4.3: Extend previous understanding of fractions and numbers to apply ratio and rate reasoning to solve real-world problems (tables, tape diagrams, double number lines).
  • MA.6.NSO.3.1 (review): Given a mathematical or real-world context, find the greatest common factor and least common multiple of two whole numbers.
  • MA.K12.MTR.2.1: Demonstrate understanding by representing problems in multiple ways.
  • Financial literacy: comparing prices and making buying decisions (Jump$tart Spending).

Materials

  • The interactive lesson
  • 20 counters or dry beans in two colors
  • Juice or lemonade concentrate, water, a measuring scoop, four clear cups
  • Two grocery ads or receipts with multi-buy prices ("3 for $5")
  • Printable practice page

Vocabulary

  • ratio: A comparison of two quantities, such as 2 cups of water for every 1 cup of rice.
  • part-to-part ratio: A ratio that compares one part of a group to another part, like tomatoes to peppers.
  • part-to-whole ratio: A ratio that compares one part to the whole group, like tomatoes to all plants.
  • equivalent ratios: Ratios that show the same comparison, like 2:3 and 4:6.
  • simplest form: A ratio whose two numbers have no common factor other than 1.
  • tape diagram: A drawing of equal-sized boxes that shows the parts of a ratio.
  • scale: To multiply (or divide) both quantities in a ratio by the same number.

Lesson flow

6 minReconnect
Run the Week 7 Reconnect questions.
5 minHook
Rice and water. If possible, show a real bag's cooking directions.
10 minRatio language
Lay out 8 red and 12 green beans. Name part-to-part and part-to-whole ratios aloud, in both orders.
10 minEquivalent ratios
Group the beans into 4 equal groups to show 8 : 12 = 2 : 3. Discuss why adding does not work.
10 minTape diagrams
Draw the 2 : 3 tape for 20 plants, then the trail mix problem.
10 minDeals
Scale the mango deals to 15. Find a multi-buy price in a real ad and compare.
9 minMix and taste
Start the taste test together; the student finishes the record.

Key questions to ask

  • Which quantity comes first, and why does it matter?
  • Is this part-to-part or part-to-whole?
  • What did you multiply both parts by?
  • Is the cheaper price always the better choice?

Common mistakes to watch for

  • Reversing the order of a ratio.
  • Treating a part-to-part ratio as a fraction of the whole.
  • Adding the same number to both parts to make an equivalent ratio.
  • Converting a ratio like 3 : 2 to a mixed number.
  • Assuming the bigger package is always the better deal.

Support (if the student is struggling)

  • Use two colors of beans for every problem.
  • Provide a blank tape diagram template.
  • Keep a GCF list for numbers up to 50 handy.

Challenge (if the student is ready for more)

  • Find three multi-buy deals in a grocery ad and rank them.
  • A recipe uses flour, sugar and butter in the ratio 4 : 2 : 1. How much of each for a batch using 3 1/2 cups total?

Background for the teacher

Florida's B.E.S.T. benchmarks place ratio concepts in MA.6.AR.4. This lesson focuses on ratio notation and language (MA.6.AR.4.1) and on tape-diagram reasoning (part of MA.6.AR.4.3); unit rates (MA.6.AR.4.2) come next week, so deals are compared by scaling to a common quantity rather than by dividing. Verify exact benchmark wording on CPALMS before printing. The 1 : 2 rice-to-water ratio is a common stovetop guideline for long-grain white rice; package directions vary. Mortar mixes around 3 : 1 sand to cement are typical for general masonry. The juice concentrate ratio of 1 can concentrate to 3 cans water is standard on US frozen concentrate labels.

Answer key: interactive lesson

Reconnect

  1. How many 3/4-cup servings are in 6 cups of rice?
    Answer: 8 servings
    6 ÷ 3/4 = 6 × 4/3 = 24/3 = 8.
  2. 5/6 ÷ 2/3 = ? Type a mixed number.
    Answer: 1 1/4
    5/6 × 3/2 = 15/12 = 5/4 = 1 1/4.
  3. What is the greatest common factor (GCF) of 18 and 24? (Week 5)
    Answer: 6
    Factors of 18: 1, 2, 3, 6, 9, 18. Factors of 24: 1, 2, 3, 4, 6, 8, 12, 24. The greatest shared factor is 6. You will use the GCF to simplify ratios today.
  4. Last week a 1/4-cup serving was on the rice label, but our family cooked 3/4 cup. In your own words, why is it important to compare the label serving with what your family really uses?
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: The real number of servings and the real cost per meal depend on the amount actually used
    Model answer: Because the label's serving may be smaller than what we eat, so the bag gives fewer meals and each meal costs more than the label suggests.

Learn 1: Writing and Reading Ratios

  1. Use the garden bed to complete each ratio.
    Answer: 12 : 8; 12 : 20
    Peppers come first in both. All plants = 8 + 12 = 20.
  2. Is "tomatoes to all plants" a part-to-part or a part-to-whole ratio? Explain how you know.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Part-to-whole; It compares one group to the total of all groups
    Model answer: Part-to-whole, because it compares the tomatoes (one part) to all the plants together (the whole).
  3. Kofi says the ratio of tomatoes to peppers is 12:8. Explain his mistake.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: He reversed the order; the first quantity named goes first, so it is 8:12
    Model answer: He switched the order. Tomatoes are named first, so the tomato count comes first: 8:12.
  4. The ratio of tomatoes to peppers is 8/12. Does that mean 8/12 of the plants are tomatoes? Explain.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: No; 8/12 compares part to part; the fraction of plants that are tomatoes is 8/20
    Model answer: No. 8/12 compares tomatoes to peppers. The fraction of all the plants that are tomatoes is 8 out of 20, or 8/20.

Learn 2: Equivalent Ratios and Simplest Form

  1. Write 18 : 24 in simplest form.
    Answer: 3 : 4
    The GCF of 18 and 24 is 6. 18 ÷ 6 = 3 and 24 ÷ 6 = 4.
  2. Write 45 : 30 in simplest form.
    Answer: 3 : 2
    The GCF is 15: 45 ÷ 15 = 3 and 30 ÷ 15 = 2. Keep it as 3:2; do not change it to a mixed number, because a ratio compares two amounts.
  3. A juice label says to mix 1 can of concentrate with 3 cans of water. How many cans of water go with 4 cans of concentrate?
    Answer: 12 cans
    1 : 3 = 4 : 12. Multiply both parts by 4.
  4. Amara needs a bigger pot of rice. She changes 1 cup rice : 2 cups water to 2 cups rice : 3 cups water by adding 1 to each. Is her rice ratio the same? Explain and fix it.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Not the same; adding does not keep the comparison; Multiply both by 2: 2 cups rice to 4 cups water
    Model answer: No. Adding 1 to each changes the comparison: 2:3 has less water per cup of rice than 1:2. She should multiply both by 2 to get 2 cups of rice and 4 cups of water.

Learn 3: Solving Problems with Tape Diagrams

  1. A trail mix uses raisins and peanuts in the ratio 3 : 5. The bag holds 40 ounces. How many ounces are peanuts?
    Answer: 25 ounces
    3 + 5 = 8 boxes. 40 ÷ 8 = 5 ounces per box. Peanuts: 5 × 5 = 25 ounces.
  2. Mortar for a garden wall is 3 parts sand to 1 part cement. A builder uses 9 buckets of sand. How many buckets of cement are needed?
    Answer: 3 buckets
    Sand is 3 boxes and cement is 1 box. 9 ÷ 3 = 3 buckets per box, so cement is 3 buckets.
  3. The ratio of boys to girls in a homeschool co-op is 4 : 5. There are 12 boys. How many students are in the co-op in all?
    Answer: 27 students
    4 boxes = 12 boys, so each box is 3. Girls: 5 × 3 = 15. Total: 12 + 15 = 27 (or 9 boxes × 3).
  4. Explain why, in a tape diagram, all the boxes must be the same size.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Each box stands for the same amount, so the ratio of box counts equals the ratio of amounts
    Model answer: Each box has to stand for the same amount. If the boxes were different sizes, the number of boxes would not show the real ratio between the amounts.

Learn 4: Money: Comparing Deals with Ratios

  1. Store A sells 4 cans of beans for $3. Store B sells 6 cans for $5. Scale both deals to the same number of cans and decide which is the better deal. Show your work.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Common quantity 12 cans; A: 12 for $9; B: 12 for $10; Store A is the better deal
    Model answer: The LCM of 4 and 6 is 12. Store A: 4 for $3 × 3 = 12 for $9. Store B: 6 for $5 × 2 = 12 for $10. Store A is the better deal.
  2. Plantains are 2 for $1 at one stand and 7 for $4 at another. If you scale both to 14 plantains, how much do 14 cost at the cheaper stand?
    Answer: $7
    2 for $1 × 7 = 14 for $7. 7 for $4 × 2 = 14 for $8. The cheaper price for 14 is $7.
  3. A big box of 48 granola bars costs less per bar than a box of 12. Give two reasons a family might still choose the smaller box.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Two sensible reasons: they would expire or go stale before being eaten, not enough money now, no storage space, trying a new flavor, waste
    Model answer: The bars might go stale before the family eats 48, and they might not have enough money right now or room in the pantry for the big box.

Hands-On

  1. Record each cup's ratio, its simplest form, your prediction and your tasting rank. Did the results match your predictions?
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Ratios and simplest forms recorded correctly (A 1:3, B 1:3, C 1:5, D 2:3); Predictions and ranks recorded; Comparison of results with predictions
    Model answer: A 1:3 and B 2:6 = 1:3 tasted the same, medium. C 1:5 was weakest. D 2:3 was strongest. My prediction was right except I thought C and D would be closer.
  2. Explain why Cup A and Cup B taste the same even though Cup B has more of everything. Then explain why Cup D is stronger.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: A and B are equivalent (1:3 = 2:6): same amount of concentrate per part of water; D has 2 parts concentrate for only 3 parts water, which is more concentrate per part of water than 1:3 (2:6)
    Model answer: Cup B has double the concentrate and double the water, so each sip has the same mix as Cup A: 1:3 and 2:6 are equivalent. Cup D has 2 parts concentrate for only 3 parts water. Compared with 2:6, it has the same concentrate but half the water, so it is stronger.

Think Deeper

  1. A family recipe for jollof rice uses 3 cups of rice and 4 1/2 cups of broth for 6 people. You are cooking for 10. Use ratio reasoning to find how much rice and broth you need, and explain each step.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Finds rice for 1 or 2 people or uses a scale factor of 10/6; Rice: 5 cups; broth: 7 1/2 cups; Clear steps that keep the ratio of rice to broth the same
    Model answer: For 6 people the recipe uses 3 cups of rice, so that is 1 cup for every 2 people. 10 people is 5 groups of 2, so I need 5 cups of rice. Broth is 4 1/2 cups for 6 people, which is 1 1/2 cups for every 2 people. 5 × 1 1/2 = 7 1/2 cups of broth. The ratio of rice to broth stays 3 : 4 1/2, or 2 : 3, because 5 : 7 1/2 is also 2 : 3.
  2. Rice: Store A sells a 5-lb bag for $6. Store B sells a 2-lb bag for $3. Decide which is the better price per pound by scaling to the same number of pounds. Then name one situation where buying the other bag makes more sense.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Common quantity 10 lb: A $12, B $15; Store A is the better price; A sensible situation for buying the smaller bag (small family, limited money now, storage, trying a new kind)
    Model answer: Scale to 10 pounds: Store A, 5 lb for $6 × 2 = 10 lb for $12. Store B, 2 lb for $3 × 5 = 10 lb for $15. Store A is the better price. But a person living alone who rarely cooks rice, or who has only $3 to spend today, might buy the 2-lb bag.

Exit Check

  1. A class has 9 girls and 6 boys. Write the ratio of boys to girls in simplest form.
    Answer: 2 : 3
    Boys come first: 6 : 9. The GCF is 3, so 6 : 9 = 2 : 3.
  2. In the same class, write the ratio of girls to all students in simplest form.
    Answer: 3 : 5
    9 girls out of 15 students: 9 : 15 = 3 : 5. This is part-to-whole.
  3. Paint is mixed 2 parts blue to 5 parts white. How much white paint goes with 6 cups of blue?
    Answer: 15 cups
    2 : 5 = 6 : 15 (multiply both by 3).
  4. Which is the better deal: 3 notebooks for $5 or 4 notebooks for $6? Show how you scaled.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: 12 notebooks: $20 versus $18; 4 for $6 is the better deal
    Model answer: Scale to 12: 3 for $5 × 4 = 12 for $20; 4 for $6 × 3 = 12 for $18. 4 for $6 is the better deal.

Answer key: printable page

  1. A fruit bowl has 6 bananas and 10 oranges. Write the ratio of oranges to bananas three ways.
    Answer: 10 to 6, 10 : 6, 10/6 (simplest 5 : 3)
  2. Write the ratio of bananas to all fruit in simplest form.
    Answer: 6 : 16 = 3 : 8
  3. Write 28 : 42 in simplest form.
    Answer: 2 : 3 (GCF 14)
  4. Soil mix is 3 parts potting soil to 2 parts compost. How much compost goes with 12 cups of soil?
    Answer: 8 cups
  5. Beads are red and gold in the ratio 5 : 3. There are 48 beads. How many are gold? Draw a tape diagram.
    Answer: 8 boxes, 6 each; gold = 18
  6. Which is the better deal: 6 eggs for $2 or 10 eggs for $3? Scale to the same number of eggs.
    Answer: 30 eggs: $10 vs $9; 10 for $3 is better
