Week 8: Nubia and Kush, Plant Cells, Ratios and Summarizing

This week your child travels south of Egypt to Nubia and the Kingdom of Kush, discovers how plant cells make food from sunlight, learns the language of ratios and uses it to compare deals, and learns to summarize a text in their own words without copying.

How scoring works: each lesson scores itself. Your child gets two tries per question: full points on the first try, half points on the second, then the answer and explanation appear. A score of 85 or higher means the lesson is mastered. Written answers go to Jonathan, who scores them with a rubric.

Time this week: About 60 minutes of live lessons per subject, plus 30 to 45 minutes of after-class work per subject.

Gather this week: About 4 cups of soil with some clay in it, water, dry grass or straw, an ice cube tray or small paper cups, a bowl and spoon, a ruler and a few books (Social Studies; start the mud bricks early in the week because they need 3 to 5 days to dry); fresh spinach, a drinking straw or hole punch, baking soda, dish soap, a plastic syringe without a needle, two clear cups and a bright lamp (Science); lemonade or juice concentrate, water, a measuring scoop, four clear cups and a grocery ad with "3 for $5" style prices (Math); a short article from home or the library, sticky notes, index cards in three sizes and a timer (ELA).

Heads up: The ELA article tells how pilot Bessie Coleman was refused by American flight schools because she was Black and a woman, and that she died in a plane crash in Jacksonville in 1926. Science opens with the 2021 manatee deaths in Florida. Both are handled with care.

ELA: Summarizing Without Copying

Your child reads an article about Bessie Coleman, who learned French so she could earn her pilot's license in France in 1921. They separate key points from minor details, learn to paraphrase instead of swapping a few words, and write a short, objective summary.

Ask at dinner: "Tell me what you read in three sentences. What did you leave out, and why?"

Try it together: Listen to your child's one-sentence summary from the Shrink Ray activity without reading the article first. Can you tell what it was about?

Signs it's clicking: They write with the text covered and catch phrases that match the original word for word. Signs they need help: They copy a sentence and change one or two words. Ask them to close the text and say it out loud first.

Math: Ratios and Ratio Language

Your child writes ratios three ways, tells part-to-part from part-to-whole ratios, finds equivalent ratios and simplest form, uses tape diagrams, and compares deals like "3 for $4" and "5 for $7" by scaling them to the same quantity.

Ask at dinner: "Which deal is better, and how do you know without finding the price of one?"

Try it together: Taste-test the juice mixes together, and find one multi-buy deal in a grocery ad for your child to check.

Signs it's clicking: They multiply both parts of a ratio by the same number and keep the order straight. Signs they need help: They add the same number to both parts. Ask: does 2 cups rice to 3 cups water taste like 1 to 2?

Life Science: Plant Cells and Photosynthesis

Your child learns the three parts plant cells have that animal cells lack (cell wall, chloroplasts and a central vacuole), compares plant and animal cells, learns the inputs and outputs of photosynthesis, and runs the floating leaf-disk investigation.

Ask at dinner: "Where does a tree's weight come from: the soil, the water or the air?"

Try it together: Water a drooping houseplant together and ask your child to explain what is happening inside its cells.

Signs it's clicking: They explain that plants have both chloroplasts and mitochondria and trace food energy back to the Sun. Signs they need help: They say plants eat soil. Ask what Van Helmont's willow tree experiment showed.

World and American History: Nubia and the Kingdom of Kush

Your child explores Nubia, the land of the cataracts south of Egypt: the A-Group rulers of Qustul and the debate over their royal symbols, the Kingdom of Kerma and its giant mud-brick temple, a 2003 discovery showing Kush once invaded Egypt, Egypt's conquest of Nubia, and the rise of a new Kush at Napata.

Ask at dinner: "Why did the Egyptians call Nubia the Land of the Bow?"

Try it together: Help with the mud-brick test and ask why straw makes the bricks stronger.

Signs it's clicking: They describe Kush and Egypt as neighbors whose power rose and fell, not as ruler and ruled. Signs they need help: They think Nubia was just part of Egypt. Ask what the Sobeknakht inscription revealed.
