Week 26: Area, Research Questions, Traits and Inheritance, and the Olmec and Maya

Unit 6 begins. Math finds the area of triangles, quadrilaterals and composite shapes and prices a room makeover. ELA starts the year-end research presentation by turning topics into strong research questions. Science explores DNA, genes and how traits pass from parents to children. Social Studies crosses the Atlantic to the Olmec, who carved colossal stone heads, and the Maya, with their city-states, writing and zero.

How scoring works: each lesson scores itself. Your child gets two tries per question: full points on the first try, half points on the second, then the answer and explanation appear. A score of 85 or higher means the lesson is mastered. Written answers go to Jonathan, who scores them with a rubric.

Time this week: About 60 minutes of live lessons per subject, plus a hands-on activity, Think Deeper questions and an Exit Check for each.

Gather this week: A tape measure, graph paper and a ruler, colored pencils, scissors and a sheet of paper, optionally a calculator, and a home improvement store website or flyer for prices (Math); a timer, sticky notes or slips of paper (about 30), two markers in different colors, a large sheet of paper or a wall, a family member, and paper and pencil (ELA); paper and pencil, a mirror, two paper bags or cups, beads, buttons or paper squares in two colors (about 10 of each), a coin, and family members (Science); air-dry clay, salt dough or a new craft foam sheet, a pencil or craft stick for carving, dried beans, craft or pretzel sticks, and small shells or paper circles, paper and pencil, and a family member (Social Studies).

Heads up: Science asks your child to survey inherited traits such as freckles, dimples and earlobes in family members. If your family includes adoption, donors or other situations where this could be sensitive, your child can survey any willing group of people instead; please guide the activity so no one feels singled out. The ELA research presentation begins this week and is due in Week 30, so help your child choose a question they truly care about. Keep beans, beads, buttons, shells and small sticks away from young children (choking hazard) and pets, keep scissors away from little ones, and ask an adult to measure high walls instead of climbing on furniture.

ELA: Asking Research Questions

Your child learns the difference between a topic and a research question and between open and closed questions, narrows a topic step by step into a focused question, breaks it into sub-questions with a plan for where to look, and learns to refocus when research leads somewhere new. They choose the question for their Unit 6 research presentation.

Ask at dinner: "Why does this question matter to you, and who else would care about the answer?"

Try it together: Ask your child the "Why does this matter?" question during the question storm.

Signs it's clicking: Their question asks how or why and could be answered in 3 to 5 minutes with three sources. Signs they need help: They pick a topic ("sharks") instead of a question. Ask, "What do you want to know about sharks?"

Math: Area of Triangles, Quadrilaterals and Composite Figures

Your child learns why the area formulas for parallelograms (b × h) and triangles (½ × b × h) come from the rectangle, finds the area of trapezoids and L-shaped rooms by splitting them into simpler shapes, and prices flooring and paint for a real room, adding 10 percent extra for cuts.

Ask at dinner: "Which length is the height, and how do you know?"

Try it together: Measure a room together and compare your child's flooring price with a real store quote.

Signs it's clicking: They use the perpendicular height, not the slanted side. Signs they need help: They forget the ½ for triangles. Ask them to show two triangles making a parallelogram.

Life Science: Traits and Inheritance

Your child zooms from a cell to its nucleus, chromosomes and genes, tells inherited traits from acquired ones, learns how sexual and asexual reproduction pass on genes, explains dominant and recessive alleles, reads a family pedigree and learns about Rosalind Franklin's role in discovering DNA's shape.

Ask at dinner: "Is that trait something you were born with, something you learned, or both?"

Try it together: Do the bead babies activity together and see how often the recessive trait appears.

Signs it's clicking: They explain how a trait can skip a generation. Signs they need help: They think acquired traits like big muscles are passed on. Ask whether a pianist's baby is born knowing how to play.

World and American History: The Olmec and the Maya

Your child learns about Mesoamerica's geography and crops, the Olmec colossal heads and rubber ballgame, the Maya city-states and their rulers, Maya writing, bar-and-dot numbers and the zero, the burning of Maya books in 1562, the causes of the Classic Maya decline and the millions of Maya people who live in Mexico and Central America today.

Ask at dinner: "Can you show me how to write today's date in Maya numbers?"

Try it together: Let your child teach you to add with Maya bars and dots.

Signs it's clicking: They explain why the Maya did not "vanish." Signs they need help: They think the Maya were one empire. Remind them of the Greek city-states from Week 17.
