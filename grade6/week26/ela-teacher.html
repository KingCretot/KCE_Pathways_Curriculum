Unit 6: Research and Presentation · Week 26 · ELA · Teacher Plan

Asking Research Questions

Lesson length: 60 minutes live, plus the after-class interactive lesson

Objectives

  • Distinguish a topic from a research question, and closed questions from open ones.
  • Narrow a broad topic into a focused, researchable how or why question.
  • Break a research question into sub-questions and plan which kinds of sources could answer each.
  • Refocus a question when early research shows it is too broad, too narrow or unanswerable.
  • Choose and plan the question for your Unit 6 research presentation.

Standards

  • ELA.6.C.4.1: Conduct research to answer a question, drawing on multiple reliable and valid sources, and refocusing the inquiry when appropriate.
  • ELA.K12.EE.1.1: Cite evidence to explain and justify reasoning.

Materials

  • The interactive lesson
  • A timer, about 30 sticky notes, two markers, a large sheet of paper
  • Printable practice page

Vocabulary

  • inquiry: An investigation driven by a question.
  • open question: A question with many possible answers that needs explanation, often beginning with how or why.
  • closed question: A question with one short factual answer, such as yes or no or a single number.
  • sub-question: A smaller question that helps answer the main research question.
  • refocus: To change a research question based on what you learn.
  • scope: How much a question covers; whether it is broad or narrow.

Key questions to ask

  • Is this a topic or a question?
  • Could you answer it in 3 to 5 minutes with three sources?
  • What smaller questions do you need to answer first?
  • What would make you change your question?

Common mistakes to watch for

  • A topic is the same as a question.
  • Changing your question means you failed.
  • One good source is enough.
  • Googling the question word for word is research.

Support (if the student is struggling)

  • Provide question starters (How, Why, To what extent, What are the effects of).
  • Model one question ladder aloud.
  • Let students choose from a short list of topics if they are stuck.

Challenge (if the student is ready for more)

  • Write a research question that would require both a historian's and a scientist's methods.
  • Interview a librarian about how they help people narrow research questions.

Background for the teacher

This week starts the Unit 6 research presentation, due in Week 30 (see the Unit 6 projects page when it is posted). Week 9 introduced research questions and note cards; this week builds on that with open and closed questions, scope, sub-questions, planning by source type and refocusing, as ELA.6.C.4.1 requires. Students should keep their research log through Week 30.

How the lesson runs

During the live lesson, the student shares their screen and answers the Reconnect questions and the Learn checks live while you teach. The Hands-On activity, Think Deeper and Exit Check are completed after class.

Scoring

The interactive lesson scores itself: full points on the first try, half points on the second try, then the answer and explanation appear. Mastery is 85. Written responses arrive in your results email with the score; score them with the rubric below before the student moves on.

Answer key: interactive lesson

Reconnect

  1. In an analysis paragraph, what do we call your explanation of how evidence supports a claim?
    Answer: commentary
    Claim, evidence, commentary.
  2. What do we call a quotation or specific detail from a text that supports a claim?
    Answer: textual evidence
    Research uses evidence from many sources.
  3. Think of one question you still have after reading The Call of the Wild that the novel itself can't answer (about sled dogs, the Klondike, Jack London or anything else).
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: A real question the novel doesn't answer
    Model answer: How did real sled dogs survive temperatures of 60 below zero, and how were they treated during the gold rush?
  4. In Week 9 you wrote a research question and took note cards. What made your Week 9 question work, or what would you change about it now?
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Recalls the Week 9 question or process; Evaluates it honestly
    Model answer: My Week 9 question about the coral reef was a good start, but it was so big it covered the whole reef. Now I would ask why one reef area lost the most coral, which I could answer with specific evidence.

Learn 1: Open and Closed Questions

  1. Turn this closed question into an open one: "How many manatees visited Blue Spring last winter?"
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: A question that needs explanation or evidence; Keeps the same subject
    Model answer: Why have so many more manatees been visiting Blue Spring in winter over the past 30 years?
  2. If closed questions aren't good main research questions, how can they still help a researcher? Give an example.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Explains closed questions as sub-questions or fact-finding; A fitting example
    Model answer: Closed questions gather the facts you need to answer the bigger question. To explain why manatee numbers grew at Blue Spring, I'd first need to know how many came in 1990 and how many come now.

Learn 2: Narrowing a Topic: The Question Ladder

  1. Order these from broadest to most focused.
    Answer: space → the Moon → returning to the Moon → Why is NASA's Artemis program sending astronauts back to the Moon?
    Each step adds focus.
  2. Narrow the topic "African empires" into a focused, researchable question using what you learned in Unit 5.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: An open how or why question; Focused on a specific place, time or group; Researchable with several sources
    Model answer: How did the trade in gold and salt make the Mali Empire rich under Mansa Musa?
  3. Explain what is wrong with each question and fix one: (a) "Why is history important?" (b) "What color was Mansa Musa's favorite robe?"
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Explains the problem with (a); Explains the problem with (b); A fixed, researchable version of one
    Model answer: (a) is so broad that no presentation could answer it. (b) is too narrow and probably impossible to answer from any source. A better version: How did Mansa Musa's hajj change the way Europe and North Africa saw Mali?

Learn 3: Sub-Questions and a Research Plan

  1. Main question: "Why are Florida's oyster reefs disappearing, and what is being done to restore them?" Write three sub-questions and name a kind of source for each.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Three sub-questions that each help answer the main question; A sensible source type for each
    Model answer: 1. How much oyster reef has Apalachicola Bay lost since 2012? (state or university data) 2. What causes oyster die-offs? (science websites, NOAA) 3. What restoration projects are underway? (FWC website, news articles)

Learn 4: Refocusing: When Research Changes Your Question

  1. Lucía planned to research "Why do Burmese pythons in the Everglades prefer eating raccoons?" She found only one article about raccoons, but dozens about how pythons are hunted and tracked. How should she refocus, and what should she write in her research log?
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: A sensible refocused question; A log entry with the old question, new question and reason
    Model answer: She could widen it to "How are scientists and hunters trying to control Burmese pythons in the Everglades?" Her log: Old question: why pythons prefer raccoons. New question: how pythons are being controlled. Reason: almost no sources on the old question, many reliable sources on the new one.

Hands-On

  1. How many questions did you write in each storm? Copy two closed questions you turned into open ones, and explain which topic produced your best question and why.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Reports the question counts; Two closed-to-open conversions; Explains the choice of topic
    Model answer: I wrote 14, 9 and 17 questions. I turned "When was the Hubble telescope launched?" into "How has the Hubble telescope changed what we know about the universe?" and "Is Lake Okeechobee polluted?" into "Why do algae blooms keep spreading from Lake Okeechobee, and who is affected?" Space gave the most questions, but Lake Okeechobee won because it affects people I know.
  2. Write your final research question, your sub-questions with a source type for each, and your first research log entry.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: A focused, open research question; Three to five sub-questions with source types; A dated log entry
    Model answer: Question: Why do algae blooms keep spreading from Lake Okeechobee, and what could stop them? Sub-questions: 1. What causes the blooms? (South Florida Water Management District, university sites) 2. How do the blooms reach the coasts? (news articles, maps) 3. How do they affect people and wildlife? (Florida Department of Health) 4. What solutions are being tried? (Everglades restoration project site). Log, Nov 16: chose my question because my cousin in Stuart can't swim at her beach in summer.

Think Deeper

  1. Write your own definition of a good research question, with at least three features. Then test your Unit 6 question against your definition and explain whether it passes.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: A definition with at least three features; Applies the definition to your own question; An honest conclusion
    Model answer: A good research question is open (it asks how or why), focused enough to answer in a short presentation, answerable with at least three reliable sources, and important to someone. My question about Lake Okeechobee algae passes: it asks why and what, it focuses on one lake, I already found several government sources, and it matters to families on the coast.
  2. Historians and scientists both ask questions. Compare a question a historian asked in Unit 5 (such as who built Great Zimbabwe) with a question a scientist asked in Unit 5 (such as why the lynx peak comes after the hare peak). How were their methods of answering alike and different?
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Accurate examples from both; At least one similarity in method; At least one difference in method
    Model answer: Archaeologists asked who built Great Zimbabwe and answered by digging, dating objects and comparing them with Shona culture. Ecologists asked why the lynx peak comes after the hare peak and answered with long-term population data. Both gathered evidence and tested ideas instead of trusting assumptions. But the historians studied a past that can't be repeated, while ecologists could keep collecting new data and even build models.

Exit Check

  1. Turn this closed question into an open research question: "Is the Florida panther endangered?"
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: An open how or why question about the same subject
    Model answer: Why is the Florida panther still endangered, and what is helping its numbers grow?
  2. What do we call a smaller question that helps answer the main research question?
    Answer: a sub-question
    Three to five make a research plan.
  3. Give one reason a researcher might refocus a question.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: A valid reason drawn from the lesson
    Model answer: It was too broad: there were thousands of sources and no way to cover them.

Answer key: printable page

  1. Label each question open or closed: (a) What year did Florida become a state? (b) Why did Florida's population grow so fast after 1950?
    Answer: (a) closed (b) open
  2. Narrow the topic "sports" into a focused research question.
    Answer: Model: How have new rules about head injuries changed youth football in Florida?
  3. Write three sub-questions for: "How did the Maya build such accurate calendars?"
    Answer: Model: What did they observe in the sky? How did their number system work? How did they record dates?
  4. Explain the three tests for a researchable question.
    Answer: Three or more sources; can be answered in 3 to 5 minutes; you genuinely want to know.
  5. Write your Unit 6 research question.
    Answer: Model: How have pumping and rainfall changed Silver Springs' flow, and what could restore it? (Open, focused and answerable with three or more sources.)
