Unit 6: Heredity and Adaptation · Week 26 · Life Science · Teacher Plan

Traits and Inheritance

Lesson length: 60 minutes live, plus the after-class interactive lesson

Objectives

  • Explain that DNA in chromosomes carries genes, the instructions for traits.
  • Distinguish inherited traits from acquired (learned or environmental) traits.
  • Explain that offspring get one copy of each gene from each parent in sexual reproduction, while asexual reproduction makes copies.
  • Explain dominant and recessive alleles and why a trait can skip a generation.
  • Interpret a pedigree and explain how the environment can affect how traits are expressed.

Standards

  • SC.7.L.16.1: Understand and explain that every organism requires a set of instructions that specifies its traits, that this hereditary information (DNA) contains genes located in the chromosomes of each cell, and that heredity is the passage of these instructions from one generation to another.
  • SC.7.L.16.3: Compare and contrast the general processes of sexual reproduction requiring meiosis and asexual reproduction requiring mitosis.
  • SC.6.N.3.4: Identify the role of models in the context of the sixth grade science benchmarks.

Materials

  • The interactive lesson
  • A mirror, two paper bags, about 20 beads or buttons in two colors
  • Printable practice page

Vocabulary

  • trait: A characteristic of an organism, such as eye color, height or flower color.
  • heredity: The passing of traits from parents to offspring.
  • chromosome: A long, coiled strand of DNA inside the nucleus; humans have 46 (23 pairs).
  • gene: A section of DNA that carries the instructions for a trait.
  • allele: One of the different forms of a gene, such as the form for brown eyes or blue eyes.
  • dominant: An allele whose trait shows when at least one copy is present.
  • recessive: An allele whose trait shows only when two copies are present.

Key questions to ask

  • Is this trait inherited, acquired or both?
  • Where are the instructions stored?
  • How can a trait skip a generation?
  • Why aren't siblings identical?

Common mistakes to watch for

  • Acquired traits can be inherited.
  • Dominant traits are always the most common.
  • Children are a blend of exactly half each parent's looks.
  • Earlobes and dimples are always simple one-gene traits (they are more complex).

Support (if the student is struggling)

  • Use a nesting-dolls model for cell, nucleus, chromosome and gene.
  • Provide a trait sorting chart.
  • Act out the bead draw together first.

Challenge (if the student is ready for more)

  • Research how Gregor Mendel used pea plants to discover dominant and recessive traits (a preview of next week).
  • Find out why some cats are calico and what it has to do with chromosomes.

Background for the teacher

Family trait surveys can raise questions about adoption, donors or parentage. The lesson offers surveying any willing group instead; please steer the activity so no child feels singled out. Classic classroom traits (earlobes, dimples, widow's peak, tongue rolling) are not truly single-gene traits; the lesson flags this. Human genome estimates are about 19,000 to 20,000 protein-coding genes. Florida places heredity in Grade 7 (SC.7.L.16.x); KCE teaches it here per the Grade 6 scope.

Answer key: interactive lesson

Reconnect

  1. What happened to green anoles' toe pads over about 20 generations after brown anoles arrived?
    Answer: they grew larger and stickier
    A trait that helped survival became more common.
  2. In which part of a cell is DNA stored (Week 7)?
    Answer: the nucleus
    The control center.
  3. What is the largest population an environment can support over time?
    Answer: carrying capacity
    Set by limiting factors.
  4. The anoles' toe pads changed over generations, not within one lizard's life. What must be true about toe pad size for it to change across generations?
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Toe pad size must be passed from parents to offspring (inherited)
    Model answer: Toe pad size must be inherited, passed from parent lizards to their babies, so lizards that survived better passed bigger toe pads on.

Learn 1: DNA, Chromosomes and Genes

  1. Order from largest to smallest.
    Answer: cell → nucleus → chromosome → gene
    Each is inside the one before it.
  2. Humans have 46 chromosomes in each body cell. How many came from each parent?
    Answer: 23
    One of each pair from each parent.
  3. Rosalind Franklin's photograph was used by Watson and Crick, but for many years she received little credit. Why does it matter to give credit to every scientist whose evidence leads to a discovery?
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Explains fairness and accurate history; Connects to how science builds on evidence from many people
    Model answer: Science builds on many people's evidence, and Franklin's photograph was key to discovering the double helix. Leaving her out gives a false history and can discourage people, especially women, who might think scientists like them don't count.

Learn 2: Inherited and Acquired Traits

  1. Inherited or acquired?
    Answer: inherited; acquired; acquired
    Blood type comes from genes; skills are learned.
  2. A flamingo raised in a zoo on a diet without shrimp or algae turns pale. Is pink color an inherited trait, an acquired trait or both? Explain.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: A clear conclusion about the type of trait; Explains the role of genes and the role of diet
    Model answer: Both. Flamingo genes give them the ability to store pigments in their feathers, but the pink color only appears if their diet contains those pigments. The environment decides how the inherited trait shows.

Learn 3: Passing Genes On

  1. What kind of reproduction makes offspring with DNA identical to the parent?
    Answer: asexual reproduction
    Like strawberry runners.
  2. Use the words dominant and recessive to explain how two parents with free earlobes could have a child with attached earlobes (see the pedigree).
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Each parent carries a hidden recessive allele; The child received the recessive allele from both parents
    Model answer: Free earlobes are dominant in this example. Each parent has one dominant allele and one hidden recessive allele, so both show free earlobes. Their daughter got the recessive allele from both parents, so with two recessive alleles, her earlobes are attached.
  3. Explain why two siblings with the same parents can look quite different, but identical twins look almost the same.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Each sibling gets a different random mix of the parents' genes; Identical twins come from one fertilized egg, so they share the same DNA
    Model answer: Each child gets a different random half of each parent's genes, so siblings get different mixes. Identical twins come from one fertilized egg that split in two, so they have the same DNA.

Learn 4: Reading a Pedigree

  1. In the pedigree, how many people show the shaded trait?
    Answer: 1
    Only the daughter.
  2. Using the pedigree, explain how we know attached earlobes are recessive in this family.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Neither parent shows the trait, but a child does; So both parents must carry a hidden recessive allele
    Model answer: Neither grandparent has attached earlobes, but their daughter does. The trait was hidden in both parents, so it must be recessive.

Hands-On

  1. Share your trait survey table. Did any trait show up in a child but not in either parent? What might explain it?
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: A completed table; An observation about patterns; An explanation using dominant and recessive alleles (or an honest note that no such case appeared)
    Model answer: I surveyed six people. My mom and dad both have free earlobes, but I have attached earlobes, so they might both carry a recessive allele. My sister has dimples like my dad. Nobody in my family has a widow's peak.
  2. Report your bead baby results: how many DD, Dd and dd? What fraction showed the recessive trait? Why might your results be different from another family's?
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Counts for all three combinations; The recessive fraction; Explains that chance causes variation
    Model answer: I got 6 DD, 9 Dd and 5 dd. So 5 of 20, or one-fourth, showed the recessive trait. Another family might get 3 or 7, because each draw is random chance; the more babies you make, the closer you get to one-fourth.

Think Deeper

  1. A scientist in the 1800s claimed giraffes got long necks because each giraffe stretched to reach leaves and then passed its stretched neck to its babies. Use what you learned about acquired and inherited traits to explain what is wrong with this idea.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Stretching would be an acquired trait; Acquired traits are not passed on through genes; Neck length differences must be inherited
    Model answer: A neck stretched during a giraffe's life would be an acquired trait, like a weightlifter's muscles, and acquired traits aren't passed through genes. Babies inherit genes for neck length from their parents. Next week's Punnett squares and the natural selection lesson will show how long necks really became common.
  2. Choose one human trait that depends on both genes and environment (such as height, skin tone in the sun, or a talent). Explain how each one contributes, and why it matters not to judge people only by traits they were born with.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Accurate explanation of both contributions; A thoughtful reason about fairness
    Model answer: Height depends on many genes from both parents, but good nutrition and sleep while growing also matter. Someone can be shorter because of genes or because they didn't have enough food as a child. That's one reason it's unfair to judge people by traits they were born with or couldn't control.

Exit Check

  1. A section of DNA that carries instructions for a trait is a ___.
    Answer: gene
    Humans have about 20,000.
  2. A parent who speaks Spanish has a baby. Will the baby be born able to speak Spanish? Name the kind of trait that speaking a language is.
    Answer: acquired (learned), so no
    Languages are learned after birth.
  3. An allele whose trait shows only when two copies are present is called ___.
    Answer: recessive
    It can hide for generations.
  4. Why does each child get exactly half of their chromosomes from each parent?
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Each sex cell carries half the chromosomes; Together they make a full set
    Model answer: Each egg and sperm carries 23 chromosomes, half a set, so together they make a full set of 46.

Answer key: printable page

  1. Explain the relationship among DNA, chromosomes and genes.
    Answer: DNA is coiled into chromosomes; a gene is a section of DNA that codes for a trait.
  2. Give two inherited traits and two acquired traits.
    Answer: Inherited: eye color, blood type; acquired: a scar, riding a bike.
  3. Compare sexual and asexual reproduction.
    Answer: Sexual: two parents, offspring get a mix of genes; asexual: one parent, identical offspring.
  4. How can two brown-eyed parents have a blue-eyed child?
    Answer: Both carry a hidden recessive allele; the child received the recessive allele from each parent.
  5. Give one example of the environment affecting how a trait shows.
    Answer: Hydrangea color depends on soil; flamingo color depends on diet; height depends on nutrition.
