Unit 2: Cells, the Building Blocks of Life · Week 10 · Life Science · Teacher Plan

Unit 2 Checkpoint and Cell Model Project

Lesson length: 60 minutes live, plus the after-class interactive lesson

Objectives

  • Explain how cell theory, organelles, plant cells and membrane transport connect.
  • Evaluate the evidence that mitochondria and chloroplasts were once free-living bacteria.
  • Plan a scale-minded cell model with an analogy and a key.
  • Show mastery of Unit 2 on the checkpoint (85 or higher).

Standards

  • SC.6.L.14.2: Investigate and explain the components of the scientific theory of cells (cell theory): all organisms are composed of cells (single-celled or multi-cellular), all cells come from pre-existing cells, and cells are the basic unit of life.
  • SC.6.L.14.3: Recognize and explore how cells of all organisms undergo similar processes to maintain homeostasis, including extracting energy from food, getting rid of waste, and reproducing.
  • SC.6.L.14.4: Compare and contrast the structure and function of major organelles of plant and animal cells, including cell wall, cell membrane, nucleus, cytoplasm, chloroplasts, mitochondria, and vacuoles.
  • SC.6.N.2.2: Explain that scientific knowledge is durable because it is open to change as new evidence or interpretations are encountered.
  • SC.6.N.3.4: Identify the role of models in the context of the sixth grade science benchmarks.

Materials

  • The interactive lesson
  • Cell model materials (shoebox or plate, clay, beans, yarn, buttons, gel or gelatin), index cards, glue, tape, markers
  • A phone or camera
  • Paper and pencil for the checkpoint

Vocabulary

  • concept map: A diagram that shows how ideas connect, with arrows and linking words.
  • endosymbiosis: The idea that some organelles began as bacteria living inside another cell.
  • model: A representation that helps explain something too small, large or complex to see directly.
  • analogy: A comparison that explains something by showing how it is like something familiar.
  • key (legend): A list that explains what each part or color of a model stands for.

Key questions to ask

  • How does this part depend on another part?
  • What evidence would convince you that an organelle was once a bacterium?
  • What does your model show well, and what can it not show?

Common mistakes to watch for

  • Unit topics are separate rather than connected.
  • Endosymbiosis means organelles are still bacteria today.
  • A model is a craft rather than an explanation.
  • Plants do not have mitochondria.

Support (if the student is struggling)

  • Provide a partly completed concept map.
  • Give a model planning sheet with spaces for each part and material.
  • Offer analogy starters: a school, a farm, a kitchen, a sports team.

Challenge (if the student is ready for more)

  • Research the other evidence for endosymbiosis, such as bacteria found living inside other cells today.
  • Make a second model at a consistent scale: if a cell were the size of your room, how big would a mitochondrion be?

Background for the teacher

The checkpoint covers Weeks 6 to 9. Lynn Margulis (1938 to 2011) published "On the origin of mitosing cells" in the Journal of Theoretical Biology in 1967 under the name Lynn Sagan, after rejections by about fifteen journals; endosymbiotic origin of mitochondria and chloroplasts is now the consensus view, supported by circular organelle DNA, binary fission, 70S-like ribosomes and double membranes. The Cell Model Project rubric is on the Unit 2 Projects page.

Answer key: interactive lesson

Reconnect

  1. What is the name for the diffusion of water across a membrane?
    Answer: osmosis
    Osmosis.
  2. A potato strip is placed in very salty water. Will it get longer or shorter?
    Answer: shorter
    The strip gets shorter. You will explain why on the checkpoint.
  3. Which kind of transport needs energy, and which organelle supplies that energy?
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Names the energy-using kind of transport and the organelle that releases energy from glucose
    Model answer: Active transport needs energy, and the mitochondria supply it by releasing energy from glucose.
  4. Week 8: Plant cells have chloroplasts. Do they also have mitochondria? Explain.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Yes; plants also release energy from glucose in mitochondria
    Model answer: Yes. Chloroplasts make glucose, but plant cells still need mitochondria to release the energy stored in it.

Learn 1: Connecting the Big Ideas

  1. Trace a carbon atom through a plant cell from the air and back to the air. Name at least four steps and the cell part or process involved in each.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Enters the leaf as CO₂ through stomata and diffuses into cells; Chloroplast uses it in photosynthesis to make glucose; Glucose is used by mitochondria to release energy; Carbon leaves as CO₂ by diffusion
    Model answer: Carbon dioxide enters the leaf through the stomata and diffuses into a leaf cell. In a chloroplast, photosynthesis uses it to make glucose. The glucose goes to a mitochondrion, which releases its energy, and the carbon is given off as carbon dioxide. That carbon dioxide diffuses out of the cell and back to the air.
  2. Write one linking sentence that connects osmosis (Week 9) to the central vacuole (Week 8).
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Osmosis moves water into or out of the central vacuole, making the cell firm or wilted
    Model answer: Osmosis moves water into the central vacuole, which swells and keeps the plant cell firm, and out of it, which makes the plant wilt.
  3. What would happen to a plant cell if its mitochondria stopped working, even though its chloroplasts were fine? Explain.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: It could still make glucose but could not release its energy; Active transport and other work needing energy would stop, so the cell would die
    Model answer: The chloroplasts could still make glucose, but the cell could not release the energy stored in it. Active transport and other jobs that need energy would stop, so the cell would eventually die.

Learn 2: A Bold Idea: Organelles That Were Once Bacteria

  1. Choose the two pieces of evidence for endosymbiosis that you find most convincing, and explain why each one supports the idea.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Two accurate pieces of evidence (own DNA, divide like bacteria, bacterial size and ribosomes, two membranes); Explains how each points to a bacterial origin
    Model answer: The strongest evidence is that mitochondria have their own loop of DNA, like bacteria, which would make sense if they were once separate living things. Also, they divide in two the way bacteria do, instead of being built by the cell from scratch.
  2. Margulis's idea was rejected by many scientists before it was accepted. Using her story, explain how scientific knowledge changes.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: New ideas are tested against evidence; when evidence builds up, explanations change; Science is open to revision; peer review can be slow to accept new ideas
    Model answer: Scientific knowledge changes when new evidence supports a better explanation. At first many scientists doubted Margulis, but as studies found DNA, ribosomes and double membranes that matched bacteria, the evidence became strong enough that most scientists changed their minds.
  3. Which American biologist argued in 1967, with evidence, that mitochondria and chloroplasts began as bacteria?
    Answer: Lynn Margulis
    Lynn Margulis. Her paper was published under the name Lynn Sagan.

Learn 3: Planning Your Cell Model Project

  1. Plan your model: which cell type, what material will stand for each of at least five parts, and why you chose each material.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Cell type chosen; At least five parts matched to materials; Reasons tie the material to the structure or job (e.g. stiff cardboard for the wall)
    Model answer: Plant cell in a shoebox. Cell wall: the stiff shoebox, because the wall is rigid. Membrane: plastic wrap lining the box, because it is thin and flexible. Central vacuole: a water-filled zipper bag, because it stores water. Chloroplasts: green lima beans. Nucleus: a purple clay ball with a smaller ball inside for the nucleolus.
  2. Draft your analogy: "A cell is like a ___ because..." Match at least five parts to parts of your choice, and explain one match fully.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: A new analogy (not a city); Five matched parts; One match explained by job
    Model answer: A plant cell is like a school. The nucleus is the principal's office, the mitochondria are the cafeteria that gives energy, the cell membrane is the front door with a security desk, the cell wall is the fence, and the chloroplasts are the school garden. The cell membrane is like the security desk because it decides who comes in and who leaves.
  3. Every model has limits. Name one way your planned model will not be like a real cell.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: A real limit: scale, number of organelles, a real cell is 3D and moving, organelles are not solid, colors are not real
    Model answer: A real cell has hundreds of mitochondria and thousands of ribosomes, but my model shows only a few, and a real cell's parts are always moving.

Hands-On

  1. Write the script for your 2-minute tour: name each part, its job, and walk through the process you showed.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: All labeled parts named with correct jobs; Process explained step by step with accurate science; Uses Unit 2 vocabulary; Organized like a tour
    Model answer: Welcome to my plant cell. The shoebox is the cell wall, which supports and protects the cell. Inside it, plastic wrap is the cell membrane, which controls what enters and leaves. The purple clay ball is the nucleus, which holds the DNA and directs the cell. The water bag is the central vacuole, which stores water and keeps the cell firm. The green beans are chloroplasts. Follow the arrows: carbon dioxide diffuses in, the chloroplast uses light to make glucose and oxygen, and the glucose travels to the orange mitochondria, which release its energy...
  2. What question did your family member ask during the tour, and how did you answer it? What would you improve if you built the model again?
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Reports a real question and answer; Names a specific improvement
    Model answer: My brother asked why the vacuole is so big. I said it stores water and pushes on the wall to keep the plant standing. Next time I would make the ribosomes smaller and add more of them.

Think Deeper

  1. Answer the unit's big question in a paragraph: How do the parts of a cell work together to keep it alive? Use at least six Unit 2 vocabulary words correctly and give at least two examples of parts depending on each other.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Six or more vocabulary words used correctly; Two or more accurate examples of interdependence; Clear explanation of how the cell stays alive; Organized paragraph
    Model answer: A cell stays alive because its parts work as a team. The cell membrane controls what enters and leaves, letting oxygen and carbon dioxide in and out by diffusion and water by osmosis. In a plant cell, chloroplasts use light to make glucose by photosynthesis, and the mitochondria release the energy in that glucose. That energy powers active transport, which pumps minerals into the cell. The nucleus holds the DNA, which directs the ribosomes as they build proteins, and the Golgi apparatus packages and ships them. If any one part stopped, the others would fail, so the cell depends on all of them working together.
  2. A friend says, "Cells are just tiny blobs. Nothing important happens in them." Write a respectful reply using three facts from Unit 2 to change their mind.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Respectful tone; Three accurate, specific facts from Unit 2; Persuasive reasoning
    Model answer: I used to think so too, but cells are amazing. Every living thing is made of them, and all of your body's cells came from other cells. Inside each one, mitochondria release energy from food, and in plants, chloroplasts turn sunlight into the sugar that feeds almost every living thing. Even drinking seawater shows how important cells are: it pulls water out of them by osmosis. Cells are where life actually happens.

    Model answer: All living things are made of cells, the cell is the basic unit of life, and all cells come from existing cells.
  3. Which scientist, in the 1670s, was the first to see living single-celled organisms through a microscope he made himself?

Checkpoint: Part B: Organelles

  1. Which organelle builds proteins?
    Answer: ribosomes
    Ribosomes build proteins.
  2. Which organelle packages and ships proteins?
    Answer: Golgi apparatus
    The Golgi apparatus.
  3. Which organelle holds the cell's DNA and directs its activities?
    Answer: nucleus
    The nucleus.
  4. Explain why Henrietta Lacks's story matters for how scientists treat people today.

    Model answer: Her cells were taken in 1951 without her permission and used in research around the world. Her story shows why scientists must get informed consent and respect patients and their families.

Checkpoint: Part C: Plant cells and photosynthesis

  1. Name the three parts plant cells have that animal cells lack, with the job of each.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric (3 points toward the checkpoint).
    Rubric: Cell wall: support and protection; Chloroplasts: photosynthesis; Central vacuole: stores water, keeps cell firm
    Model answer: The cell wall supports and protects the cell, chloroplasts carry out photosynthesis, and the central vacuole stores water and keeps the cell firm.
  2. What is the green pigment in chloroplasts that captures light energy for photosynthesis?
    Answer: chlorophyll
    Chlorophyll (Week 8).
  3. Where does most of a tree's mass come from? Explain.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric (3 points toward the checkpoint).
    Rubric: Carbon dioxide from the air (plus water); Photosynthesis builds glucose and then wood from those atoms
    Model answer: Mostly from carbon dioxide in the air, plus water. Photosynthesis builds those atoms into glucose, which the tree turns into wood.

Checkpoint: Part D: Moving in and out of cells

  1. A plant cell is placed in fresh water. Will water move into the cell or out of it?
    Answer: into the cell
    The cell has more dissolved particles than fresh water, so water moves in.
  2. Which kind of transport moves particles from low to high concentration?
    Answer: active transport
    Active transport, which uses energy.
  3. Data: cucumber slices started at 8.0 g. After 1 hour, slices in fresh water had a mass of 8.6 g, and slices in salt water had a mass of 6.9 g. Write a CER conclusion.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric (4 points toward the checkpoint).
    Rubric: Claim: water moved into cells in fresh water and out in salt water; Evidence: +0.6 g in fresh water, −1.1 g in salt water; Reasoning: osmosis; water moves toward the side with more dissolved particles
    Model answer: Claim: Water moved into the cucumber cells in fresh water and out of them in salt water. Evidence: The fresh-water slices gained 0.6 g (8.0 to 8.6 g), and the salt-water slices lost 1.1 g (8.0 to 6.9 g). Reasoning: By osmosis, water moves toward the side with more dissolved particles. The cucumber cells had more than fresh water, so water moved in; the salt water had more than the cells, so water moved out.

Answer key: printable page

  1. State the three parts of cell theory.
    Answer: All living things are made of cells; the cell is the basic unit of life; all cells come from existing cells.
  2. Match: nucleus, mitochondria, chloroplast, cell membrane, ribosome.
    Answer: DNA and control; release energy; photosynthesis; controls what enters and leaves; builds proteins.
  3. Write the word equation for photosynthesis.
    Answer: carbon dioxide + water + light energy → glucose + oxygen
  4. What happens to a plant cell in very salty water, and why?
    Answer: Water moves out by osmosis, so the vacuole shrinks and the cell wilts.
  5. Give two pieces of evidence that mitochondria were once bacteria.
    Answer: Their own loop of DNA; they divide like bacteria (also bacterial-size ribosomes, two membranes).
  6. Name one limit of a cell model.
    Answer: Not to scale; too few organelles; parts do not move as in a real cell.
