Unit 2: Explaining the World (Informational) · Week 10 · ELA · Teacher Plan

Unit 2 Checkpoint and Informational Article Project

Lesson length: 60 minutes live, plus the after-class interactive lesson

Objectives

  • Organize note card groups into an outline with an introduction, body sections and a conclusion.
  • Write an introduction with a hook and a central idea (thesis).
  • Write body paragraphs that combine facts from sources, with in-text citations and varied transitions.
  • Use text features (title, headings, a visual with a caption) to help readers.
  • Show mastery of Unit 2 on the checkpoint (85 or higher).

Standards

  • ELA.6.C.1.4: Write expository texts to explain and/or analyze information from multiple sources, using a logical organizational structure, relevant elaboration, and varied transitions.
  • ELA.6.C.4.1: Conduct research to answer a question, drawing on multiple reliable and valid sources, and refocusing the inquiry when appropriate.
  • ELA.6.R.2.2: Analyze the central idea(s), implied or explicit, and its development throughout a text.
  • ELA.6.R.3.2: Paraphrase content from grade-level texts.
  • ELA.6.V.1.2: Apply knowledge of Greek and Latin roots and affixes to determine meanings of words and phrases in grade-level content.

Materials

  • The interactive lesson
  • The student's note cards, outline and works cited list from Week 9
  • Colored pencils
  • The Unit 2 Projects rubric
  • A computer or paper for the final copy

Vocabulary

  • thesis: In an informational article, the sentence that states the central idea the whole article explains.
  • text features: Titles, headings, captions, diagrams and bold words that help readers find and understand information.
  • elaboration: Explaining a fact so readers understand why it matters or how it connects.
  • transition: A word or phrase that connects ideas, such as as a result, however or for example.
  • caption: A short sentence under a picture or diagram that explains it.

Key questions to ask

  • Does every section support your central idea?
  • Where is the citation for this fact?
  • What does this fact mean? (Elaborate.)
  • Would a reader who knows nothing about this topic understand it?

Common mistakes to watch for

  • An informational article is a list of facts with no explanation.
  • Only quotations need citations.
  • The conclusion should add new facts.
  • Headings are decoration.

Support (if the student is struggling)

  • Provide a T-E-E-C paragraph frame.
  • Allow a shorter article (300 to 500 words) with two body sections; note the accommodation on the rubric.
  • Read the checkpoint article aloud if decoding is a barrier.

Challenge (if the student is ready for more)

  • Add a third source and a sidebar with a fun fact.
  • Turn the article into a one-page infographic for the co-op.

Background for the teacher

The checkpoint article is original, written from established facts: the Winter Park sinkhole opened on May 8, 1981, and reached about 350 feet across and 75 feet deep; freeze-protection pumping near Plant City in January 2010 dropped the aquifer level sharply and dozens of sinkholes (reports range up to about 140) were documented; Devil's Millhopper is about 120 feet deep and 500 feet across (Florida State Parks). Limestone dissolution by weak carbonic acid is the standard karst explanation (U.S. Geological Survey). Checkpoint scoring

Checkpoint questions are scored separately from the lesson. The student gets one try per question and the answers are not shown, so the checkpoint measures independent mastery and a retake stays fair. Your results email shows the auto-scored checkpoint points, a list of each item marked correct or MISSED, and the points available for the written items. Score the written items with their rubrics, then: checkpoint score = (auto points + rubric points) / total possible × 100. Mastery is 85. Below 85: teach a targeted review lesson on the parts missed (A to D), then the student retakes the checkpoint. Record both attempts in the Weekly Score Log.

Answer key: interactive lesson

Reconnect

  1. Name the four parts of a research note card.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Subtopic heading, source label, notes in own words, exact quotes in quotation marks with location
    Model answer: A subtopic heading, the source label, notes in my own words, and exact words in quotation marks with the page or paragraph.
  2. What is the MLA name for the list of sources at the end of a paper?
    Answer: works cited
    Works Cited.
  3. Week 8: What kind of statement must a summary leave out? Type one word.
    Answer: opinions
    A summary leaves out the writer's opinions.
  4. Week 7: "First, mix the soil. Next, add water. Finally, plant the seeds." Which text structure is this?
    Answer: sequence
    Sequence: the signal words first, next and finally show steps in order.
  5. Week 6: what is the difference between a topic and a central idea?
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Topic: word or phrase naming the subject; Central idea: a sentence stating the main point about it
    Model answer: A topic is a word or phrase naming what a text is about. A central idea is a full sentence stating the main point the author makes about that topic.

Learn 1: Outline and Introduction

  1. Write your article's outline: title, central idea sentence, and a heading for each body section in order. Name the text structure you chose and why.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Title and a central idea sentence that answers the research question; Two to four section headings in logical order; Names a fitting text structure with a reason
    Model answer: Title: Into the Eye of the Storm. Central idea: Hurricanes form from warm ocean water and spinning winds, and scientists use planes, satellites and computer models to predict their paths. Sections: How a Hurricane Forms; Hurricane Hunters and Satellites; Staying Safe. Structure: sequence for the first section and problem and solution for the article overall, because hurricanes are a danger and forecasting is part of the solution.
  2. Draft your introduction: a hook, one or two background sentences and your central idea.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: An engaging hook; Background; A clear central idea sentence
    Model answer: Every summer, scientists fly airplanes straight into hurricanes on purpose. These storms are among the most powerful on Earth, and Florida faces them almost every year. Hurricanes form from warm ocean water and spinning winds, and scientists use planes, satellites and computer models to predict where they will go.

Learn 2: Body Paragraphs: Facts, Citations, Elaboration

  1. Rewrite this paragraph with varied transitions and one sentence of elaboration: "Public libraries are important. Also they lend books. Also they offer free internet. Also many have lost funding."
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: At least two varied transitions (in addition, as a result, however); One sentence of elaboration explaining why a fact matters; Smooth, connected sentences
    Model answer: Public libraries are important to every community. They lend books, movies and e-books for free. In addition, many families without home internet depend on library computers and Wi-Fi, which helps students finish schoolwork. However, many libraries have lost funding in recent years.
  2. Draft one body paragraph for your article using T-E-E-C. Include at least two facts from your note cards with in-text citations.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Topic sentence; Two facts from sources with in-text citations; Elaboration in the writer's own words; Connection sentence; Varied transitions
    Model answer: Hurricane hunters fly directly into storms to collect information that cannot be gathered any other way. The planes drop sensors that measure wind speed, air pressure and temperature as they fall (National Weather Service). In addition, satellites photograph the storm from space every few minutes (Simon). Together, these measurements help computer models predict a hurricane's path. Because of this teamwork, forecasters can warn people days before a storm arrives.

Learn 3: Text Features, Conclusion and Revision

  1. Write a caption for a visual you plan to include. A good caption says what the visual shows and why it matters.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Says what the visual shows; Explains why it matters to the article
    Model answer: A satellite image of Hurricane Ian approaching Florida in 2022. Images like this help forecasters track a storm's size and path.
  2. Draft your conclusion: restate your central idea in new words and explain why it matters.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Restates the central idea in new words; Explains why it matters; No brand-new facts
    Model answer: Hurricanes may be among nature's most powerful storms, but they are no longer complete surprises. Thanks to hurricane hunters, satellites and computer models, scientists can now warn people in time to prepare. Understanding how these storms work helps Florida families stay safe.

  1. Paste or type your final article here, including the works cited list (400 to 700 words).
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Meets every requirement on the Unit 2 Informational Article rubric
    Model answer: Into the Eye of the Storm Every summer, scientists fly airplanes straight into hurricanes on purpose... (full article with headings, citations, a captioned visual and a works cited list)
  2. Reflect: Which part of the research and writing process was hardest, how did you solve it, and what did your audience learn from your article?
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Names a real challenge and how it was solved; Reports what the audience learned
    Model answer: Taking notes in my own words was hardest because the sources explained things so well. I solved it by covering the page before writing. My grandmother said she never knew planes flew into hurricanes, so she learned how forecasts are made.

Think Deeper

  1. Compare your Unit 1 personal narrative with your Unit 2 informational article. How are narrative and informational writing different in purpose, structure and language? Use examples from your own writing.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Purpose: tell a story and share an experience versus explain and inform; Structure: story mountain versus introduction, sections with headings and conclusion; Language: first person, dialogue and sensory details versus facts, citations and precise terms; Examples from the student's own writing
    Model answer: My personal narrative told the story of the day I got lost at the fair, so its purpose was to share an experience. It followed a story mountain and used first person, dialogue and sensory details. My informational article explains how hurricanes form and are predicted, so its purpose is to teach. It uses an introduction with a central idea, sections with headings and a conclusion, and its language is factual, with precise terms like air pressure and citations from my sources.
  2. How can a reader tell that an informational article is trustworthy? Give three features, and explain which one your own article does best.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Three features: citations and works cited, reliable sources, accurate facts, objective tone, author or publisher named, recent date; Identifies and explains the strongest in their article
    Model answer: A trustworthy article cites its sources, uses reliable sources like government or science organizations, and stays objective instead of giving opinions. My article does citations best, because every fact has a source in parentheses and a matching works cited entry.

Checkpoint: Part A: Read the article

  1. Why the Ground Opens Up in Florida An original article by KingCretot Experience · KCE Practice Reader, 2026 [1] On a May evening in 1981, a sinkhole opened in Winter Park, Florida. Over the next day it grew to about 350 feet wide and 75 feet deep, swallowing a house, several cars and part of a city swimming pool. Television crews from around the world came to watch. To many viewers, it looked like the earth itself was collapsing. [2] Sinkholes are common in Florida because of what lies beneath the state. Much of Florida sits on thick layers of limestone, a rock made long ago from the shells and skeletons of sea creatures. Rainwater picks up carbon dioxide from the air and soil, which makes it slightly acidic. As this water seeps underground over thousands of years, it slowly dissolves the limestone, leaving cracks, tunnels and caves. [3] Usually, groundwater fills these spaces and helps hold up the soil above. When the water level drops, however, the support disappears. Then the sand and clay on top can sag slowly or collapse all at once. Droughts can lower the water level, and so can heavy pumping of groundwater. In January 2010, farmers near Plant City pumped huge amounts of water to spray on their strawberries and protect them from a freeze. As a result, the water level dropped sharply, and dozens of sinkholes opened in the area. [4] Not all sinkholes are disasters. Some formed long ago and have become beautiful natural places. At Devil's Millhopper Geological State Park near Gainesville, visitors walk down a staircase into a sinkhole about 120 feet deep, where small waterfalls trickle down the green walls. Many of Florida's crystal-clear springs also flow from openings in the limestone. [5] Scientists cannot predict exactly where the next sinkhole will open, but they can map areas at higher risk. Homeowners can watch for warning signs, such as new cracks in walls or floors, doors that suddenly stick, or a circular depression forming in the yard. Understanding the rock beneath our feet helps Floridians live safely on a landscape that is always slowly changing. What is the central idea of the article? Write it as one sentence.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric (4 points toward the checkpoint).
    Rubric: Florida's limestone, dissolved by slightly acidic water, makes sinkholes common; understanding this helps people live safely (a full sentence about the topic, not just "sinkholes")
    Model answer: Sinkholes are common in Florida because acidic water dissolves the limestone underground, and understanding this helps people stay safe.
  2. Which text structure does paragraph 3 mainly use?
    Answer: cause and effect
    Paragraph 3 explains causes (the water level drops, pumping) and effects (the soil collapses). "As a result" signals cause and effect.
  3. According to paragraph 3, what caused the sinkholes near Plant City in 2010? Explain the chain of events.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric (3 points toward the checkpoint).
    Rubric: Farmers pumped large amounts of groundwater to protect strawberries from a freeze; The water level dropped, removing support for the soil, so sinkholes opened
    Model answer: Farmers pumped huge amounts of groundwater to spray on their strawberries during a freeze. The water level dropped, so the water no longer supported the soil above the limestone spaces, and dozens of sinkholes opened.
  4. Which rock lies beneath much of Florida and is slowly dissolved by acidic water?
    Answer: limestone
    Limestone (paragraph 2).
  5. Why does the author include paragraph 4 about Devil's Millhopper and the springs? How does it develop the central idea?
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric (3 points toward the checkpoint).
    Rubric: Shows that sinkholes are not always disasters; the same process creates natural wonders; Adds balance and develops the idea that the landscape is always changing
    Model answer: Paragraph 4 shows that sinkholes are not always disasters, because the same dissolving limestone created beautiful places like Devil's Millhopper and the springs. It develops the central idea that Florida's landscape is shaped by the rock beneath it.

When groundwater drops from drought or pumping, soil can collapse, as in Plant City in 2010. Some old sinkholes are now parks and springs, and residents can learn warning signs.

  3. A student writes in a summary: "I think sinkholes are the scariest thing in Florida." What rule of summarizing does this break? Type the word that names the rule.

Checkpoint: Part D: Vocabulary from Unit 2

  1. Scientists who study groundwater like the water in paragraph 3 are called hydrogeologists. Using the roots hydr and geo, what does a hydrogeologist study?
    Answer: water in the ground (water and the earth)
    hydr (water) + geo (earth) + -logist (one who studies): a scientist who studies water underground.
  2. The root terr means land or earth. What word for the land area that an animal defends begins with terr?
    Answer: territory
    Territory: terr (land).
  3. The root dict means say or speak. What dict word names a book that tells what words mean and how to say them?
    Answer: dictionary
    dict (say) + -ionary: a book of words and how to say them.
  4. The root nav means ship. What do you call a country's fleet of warships?
    Answer: navy
    Navy: nav (ship).

Answer key: printable page

  1. What three jobs does an introduction do?
    Answer: Hook the reader, give background, state the central idea.
  2. What does T-E-E-C stand for?
    Answer: Topic sentence, Evidence, Elaboration, Connection.
  3. Rewrite with a varied transition: "It rained for weeks. Also the river flooded."
    Answer: It rained for weeks. As a result, the river flooded.
  4. Name three text features and what each does.
    Answer: Headings organize sections; captions explain visuals; bold words mark key terms.
  5. What should a conclusion do, and what should it not do?
    Answer: Restate the central idea in new words and say why it matters; no brand-new facts.
  6. Use the roots: what do aquarium, territory and navigate have in common in how they were built?
    Answer: Each is built on a root: aqua (water), terr (land), nav (ship).
