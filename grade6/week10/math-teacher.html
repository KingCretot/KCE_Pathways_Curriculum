Unit 2: Fractions and Ratios · Week 10 · Math · Teacher Plan

Unit 2 Review and Checkpoint

Lesson length: 60 minutes live, plus the after-class interactive lesson

Objectives

  • Choose whether a fraction problem calls for multiplication or division, and solve it.
  • Use ratio language, tape diagrams and ratio tables to solve problems.
  • Plan a budget using fractions of a total and ratio reasoning.
  • Check answers for reasonableness.
  • Show mastery of Unit 2 on the checkpoint (85 or higher).

Standards

  • MA.6.NSO.2.2: Extend previous understanding of multiplication and division to compute products and quotients of positive fractions by positive fractions, including mixed numbers, with procedural fluency.
  • MA.6.NSO.2.3: Solve multi-step real-world problems involving any of the four operations with positive multi-digit decimals or positive fractions, including mixed numbers.
  • MA.6.AR.3.1: Given a real-world context, write and interpret ratios to show the relative sizes of two quantities using appropriate notation: a/b, a to b, or a:b where b ≠ 0.
  • MA.6.AR.3.5: Solve mathematical and real-world problems involving ratios, rates and unit rates, including comparisons, mixtures, ratios of lengths and conversions within the same measurement system.
  • MA.K12.MTR.6.1: Assess the reasonableness of solutions.

Materials

  • The interactive lesson
  • Paper and pencil for the checkpoint (no calculator)
  • Store ads or receipts for the budget activity
  • Printable practice page

Vocabulary

  • budget: A plan for how money will be spent and saved.
  • fraction of a total: A part of a whole amount, found by multiplying: 1/4 of $240 = $60.
  • reasonable: Making sense for the situation; close to an estimate.
  • multi-step problem: A problem that needs more than one operation to solve.
  • emergency fund: Money set aside for unexpected costs.

Key questions to ask

  • Is this a fraction of an amount, or how many groups fit?
  • Is your answer reasonable compared with your estimate?
  • Did you do the same thing to both parts of the ratio?
  • What will you do with money left over?

Common mistakes to watch for

  • Multiplying when division is needed (and the reverse).
  • Forgetting to convert mixed numbers before multiplying or dividing.
  • Adding the same amount to both parts of a ratio.
  • Rounding down when you need enough for everyone (cans, bags).

Support (if the student is struggling)

  • Allow a one-page Unit 2 reference sheet of methods (no worked answers) during review, not during the checkpoint.
  • Read word problems aloud.
  • Break multi-step problems into numbered steps.

Challenge (if the student is ready for more)

  • Plan the Field Day for 36 people with the same $240 budget and explain the trade-offs.
  • Write a three-step budget problem for a family member to solve.

Answer key: interactive lesson

Reconnect

  1. Complete the ratio table for 3 : 5.
    Answer: 12; 10
    3 × 4 = 12 (because 20 = 5 × 4); 5 × 2 = 10 (because 6 = 3 × 2).
  2. Is 8 : 12 equivalent to 14 : 21? Type yes or no.
    Answer: yes
    Both simplify to 2 : 3.
  3. Week 8: "For every 3 red tiles there are 5 blue tiles." Write the part-to-whole ratio of red tiles to all tiles.
    Answer: 3 : 8
    3 red out of 3 + 5 = 8 tiles.
  4. On a graph of two price tables, why does the less steep line show the better buy?
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: For the same quantity, the less steep line is lower, so it costs less
    Model answer: For the same number of items, the less steep line's point is lower, so those items cost less.
  5. Week 6 multiplied fractions and Week 7 divided them. In your own words, how do you decide whether a word problem needs multiplication or division?
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Multiply to find a fraction of an amount ("of", scaling); Divide to find how many groups fit or how much in each group ("how many ... in", sharing)
    Model answer: If I need a fraction of an amount, like 2/3 of a recipe, I multiply. If I need to know how many pieces of a certain size fit into an amount, like how many 3/4-cup servings are in 6 cups, I divide.

Learn 1: Review: Multiply or Divide?

  1. How much of the $240 budget goes to food (1/2), games (1/4) and prizes (1/6) together?
    Answer: $220
    $120 + $60 + $40 = $220.
  2. What fraction of the budget is left for emergencies? Type it in simplest form.
    Answer: 1/12
    1/2 + 1/4 + 1/6 = 6/12 + 3/12 + 2/12 = 11/12, so 1 − 11/12 = 1/12, which is $20.
  3. The games team has 4 1/2 yards of rope to cut into jump ropes 3/4 of a yard long. How many jump ropes can they make?
    Answer: 6 jump ropes
    4 1/2 ÷ 3/4 = 9/2 × 4/3 = 36/6 = 6. Dividing: how many 3/4-yard pieces fit.
  4. Before calculating 4 1/2 ÷ 3/4, explain how you know the answer must be greater than 4 1/2.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Dividing by a number less than 1 gives a larger quotient, because many small pieces fit
    Model answer: Each piece is less than a whole yard, so more pieces than 4 1/2 will fit. Dividing by a fraction less than 1 makes the answer larger.

Learn 2: Review: Ratios, Tapes and Tables

  1. Each of the 24 people gets 1 cup of lemonade. One can of concentrate makes 5 cups. How many cans of concentrate are needed? (You cannot buy part of a can.)
    Answer: 5 cans
    24 ÷ 5 = 4.8, so 4 cans make only 20 cups. Buy 5 cans.
  2. Hot dogs come 8 for $5. Each of the 24 people eats 2. Use a ratio table to find the cost of the hot dogs.
    Answer: $30
    24 × 2 = 48 hot dogs. 48 = 8 × 6, so the cost is $5 × 6 = $30.
  3. Teams at Field Day are split so the ratio of younger to older students is 5 : 3. There are 24 people. How many are younger students?
    Answer: 15 students
    5 + 3 = 8 boxes; 24 ÷ 8 = 3 per box; younger: 5 × 3 = 15.
  4. In the lemonade, write the ratio of water to total lemonade in simplest form.
    Answer: 4 : 5
    4 cans of water out of 5 cans of lemonade in all. This is part-to-whole.

Learn 3: Money: Budget Review

  1. The food plan totals $97. How many dollars of the $120 food budget are left over?
    Answer: $23
    $120 − $97 = $23.
  2. Prizes get 1/6 of $240. Medals cost $3 each, and the co-op wants one medal for each of the 24 people. Is the prize budget enough? Show your work and suggest a plan if it is not.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Prize budget: 1/6 × $240 = $40; Medals: 24 × $3 = $72, so not enough (short $32); A reasonable plan: medals only for some events, cheaper prizes, move leftover food money ($23) plus emergency money, or ask for donations
    Model answer: The prize budget is 1/6 of $240 = $40, but 24 medals cost 24 × $3 = $72, so we are $32 short. We could buy medals only for the 8 event winners (8 × $3 = $24) and give everyone a $0.50 ribbon (24 × $0.50 = $12), for a total of $36.
  3. Why is it wise to keep 1/12 of the budget ($20) for emergencies? Give a realistic example of how it might be used at Field Day.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Unexpected costs happen; prevents going over budget; Realistic example (extra ice on a hot day, replacing a broken game, first-aid supplies, more guests)
    Model answer: Unexpected costs happen, and the emergency money keeps the co-op from going over budget. If it is very hot, we might need to buy extra ice and water.

Hands-On

  1. Record your event, guest count, total budget, your category fractions (and dollar amounts), and the two ratio or price tables you made.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Event, guests and total budget; Category fractions that add to 1, with correct dollar amounts; Two correct ratio or price tables scaled to the guest count
    Model answer: Event: game night for 10 people, $60 budget. Food 1/2 = $30, prizes 1/3 = $20, emergency 1/6 = $10. Popcorn: 1 bag serves 3, $2 each: 3 → $2, 6 → $4, 9 → $6, 12 → $8, so 4 bags = $8. Juice: 1 bottle per 4 people, $3 each: 4 → $3, 8 → $6, 12 → $9, so 3 bottles = $9.
  2. Did your planned costs fit each category? What did you change, and what question or change did your family suggest?
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Compares totals with category budgets; Describes an adjustment; Reports family feedback
    Model answer: Food came to $27, under the $30 budget. Prizes came to $24, so I changed from 4 prizes to 3. My dad asked whether we needed to buy plates or could use our own, which saved $4.

Think Deeper

  1. Find and fix the mistake in each student's work, and explain the error. (1) Malik: "3/4 ÷ 1/2 = 3/8." (2) Zara: "The ratio of 6 boys to 9 girls is the same as 7 boys to 10 girls, because I added 1 to each." (3) Leon: "2/3 of 18 cupcakes is 27."
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Malik multiplied instead of dividing; correct answer 3/4 × 2/1 = 6/4 = 1 1/2; Zara added instead of multiplying; 6 : 9 = 2 : 3 but 7 : 10 is not equivalent; Leon divided by 2/3 instead of multiplying; 2/3 × 18 = 12, and 2/3 of something must be less than it
    Model answer: (1) Malik multiplied instead of dividing. 3/4 ÷ 1/2 = 3/4 × 2/1 = 6/4 = 1 1/2. Dividing by 1/2 should give a bigger answer. (2) Zara added 1 to each part, which changes the ratio. 6 : 9 = 2 : 3, but 7 : 10 does not simplify to 2 : 3. She should multiply or divide both parts by the same number. (3) Leon divided by 2/3 instead of multiplying. 2/3 of 18 is 2/3 × 18 = 12. Since 2/3 is less than 1, the answer must be less than 18.
  2. Look back at Unit 2. Which skill do you feel strongest in, and which do you want more practice with? Give an example problem for each and explain why.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Names a strong skill with an example; Names a skill to practice with an example; Honest, specific reflection
    Model answer: I am strongest at ratio tables, because doubling and adding columns makes sense, like scaling 3 : 2 to 15 : 10. I want more practice dividing mixed numbers, like 2 1/3 ÷ 1 3/4, because I sometimes forget to change the mixed numbers to fractions first.

Checkpoint: Part A: Multiplying fractions

  1. 2/5 × 3/4 = ? Type in simplest form.
    Answer: 3/10
    6/20 = 3/10.
  2. 1 2/3 × 2 1/4 = ? Type a mixed number in simplest form.
    Answer: 3 3/4
    5/3 × 9/4 = 45/12 = 15/4 = 3 3/4.
  3. A garden bed is 3/4 of a yard wide and 4 2/3 yards long. What is its area in square yards? Type a mixed number.
    Answer: 3 1/2 square yards
    3/4 × 14/3 = 42/12 = 3 1/2.

Checkpoint: Part B: Dividing fractions

  1. 5/6 ÷ 5/12 = ?
    Answer: 2
    5/6 × 12/5 = 60/30 = 2.
  2. 3 1/3 ÷ 1 1/4 = ? Type a mixed number in simplest form.
    Answer: 2 2/3
    10/3 × 4/5 = 40/15 = 8/3 = 2 2/3.
  3. A 3 3/4-pound bag of trail mix is split into 3/8-pound bags. How many bags?
    Answer: 10 bags
    15/4 × 8/3 = 120/12 = 10.
  4. Write a real-world word problem that is solved by 3 ÷ 1/4, and solve it.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric (4 points toward the checkpoint).
    Rubric: Story asks how many 1/4-size groups fit in 3; Answer 12 with units
    Model answer: I have 3 cups of birdseed and each feeder holds 1/4 cup. How many feeders can I fill? 3 ÷ 1/4 = 12 feeders.

Checkpoint: Part C: Ratios and ratio tables

  1. A shelf has 14 novels and 21 comic books. Write the ratio of comic books to novels in simplest form.
    Answer: 3 : 2
    21 : 14; the GCF is 7, so 3 : 2.
  2. On the same shelf, write the ratio of novels to all books in simplest form.
    Answer: 2 : 5
    14 : 35 = 2 : 5.
  3. A paint mix is 3 parts red to 7 parts white. A painter uses 21 cups of white. How many cups of red?
    Answer: 9 cups
    21 = 7 × 3, so red is 3 × 3 = 9.
  4. Seeds and soil are mixed 2 : 9. A bucket holds 44 cups of the mix. How many cups are seeds?
    Answer: 8 cups
    2 + 9 = 11 boxes; 44 ÷ 11 = 4; seeds: 2 × 4 = 8.
  5. A smoothie uses 2 cups of mango for every 3 cups of yogurt. Complete the ratio table.
    Answer: 10; 9; 21
    Multiply both rows by the same number: 2 × 5 = 10, 3 × 3 = 9, 3 × 7 = 21.

Checkpoint: Part D: Money and reasoning

  1. Store A sells granola bars 6 for $4. Store B sells them 9 for $7. Scale both to 18 bars. What do 18 bars cost at the cheaper store?
    Answer: $12
    A: 6 for $4 × 3 = 18 for $12. B: 9 for $7 × 2 = 18 for $14.
  2. A family's $360 monthly food budget is split: 2/3 for groceries and the rest for eating out. How many dollars are for eating out?
    Answer: $120
    1/3 of $360 = $120.
  3. A recipe for 4 people uses 1 1/2 cups of beans. Kenji says that for 6 people he needs 3 1/2 cups, because he added 2 people and 2 cups. Is he right? Explain, and give the correct amount.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric (4 points toward the checkpoint).
    Rubric: Not right: adding does not keep the ratio; Correct: 6 is 1 1/2 times 4, so 1 1/2 × 1 1/2 = 2 1/4 cups (or ratio table: 2 people → 3/4 cup; 6 people → 2 1/4 cups)
    Model answer: No. Adding the same number does not keep the ratio. 4 people use 1 1/2 cups, so 2 people use 3/4 cup. 6 people use 3 × 3/4 = 2 1/4 cups.

Answer key: printable page

  1. 3/8 × 4/9 = ?
    Answer: 12/72 = 1/6
  2. 2 1/2 ÷ 5/8 = ?
    Answer: 5/2 × 8/5 = 4
  3. A class has 12 boys and 16 girls. Write the ratio of girls to all students in simplest form.
    Answer: 16 : 28 = 4 : 7
  4. Juice is 2 parts orange to 5 parts water. How much orange for 35 cups of water?
    Answer: 14 cups
  5. $180 is split 1/3 rent for a booth, 1/2 supplies, and the rest saved. How much is saved?
    Answer: 1/6 of $180 = $30
  6. Which is better: 4 for $5 or 6 for $8? Scale to 12.
    Answer: 12 for $15 vs 12 for $16; 4 for $5
