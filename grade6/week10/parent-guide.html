Week 10: Projects and the Unit 2 Checkpoint

Unit 2 comes together this week. Your child finishes three projects (an informational article, a cell model and a Nile Valley timeline with a Historian's Talk), reviews fractions and ratios with a real budget, and takes a checkpoint in every subject.

How scoring works: each lesson scores itself. Your child gets two tries per question: full points on the first try, half points on the second, then the answer and explanation appear. A score of 85 or higher means the lesson is mastered. Written answers go to Jonathan, who scores them with a rubric.

Checkpoint scoring: checkpoint questions give one try and do not show answers, so the checkpoint fairly measures what your child can do alone. Jonathan adds the points for written answers and shares the checkpoint score. Mastery is 85; below 85 means a review lesson, then a retake. Projects below 85 are returned with feedback and revised once.

Time this week: About 60 minutes of live lessons per subject. Plan extra time after class this week: about 2 hours to finish and publish the article, 2 hours for the cell model, 2 hours for the timeline and talk, 1 hour for the family event budget, and 30 to 40 minutes for each checkpoint.

Gather this week: Store ads or receipts and a real family event to budget for (Math); your child's Week 9 note cards, outline and sources, colored pencils, and a computer or paper for the final copy (ELA); a shoebox, paper plate or zipper bag, clay, beans, yarn, buttons, cardboard, gel or gelatin, index cards, glue, tape and markers for the cell model (Science); a long strip of paper or a poster (about 120 cm or more), a meter stick, colored pencils in three colors, index cards or sticky notes and a map of Africa (Social Studies); a phone or camera for photos and recordings.

Heads up: Checkpoints are done independently after class, without a calculator in math. Mastery is 85. Below 85 is not failure: your child gets a review lesson on what was missed and retakes it. The project directions and rubrics, the Unit 2 supply list and the book list are linked under Unit 2 on the Grade 6 page.

ELA: The Informational Article Project and Checkpoint

Your child turns their Week 9 note cards into an outline, writes an introduction with a central idea, body sections with headings and citations, and a conclusion, then revises and publishes. The checkpoint uses an original article about why sinkholes form in Florida.

Ask at dinner: "What is the one big idea your article teaches, and where did each fact come from?"

Try it together: Be the real audience. Read the published article and ask one question it made you curious about.

Signs it's clicking: Every section supports the central idea, and every borrowed fact has a citation. Signs they need help: The article is a list of facts. Ask: what does that fact mean, and why does it matter?

Math: Unit 2 Review, Budget Review and Checkpoint

Your child reviews when to multiply and when to divide fractions, uses ratios and ratio tables to plan a co-op Field Day budget, then plans a budget for a real family event before taking the checkpoint.

Ask at dinner: "How did you divide the budget, and what happens to money that's left over?"

Try it together: Choose a real event with a real budget and let your child plan it with fractions and price tables.

Signs it's clicking: They estimate first and can explain why an answer is reasonable. Signs they need help: They mix up multiplying and dividing. Ask: are we finding part of an amount, or how many pieces fit?

Life Science: Cell Model Project and Checkpoint

Your child connects the big ideas of the unit in a concept map, learns about Lynn Margulis and the evidence that mitochondria and chloroplasts were once bacteria, and builds a 3D cell model with a key, an analogy and a process, then takes the checkpoint.

Ask at dinner: "Give me the tour: what does each part do, and how do they depend on each other?"

Try it together: Take the 2-minute cell tour and ask one question.

Signs it's clicking: They explain how parts work together, not just what they are called. Signs they need help: The model looks good but they cannot explain it. Ask: what would go wrong if this part stopped working?

World and American History: Nile Valley Project and Checkpoint

Your child explores the Nile Valley's gifts to the world (the 365-day calendar, the alphabet, mathematics and medicine from Kemet, and the legacies of Kush and Aksum), builds a scale timeline and map, and gives a Historian's Talk, then takes the checkpoint.

Ask at dinner: "Why must the story of the Nile Valley include both Kemet and Kush?"

Try it together: Turn a capital A upside down together and find the ox's horns. Then listen to the Historian's Talk.

Signs it's clicking: They back up their claim with evidence from several weeks and place events correctly on the timeline. Signs they need help: Events are spaced evenly instead of to scale. Ask: how many centimeters stand for 500 years?
