Unit 2: The Nile Valley and Ancient Africa · Week 10 · World and American History · Teacher Plan

Unit 2 Checkpoint and Nile Valley Project

Lesson length: 60 minutes live, plus the after-class interactive lesson

Objectives

  • Describe lasting contributions of Kemet, Kush and Aksum to the world.
  • Explain how the English alphabet descends from Egyptian hieroglyphs.
  • Connect Egyptian mathematics and medicine to evidence in ancient papyri.
  • Plan an illustrated timeline and a historian's talk for the Nile Valley Project.
  • Show mastery of Unit 2 on the checkpoint (85 or higher).

Standards

  • SS.6.W.2.5: Summarize important achievements of Egyptian civilization.
  • SS.6.W.3.18: Describe the rise and fall of the ancient east African kingdoms of Kush and Axum and Christianity's development in Ethiopia.
  • SS.6.G.2.6: Explain the concept of cultural diffusion, and identify the influences of different ancient cultures on one another.
  • SS.6.W.1.1: Use timelines to identify chronology, continuity, and change in the ancient world.
  • SS.6.W.1.3: Interpret primary and secondary sources.

Materials

  • The interactive lesson
  • A long strip of paper or poster, a meter stick, colored pencils, index cards or sticky notes
  • A map of Africa
  • A phone or camera for the talk
  • Paper and pencil for the checkpoint

Vocabulary

  • legacy: Something handed down from the past that still affects the present.
  • papyrus: A paper-like writing material made from a Nile reed (plural: papyri).
  • unit fraction: A fraction with 1 as its numerator, such as 1/3 or 1/8.
  • Proto-Sinaitic: An early alphabet created about 1800 BCE from Egyptian hieroglyphs; the ancestor of most alphabets today.
  • civil calendar: Kemet's 365-day calendar of 12 months of 30 days plus 5 extra days.

Key questions to ask

  • What did the Nile Valley give the world?
  • How did ideas travel from one culture to another?
  • Why must the story include Kush as well as Kemet?
  • How do you know? What is your evidence?

Common mistakes to watch for

  • Thinking the alphabet was invented in Greece.
  • Believing Egypt's achievements were separate from Africa's.
  • Thinking that ideas belong only to the culture that started them (all cultures adapt ideas).
  • Placing events evenly on a timeline instead of to scale.

Support (if the student is struggling)

  • Provide a pre-marked timeline strip with the 500-year marks.
  • Give an event bank with dates for the student to choose from.
  • Offer a talk frame: claim, evidence 1 to 3, source, conclusion.

Challenge (if the student is ready for more)

  • Research the Wadi el-Hol inscriptions, among the earliest alphabetic writing, found in Egypt in 1999.
  • Use the Rhind Papyrus method to write 2/7 as a sum of unit fractions (1/4 + 1/28).

Background for the teacher

Proto-Sinaitic inscriptions (Serabit el-Khadim, Sinai) and the earlier Wadi el-Hol inscriptions (Egypt, discovered by John and Deborah Darnell, published 1999) date to roughly 1900 to 1800 BCE and adapt Egyptian signs acrophonically; the ox-head sign became Phoenician aleph, Greek alpha and Latin A. Egypt's civil calendar had 12 months of 30 days plus 5 epagomenal days; Julius Caesar's calendar reform (46 BCE) was guided by the Alexandrian astronomer Sosigenes. The Rhind Mathematical Papyrus (British Museum EA 10057 and 10058) was copied by Ahmose about 1550 BCE from a Middle Kingdom original and contains 84 problems (counts vary slightly by editor). The Edwin Smith Surgical Papyrus (New York Academy of Medicine) dates to about 1600 BCE and describes 48 cases. The Ge'ez (Ethiopic) script is used for Amharic and Tigrinya. The Nile Valley Project rubric is on the Unit 2 Projects page.

Answer key: interactive lesson

Reconnect

  1. Which Kushite king conquered Egypt around 728 BCE?
    Answer: Piye
    Piye, the Kushite king who conquered Egypt around 728 BCE.
  2. What was the Meroitic title for a royal woman, a queen mother or ruling queen?
    Answer: kandake
    Kandake, written Candace by the Greeks and Romans.
  3. Week 9: How did Kandake Amanirenas respond when Rome pushed into Kush, and how did the war end?
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Her forces attacked Roman Egypt; the war ended in a peace favorable to Kush
    Model answer: Her army attacked Roman forts in southern Egypt, and the war ended with a peace treaty in which Rome dropped its demand for tribute.
  4. Week 6: explain how the Nile's yearly flood made farming possible in Kemet.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: The flood left fertile silt and water so crops could grow beside the river
    Model answer: Each year the flood spread dark, fertile silt over the fields beside the river, so farmers could grow wheat and barley in the desert.

Learn 1: Gifts from Kemet: Calendar, Math and Medicine

  1. Kemet's civil calendar had 12 months of 30 days plus 5 extra days. How many days is that?
    Answer: 365 days
    12 × 30 = 360, plus 5 = 365.
  2. Write each fraction as Egyptians would, as a sum of different unit fractions.
    Answer: 6; 3
    2/3 − 1/2 = 4/6 − 3/6 = 1/6. 5/6 − 1/2 = 5/6 − 3/6 = 2/6 = 1/3. (Egyptians also had a special sign for 2/3.)
  3. Why do historians consider the Edwin Smith Papyrus an important step in medicine? Use details from the lesson.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: It follows a careful method: examination, diagnosis, treatment; It is honest about which injuries cannot be treated; one of the oldest medical texts
    Model answer: It treats injuries step by step, with an examination, a diagnosis and a treatment for each case, which is how doctors still work. It even admits when an injury cannot be treated, and it is one of the oldest medical texts known.

Learn 2: Gifts from Kush and Aksum

  1. Choose one legacy each from Kemet, Kush and Aksum, and explain how each one still matters today or changed how we understand history.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Kemet: calendar, alphabet origin, math or medicine; Kush: pyramids, iron, Meroitic writing, Amanirenas; Aksum: coins, trade, early Christianity, Ge'ez script; An explanation of why each matters
    Model answer: Kemet's 365-day calendar led to the calendar we use today. Kush's more than 200 pyramids show that pyramid building was an African tradition shared by more than one kingdom. Aksum's Ge'ez script grew into the writing that millions of people in Ethiopia and Eritrea still use.
  2. The alphabet passed from Egypt to the Phoenicians, Greeks and Romans. Explain how this is an example of cultural diffusion, and give one more example from Unit 2.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Cultural diffusion: ideas spread from one culture to another and are adapted; Another accurate example: Kush adopting Egyptian gods and hieroglyphs; Rome borrowing the Egyptian calendar; Aksum adopting Christianity and Greek writing on coins
    Model answer: Cultural diffusion is when an idea spreads from one culture to another and is changed along the way. The alphabet began with Egyptian hieroglyphs and was adapted by each people who used it. Another example is Kush borrowing Egyptian gods and hieroglyphs but keeping its own customs.
  3. Ge'ez, the script of Aksum, developed into the writing used for which major language of Ethiopia today?
    Answer: Amharic (also Tigrinya)
    Amharic and Tigrinya are written in the Ge'ez (Ethiopic) script.

Learn 3: Planning Your Nile Valley Project

  1. If your timeline uses 1 cm for every 50 years, how many centimeters long must it be to run from 5000 BCE to 940 CE? (Hint: 5000 BCE to 1 CE is 5,000 years.)
    Answer: about 119 cm (5,940 years ÷ 50)
    5,000 + 940 = 5,940 years (ignoring that there is no year 0). 5,940 ÷ 50 = 118.8 cm, about 119 cm.
  2. List the 12 events you plan to include, with approximate dates, in time order.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: 12 accurate events from Weeks 6 to 10; Approximate dates correct; In chronological order; Both Kemet and Kush (and Aksum) represented
    Model answer: Sahara dries, people settle by the Nile (about 5000 BCE); Qustul incense burner and A-Group royal burials (about 3200 BCE; historians debate what they show); Kemet unified (about 3100 BCE); Great Pyramid (about 2560 BCE); Kerma becomes capital of Kush (about 2500 BCE); Kush raids Egypt (about 1575 BCE); Thutmose I conquers Kerma (about 1500 BCE); Kush independent (about 1070 BCE); Piye conquers Egypt (about 728 BCE); Assyrians sack Thebes (663 BCE); Amanirenas and Rome make peace (about 21 BCE); Ezana makes Aksum Christian (about 340 CE).
  3. Draft the claim and the three pieces of evidence for your Historian's Talk.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: A clear claim answering why both Kemet and Kush belong in the story; Three accurate, specific pieces of evidence from the unit
    Model answer: Claim: The story of the Nile Valley is incomplete without Kush, because Kemet and Kush shaped each other for 3,000 years. Evidence: early Upper Egyptian cultures shared roots with Nubia; Kush invaded Egypt around 1575 BCE and later ruled it as the 25th Dynasty; and Kush kept building pyramids long after Egypt stopped.

Hands-On

  1. Write the outline or script of your Historian's Talk: claim, three pieces of evidence with explanations, one source a historian would use, and a conclusion.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Clear claim; Three accurate pieces of evidence, each explained; Names a real type of source (an inscription, papyrus, coin, archaeological site, ancient writer); Conclusion
    Model answer: Claim: The story of the Nile Valley must include both Kemet and Kush, because the two shaped each other for 3,000 years. Evidence 1: Early Upper Egyptian cultures shared pottery and cattle traditions with Nubia, so they grew from related roots. Evidence 2: Kush was strong enough to invade Egypt around 1575 BCE, as the Sobeknakht inscription shows, and later its kings ruled Egypt as the 25th Dynasty. Evidence 3: Kush kept building pyramids for centuries after Egypt stopped, leaving more than 200. Source: the Sobeknakht inscription, found in 2003. Conclusion: Telling only Egypt's story leaves out half of the Nile Valley and makes Africa's history seem smaller than it was.
  2. After your talk, what question did your audience ask, and what part of your timeline helped you answer it?
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Reports a real question; Connects the answer to the timeline or map
    Model answer: My aunt asked why Kush moved its capital to Meroe. I pointed to the timeline showing the raid on Napata around 593 BCE and to the map showing Meroe's iron and farmland.

Think Deeper

  1. Answer the unit's big question in a well-organized paragraph: What made the civilizations of the Nile Valley great, and why does it matter that their story be told fully and accurately? Use evidence from at least three different weeks.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Evidence from at least three weeks (Nile geography, Kemet as African, Nubia and Kerma, Kushite pharaohs, Meroe, Aksum, legacies); Explains what made them great (geography, farming, trade, leadership, ideas); Explains why a full and accurate story matters (racism in history writing, African achievement, accuracy); Organized with transitions
    Model answer: The civilizations of the Nile Valley became great because they used their geography and their ideas well. The Nile's yearly flood created the Black Land, where farmers grew enough food to support cities and kings. Kemet, an African civilization with roots shared with Nubia, created a 365-day calendar, careful mathematics and medicine. To the south, Kerma and later Napata and Meroe grew rich from gold, iron and trade, and Kush's kings even ruled Egypt as the 25th Dynasty. Aksum then joined Africa to the wider world through Red Sea trade, coins and the Ge'ez script. Telling this story fully matters because, as we learned in Week 7, some writers in the 1800s cut Egypt out of Africa for racist reasons. An accurate history shows that Africans built some of the world's earliest and most important civilizations.
  2. How has your thinking about ancient Africa changed during Unit 2? Describe one idea you held before and what evidence changed or strengthened it.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Names a prior idea honestly; Names specific evidence from the unit; Explains the change or strengthening
    Model answer: Before this unit, I thought ancient Egypt was the only great kingdom in Africa. Learning about Kerma's Deffufa, Kush's invasion of Egypt and Amanirenas's war with Rome showed me that Nubia was just as powerful, and sometimes stronger.

Checkpoint: Part A: The Nile and Kemet

  1. In which direction does the Nile flow?
    Answer: north
    The Nile flows north, from the highlands of East and Central Africa to the Mediterranean.
  2. Kemet, Egypt's own name for itself, means what in English?
    Answer: the Black Land
    Kemet means the Black Land, for the dark, fertile silt left by the flood.
  3. Upper Egypt is in the south, and Lower Egypt is in the north. Explain why, using the direction the Nile flows.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric (3 points toward the checkpoint).
    Rubric: Upper means upstream (higher land, closer to the source); the Nile flows north, so upstream is south
    Model answer: The names come from the river. The Nile flows north, so the upstream part, closer to the source and higher, is in the south and is called Upper Egypt.

Checkpoint: Part B: Kemet as an African civilization

  1. Give three different kinds of evidence that Kemet was an African civilization, with one example of each.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric (4 points toward the checkpoint).
    Rubric: Three different kinds (geography, archaeology, culture, language, written sources, biology) with accurate examples
    Model answer: Geography: Kemet is in Africa, and its water comes from East and Central Africa. Language: Egyptian belongs to the Afroasiatic family with Hausa and Somali. Archaeology: early Upper Egyptian pottery and cattle traditions matched Nubia's.
  2. Why did some European writers in the 1800s claim that Egypt was not truly African, and what evidence proves them wrong?
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric (3 points toward the checkpoint).
    Rubric: Racist beliefs that Black Africans could not build civilizations; justified colonialism and slavery; Evidence of African roots (geography, language, culture, archaeology)
    Model answer: They held racist beliefs that Black Africans could not build great civilizations, which helped justify colonialism and slavery. Evidence such as the Afroasiatic language family and the shared traditions of Upper Egypt and Nubia proves Kemet was African.
  3. Which Senegalese scholar's 1974 presentation at a UNESCO meeting in Cairo argued, with Théophile Obenga, for Egypt's African origins?
    Answer: Cheikh Anta Diop
    Cheikh Anta Diop.

Checkpoint: Part C: Nubia and Kush

  1. What does Ta-Seti mean?
    Answer: the Land of the Bow
    Nubian archers were famous for their skill.
  2. Which city near the Third Cataract was the capital of the first Kingdom of Kush?
    Answer: Kerma
    Kerma, about 2500 to 1500 BCE.
  3. What did the Sobeknakht inscription, found in 2003, reveal, and what does it teach about the bias of royal records?
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric (3 points toward the checkpoint).
    Rubric: Kush and its allies invaded deep into Egypt (about 1575 to 1550 BCE); Royal records celebrated victories and hid defeats; new evidence can change history
    Model answer: It revealed that an army from Kush and its allies invaded deep into Egypt around 1575 to 1550 BCE. Egyptian royal records said little about it, which shows that rulers' records hide defeats, and new evidence can change what we know.

Checkpoint: Part D: The Kushite pharaohs, Meroe and Aksum

  1. What number dynasty were the Kushite kings who ruled Egypt?
    Answer: 25th
    The 25th Dynasty, about 747 to 656 BCE.
  2. Give two reasons Meroe became a center of iron making and trade.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric (3 points toward the checkpoint).
    Rubric: Iron ore and acacia forests for fuel; Farmland, summer rain, trade routes to the Red Sea
    Model answer: Meroe had iron ore and acacia forests to fuel its furnaces, and it sat on trade routes to the Red Sea.
  3. Explain one cause of Aksum's rise and one cause of its decline.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric (3 points toward the checkpoint).
    Rubric: Rise: Red Sea trade through Adulis, coins; Decline: trade routes shifted, land worn out (by about 940 CE)
    Model answer: Aksum rose by controlling Red Sea trade through its port of Adulis. It declined as trade shifted away from the Red Sea and its farmland wore out.
  4. Put these events in time order, earliest first.
    Answer: Kemet is unified → Kerma becomes the capital of Kush → Piye conquers Egypt → Amanirenas's forces raid Roman Egypt → Ezana makes Aksum a Christian kingdom
    About 3100 BCE, 2500 BCE, 728 BCE, 25 BCE and 340 CE.

Answer key: printable page

  1. How does the letter A come from an Egyptian hieroglyph?
    Answer: An ox-head hieroglyph was used for the first sound of 'alp (ox), then passed to the Phoenicians, Greeks and Romans.
  2. Describe Kemet's civil calendar.
    Answer: 365 days: 12 months of 30 days plus 5 extra days.
  3. Write 3/4 as Egyptians would, as a sum of unit fractions.
    Answer: 1/2 + 1/4
  4. Name one legacy each of Kush and Aksum.
    Answer: Kush: more than 200 pyramids, iron, Meroitic writing. Aksum: coins, Ge'ez script, early Christianity.
  5. What is cultural diffusion? Give an example from Unit 2.
    Answer: The spread and adaptation of ideas between cultures; e.g. the alphabet, or Kush adopting Egyptian gods.
  6. Why must the Nile Valley's story include both Kemet and Kush?
    Answer: They shaped each other for 3,000 years through shared roots, trade, war and rule; leaving out Kush is inaccurate.
