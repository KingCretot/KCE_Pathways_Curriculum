Unit 5: Empires and Global Trade · Week 22 · World and American History · Teacher Plan

Mali and Mansa Musa

Lesson length: 60 minutes live, plus the after-class interactive lesson

Objectives

  • Explain how Sundiata Keita founded the Mali Empire and how the epic of Sundiata is preserved by griots.
  • Describe how Mali grew rich and powerful through gold, salt and trade, and how it was governed.
  • Explain the causes and effects of Mansa Musa's hajj to Mecca.
  • Explain how Mali's rulers supported Islam and learning.
  • Compare oral tradition and written accounts as historical sources.

Standards

  • SS.6.G.2.1: Explain how major physical characteristics, natural resources, climate, and absolute and relative locations have influenced settlement, interactions, and the economies of ancient civilizations of the world.
  • SS.6.E.3.4: Describe the relationship among civilizations that engage in trade, including the benefits and drawbacks of voluntary trade.
  • SS.6.W.1.3: Interpret primary and secondary sources.
  • SS.6.W.1.5: Describe the roles of historians and recognize varying historical interpretations (historiography).
  • SS.6.W.1.6: Describe how history transmits culture and heritage and provides models of human character.

Materials

  • The interactive lesson
  • Week 1 interview notes or a trusted adult to interview, paper and pencil, optional recorder and drum
  • About 40 pennies or yellow paper squares, about 10 household items
  • Printable practice page

Vocabulary

  • mansa: The title of the emperor of Mali, meaning king or emperor.
  • griot: A West African oral historian, storyteller and musician who preserves a people's history.
  • oral tradition: History, stories and knowledge passed down by speaking and memory.
  • hajj: The pilgrimage to Mecca that Muslims who are able are expected to make at least once in their lives.
  • pilgrimage: A journey to a sacred place for religious reasons.
  • inflation: A general rise in prices, often caused when a currency or money becomes less scarce.
  • epic: A long story about a hero's deeds, important to a people's history.

Key questions to ask

  • How do we know what we know about Mali?
  • What made Mali richer than Ghana?
  • Why did gold lose value in Cairo?
  • What does an outsider's account reveal, and what does it hide?

Common mistakes to watch for

  • Africa had no written history (Arabic-script manuscripts were written and collected in Mali's cities; Week 23 explores Timbuktu's libraries).
  • Oral history is just made-up stories.
  • Mansa Musa was the first ruler of Mali.
  • All of Mali's people were Muslim.

Support (if the student is struggling)

  • Use a Ghana-and-Mali comparison chart.
  • Listen to a griot performance with kora music (with a parent).
  • Pre-teach mansa, griot and hajj.

Challenge (if the student is ready for more)

  • Read a young readers' version of the Epic of Sundiata and compare it with this lesson.
  • Compare Ibn Battuta's praise of Mali's justice with the Manden Charter's principles.

Background for the teacher

Dates: the Battle of Kirina is traditionally dated about 1235; Mansa Musa's reign is usually given as about 1312 to 1337; his hajj began in 1324, and al-Umari, writing in Cairo about twelve years later, reported that the value of gold there had not recovered. Caravan figures (often 60,000 people, 12,000 enslaved servants, 80 to 100 camel loads of gold) come from later Arabic writers and should be treated as uncertain. The Catalan Atlas (1375) is attributed to Abraham Cresques of Majorca. Ibn Battuta visited Mali in 1352 to 1353; the excerpt is from H. A. R. Gibb, Ibn Battuta: Travels in Asia and Africa 1325-1354 (Broadway Travellers, 1929), checked against the scanned text; the sentence just before it uses a term for Black Africans common in 1929 and is omitted. The architect role of al-Sahili is traditional and debated. The Manden Charter was inscribed on UNESCO's Representative List of the Intangible Cultural Heritage of Humanity in 2009. Medieval West Africa is outside Florida's Grade 6 NGSSS world history range; the standards above cover the skills taught.

Answer key: interactive lesson

Reconnect

  1. What two goods were the heart of the trans-Saharan trade?
    Answer: gold and salt
    Gold north, salt south.
  2. What did the Soninke people call the kingdom of Ghana?
    Answer: Wagadu
    Week 21.
  3. Why did Ghana's king keep all gold nuggets? Finish: to keep gold ___.
    Answer: scarce (and valuable)
    Today: what happened when a lot of gold arrived at once.
  4. Al-Bakri never visited Ghana. What other kinds of sources could historians use to learn about West African kingdoms?
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Archaeology, oral traditions (griots), travelers who did visit, maps, objects
    Model answer: Historians can use archaeology at places like Koumbi Saleh, the oral histories kept by griots, and accounts by travelers who actually visited.

Learn 1: Sundiata and the Griots

  1. Who founded the Mali Empire after the Battle of Kirina?
    Answer: Sundiata Keita
    Around 1235.
  2. Griot families served royal families. How might that shape the way the epic portrays Sundiata and Sumanguru, and how could a historian check it?
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Explains how serving a royal family could shape the story (hero made greater, enemy made worse); A way to check (other griot versions, Arabic written sources, archaeology)
    Model answer: Griots who served the Keita family had reasons to make Sundiata a great hero and Sumanguru a cruel sorcerer-king. A historian could compare versions from different griot families, check Arabic writers like Ibn Khaldun, and look at archaeology from the period.

Learn 2: How Mali Grew Rich and Was Governed

  1. Give one way Mali was similar to Ghana and one way it was different.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Similar: grew rich from the gold-salt trade, taxes, tribute; based in the Sahel near the Niger; Different: Mali controlled the gold fields themselves, was larger, and had stronger ties to Islam and Islamic learning
    Model answer: Like Ghana, Mali grew rich from the gold and salt trade and from taxing traders. But Mali was much larger and controlled the gold fields themselves, not just the trade routes.
  2. Which river was Mali's main highway for boats and trade?
    Answer: the Niger River
    It linked Djenné, Timbuktu and Gao.

Learn 3: Mansa Musa's Hajj

  1. In what year did Mansa Musa begin his hajj?
    Answer: 1324
    He reached Cairo that year.
  2. Explain, using what you learned about Ghana's nugget rule, why Mansa Musa's gold made the value of gold fall in Cairo.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Gold's value depends on scarcity; Flooding Cairo with gold made it much less scarce, so each piece was worth less (prices rose); Connects to Ghana keeping gold scarce
    Model answer: Ghana's kings kept gold scarce so it stayed valuable. Mansa Musa did the opposite in Cairo: he spent and gave away so much gold that it was no longer scarce there, so each piece of gold was worth less and prices went up.
  3. Mansa Musa ruled from about 1312 to 1337. About how many years did he rule?
    Answer: 25 years
    1337 − 1312 = 25.

Learn 4: Ibn Battuta's Visit

  1. Which Moroccan traveler visited Mali in 1352 to 1353?
    Answer: Ibn Battuta
    He visited during Mansa Sulayman's reign.
  2. Reread the excerpt. Name two things Ibn Battuta says made Mali safe and just, and explain why a traveler's safety mattered so much to a trading empire.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Two details from the excerpt (people seldom unjust and hate injustice; the ruler punishes any injustice; no fear of robbers or violent men); Connects safe roads to merchants, caravans and trade wealth
    Model answer: He says the people hate injustice and the ruler punishes even small acts of it, so no one has to fear robbers. Safety mattered because merchants with gold and salt would only travel and trade where their goods and lives were safe, and that trade made Mali rich.
  3. Ibn Battuta praised Mali's safety and justice but criticized some customs. What does this tell you about using his account as a source?
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: He saw Mali firsthand, so his praise is valuable evidence; His criticism reflects his own culture's expectations (bias), so historians separate what he observed from his judgments
    Model answer: Because he actually traveled there, his praise of Mali's safety and justice is strong evidence. But his criticism shows he judged Mali by the customs of his own homeland, so historians have to separate what he saw from his opinions about it.

Hands-On

  1. Summarize the story you retold. What changed between your Week 1 notes and your retelling, and what does that show about how oral tradition can stay true, or change, over 800 years?
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: A summary of the family story; Honest reflection on what changed in retelling; Connects to the strengths and limits of oral tradition
    Model answer: My grandmother grew up on a farm in Georgia and moved to Jacksonville at 17 to work in a shipyard during the war; her lesson was "education is the one thing no one can take from you." When I told it from memory, I mixed up the year she moved and said Savannah instead of Jacksonville. It showed me that oral history keeps the meaning and lessons strong, but details can change, which is why griots train for years and historians compare their stories with other evidence.
  2. Record the prices before and after the gold arrived. What happened, and how does this model what happened in Cairo in 1324?
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Prices recorded; Prices rose after more gold arrived; Explains: more gold chasing the same goods made gold worth less (inflation), like Cairo
    Model answer: Before: items sold for 1 to 3 gold. After Mansa Musa gave everyone 10 more gold, the same kinds of items sold for 6 to 9 gold. With more gold but the same number of goods, each piece of gold bought less, just like in Cairo when Mansa Musa's gold made prices rise.

Think Deeper

  1. Was Mansa Musa's hajj good for Mali? Weigh at least two positive effects and one possible negative effect, and give your judgment.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Two positive effects (fame, trade ties, scholars and architects, mosques and learning in Timbuktu); One possible negative (spending vast wealth; attracting attention from outsiders, including Europeans seeking African gold; inflation hurt Cairo); A supported judgment
    Model answer: The hajj helped Mali in many ways: it made Mali famous across Africa, Asia and Europe, strengthened trade with the Islamic world, and brought scholars and the architect al-Sahili, who helped build mosques and make Timbuktu a center of learning. But it cost an enormous amount of gold, and the fame drew the attention of outsiders, including European explorers who later sailed down Africa's coast looking for the source of that gold. Overall, I think it was good for Mali, because the learning and connections it brought lasted for centuries.
  2. Compare the griots' Epic of Sundiata with Ibn Battuta's written account as sources. What can each tell historians that the other cannot?
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Griots: Mande people's own view of their history, values, heroes, genealogies; but legend and changes over time; Ibn Battuta: a dated, eyewitness description of daily life and government in 1352 to 1353; but an outsider's judgments; A thoughtful comparison
    Model answer: The Epic of Sundiata tells us how the Mande people themselves remember their founding, their heroes and their values, which no outsider could provide, though it mixes history and legend and has changed over 800 years. Ibn Battuta gives a dated eyewitness description of daily life, safety and government in 1352 to 1353, details the epic doesn't include, but he judged Mali by his own customs. Together they give a much fuller picture than either alone.

Exit Check

  1. What is a West African oral historian and storyteller called?
    Answer: a griot (jeli)
    They preserved the Epic of Sundiata.
  2. What title did Mali's emperors hold?
    Answer: mansa
    Mansa Musa is the most famous.
  3. What happened to the value of gold in Cairo after Mansa Musa's visit, and why?
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Gold lost value; Because so much arrived that it was no longer scarce
    Model answer: It fell, because so much gold arrived that it was no longer scarce.
  4. Name one lasting effect of Mansa Musa's hajj.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: A valid effect (fame on maps, scholars and architects, Djinguereber Mosque, Timbuktu learning, trade ties)
    Model answer: He brought back scholars and an architect who helped build the Djinguereber Mosque in Timbuktu, which still stands.

Answer key: printable page

  1. Who was Sundiata Keita, and how do we know his story?
    Answer: Founder of Mali after defeating Sumanguru at Kirina about 1235; his story is preserved by griots in the Epic of Sundiata.
  2. How was Mali's wealth different from Ghana's?
    Answer: Mali controlled the gold fields themselves as well as the trade routes, and was larger.
  3. List three effects of Mansa Musa's hajj.
    Answer: Fame on maps; inflation in Cairo; scholars such as al-Sahili; the Djinguereber Mosque in Timbuktu; stronger trade ties.
  4. Why did gold lose value in Cairo?
    Answer: So much gold arrived at once that it was no longer scarce.
  5. What made Timbuktu important?
    Answer: A center of trade and Islamic learning with scholars, mosques and many manuscripts.
  6. Compare oral tradition and written accounts as sources.
    Answer: Oral: insiders' memory and values, may change; written: dated and detailed, but may reflect the writer's bias.
