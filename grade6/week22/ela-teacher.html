Unit 5: Novel Study · Week 22 · ELA · Teacher Plan

Conflict and Character Change

Lesson length: 60 minutes live, plus the after-class interactive lesson

Objectives

  • Identify and explain types of conflict: character versus character, nature, society and self.
  • Trace how conflicts drive a character's change using text evidence.
  • Distinguish direct and indirect characterization.
  • Analyze how London presents Buck's change as both adaptation and loss.
  • Use the roots morph, mort and vit, and context clues, to understand challenging words.

Standards

  • ELA.6.R.1.1: Explain how the setting impacts conflict and characterization in a literary text.
  • ELA.6.R.1.2: Analyze the development of stated or implied theme(s) throughout a literary text.
  • ELA.6.R.1.3: Explain the influence of narrator(s) and point of view on a literary text.
  • ELA.6.V.1.2: Apply knowledge of Greek and Latin roots and affixes to determine meanings of words and phrases in grade-level content.
  • ELA.K12.EE.1.1: Cite evidence to explain and justify reasoning.

Materials

  • The interactive lesson
  • The Call of the Wild, Chapters II and III
  • Index cards or sticky notes in 4 colors, tape or string, markers
  • Printable practice page

Vocabulary

  • conflict: A struggle between opposing forces that drives a story's plot.
  • external conflict: A struggle between a character and an outside force: another character, nature or society.
  • internal conflict: A struggle within a character's own mind.
  • protagonist: The main character of a story.
  • antagonist: The character or force that opposes the protagonist.
  • characterization: How an author reveals a character, directly (telling) or indirectly (showing through actions, words and thoughts).
  • adaptation: A change that helps a living thing survive in its environment.

Key questions to ask

  • Who or what is Buck struggling against?
  • How did this conflict change him?
  • Is the narrator telling us or showing us?
  • Is this change growth, loss or both?

Common mistakes to watch for

  • Conflict means only a fight.
  • The antagonist must be a villain or a person.
  • A character's change is always for the better.
  • London approves of everything Buck does.

Support (if the student is struggling)

  • Read Chapters II and III aloud together or use a free audiobook.
  • Provide a conflict-type chart.
  • Mark key passages with sticky notes.

Challenge (if the student is ready for more)

  • Track how London uses the words civilized, primitive and primordial across the chapters.
  • Compare Buck's change with a character from another book who must adapt to survive.

Background for the teacher

Content note: Chapter II includes Curly's violent death; Chapter III ends with Buck and Spitz's fight to the death. Both are central to the plot; preview them and decide how to read them with your child. The lesson quotes the Gutenberg text exactly. London's portrayal of François and Perrault as French-Canadian couriers reflects the real Canadian government mail service on the Yukon trail in 1897 to 1898.

Sources

  • Jack London, The Call of the Wild (1903), Project Gutenberg.
  • Library of America, Jack London: Novels and Stories (notes).

How the lesson runs

During the live lesson, the student shares their screen and answers the Reconnect questions and the Learn checks live while you teach. The Hands-On activity, Think Deeper and Exit Check are completed after class.

Answer key: interactive lesson

Reconnect

  1. Where did Buck live before he was stolen? (Name the valley.)
    Answer: the Santa Clara Valley, California
    Judge Miller's place.
  2. What lesson did the man in the red sweater teach Buck? Finish the phrase: "a man with a club was a ___."
    Answer: lawgiver (a master to be obeyed)
    Chapter I.
  3. The root prim means ___.
    Answer: first
    Today: primordial, from the very first times.
  4. Last week you predicted how Buck would change. Was your prediction right after Chapters II and III? Explain with one detail.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Compares the prediction to what happened; One accurate detail from Chapters II or III
    Model answer: I predicted Buck would get tougher, and I was right: in Chapter II he learns to steal bacon to survive, and by Chapter III he and Spitz hate each other.

Learn 1: Types of Conflict

  1. Name the type of conflict.
    Answer: nature; character
    Snow and cold are nature; Spitz is another character.
  2. Describe Buck's internal conflict in Chapters II and III. What two sides are fighting inside him?
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Civilized habits and morals from the Southland (respecting property, rules); versus wild, primitive instincts needed to survive (stealing, fighting, cunning)
    Model answer: Inside Buck, the civilized habits he learned at Judge Miller's, like respecting property and feelings, are fighting the wild, primitive instincts he needs to survive in the North, like stealing food and being cunning.

Learn 2: Adaptation or Loss? Chapter II

  1. London says the theft "marked Buck as fit to survive" and also marked "the decay ... of his moral nature." Why does he describe the same act in two opposite ways? What is he asking readers to think about?
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Stealing helps Buck survive (adaptation) but means giving up his old morals (loss); London wants readers to question whether survival justifies losing morals, or whether morals depend on the setting
    Model answer: Stealing helps Buck survive, so it's a smart adaptation, but it also means giving up the honesty he had in the Southland. London describes it both ways so readers will wonder whether surviving is worth losing your morals, and whether right and wrong depend on where you live.
  2. London contrasts the "law of love and fellowship" in the Southland with what law in the Northland?
    Answer: the law of club and fang
    The chapter title.
  3. Use the roots.
    Answer: vital; mortal
    vit = life; mort = death.

Learn 3: How London Reveals Character

  1. "Buck stole the bacon so cleverly that Dub was punished instead." Is this direct or indirect characterization?
    Answer: indirect
    It shows Buck's cunning through an action.
  2. What lesson does Buck learn from what happens to Curly, and how does it change his behavior?
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Once a dog goes down in a fight, the others finish it; there is no fair play; Buck resolves never to go down; he becomes watchful and careful in fights
    Model answer: Buck learns that in this world there is no fair play: once a dog goes down, it's finished. After that, he decides he will never go down in a fight, and he becomes much more watchful and careful.

Learn 4: Rising Conflict: Buck and Spitz

  1. London says the "primordial beast" in Buck grew in secret. What does Buck do, and not do, that shows his wildness is growing quietly?
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: He avoids fights and acts with patience and control instead of rashness; He waits and plans, using cunning; his strength and instincts grow without showing
    Model answer: Buck doesn't pick fights and stays patient even though he hates Spitz. He waits, watches and plans, which shows his cunning and wild instincts are growing without him showing them yet.
  2. Why is Spitz the antagonist? Explain what makes the conflict between Buck and Spitz so serious.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Spitz opposes Buck and sees him as a threat to his leadership; Only one can lead the team; in this world defeat means death
    Model answer: Spitz opposes Buck at every turn because he sees Buck as a threat to his position as lead dog. The conflict is serious because only one dog can lead, and in the law of club and fang, losing a fight can mean death.

Hands-On

  1. List five conflicts from Chapters II and III, labeled by type, and the change each caused in Buck. Where on your timeline did he change the most?
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Five accurate conflicts with types; Change caused by each; A supported choice of the biggest change
    Model answer: 1. Dogs attack Curly (character vs. character): Buck vows never to go down. 2. The first snowfall at night (nature): he learns to dig a sleeping hole. 3. Hunger on the trail (nature): he learns to eat fast and steal food. 4. The club rule and team life (society): he learns to obey and to pull in harness. 5. Old morals vs. new needs (self): he steals bacon and lets another dog take the blame. He changed most at the bacon theft, because London says it marked the "decay" of his moral nature.

Think Deeper

  1. Is Buck's change in Chapters II and III growth or loss? Take a position, support it with at least two quotations, and acknowledge the other view.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: A clear position; Two accurate quotations; Explains how the evidence supports the position; Acknowledges the opposing view fairly
    Model answer: Buck's change is mostly growth, because it is the only way he can survive. London says the first theft "marked Buck as fit to survive in the hostile Northland environment," and that his "adaptability" saved him from "swift and terrible death." He learns to sleep in snow, eat fast and avoid foolish fights, all signs of a stronger, smarter dog. Still, London also calls it "the decay or going to pieces of his moral nature," and Buck lets an innocent dog be punished for his theft. Readers who value honesty above everything would call that a loss. I think London wants us to see both, but in the brutal Northland, survival comes first.
  2. Think of a time you (or someone you know, or a character in another book) had to change to fit into a new place: a move, a new team, a new school or co-op. What did you gain, and did you lose anything? Compare it with Buck.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: A specific real or fictional example; Gains and losses identified; A thoughtful comparison with Buck's change
    Model answer: When my family moved from New York to Florida, I had to get used to a new homeschool co-op where everyone already had friends. I learned to be braver about talking to new people, which was a real gain. I lost some of the inside jokes and traditions I had with my old friends. Like Buck, I adapted to survive in a new place, but unlike Buck I didn't have to give up my values. I just had to add new skills.

Exit Check

  1. Buck against the freezing snow is what type of conflict?
    Answer: character versus nature
    An external conflict.
  2. Who is the antagonist in Chapter III?
    Answer: Spitz
    The lead dog who opposes Buck.
  3. The root mort means ___.
    Answer: death
    mortal, mortality.
  4. Give one way Buck changed in Chapters II and III and the conflict that caused it.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: An accurate change linked to a specific conflict
    Model answer: Buck learned to steal food secretly because hunger on the trail (a conflict with nature) made it the only way to get enough to eat.

Answer key: printable page

  1. Name the four types of conflict and give an example of each from Chapters II and III.
    Answer: vs. character (Spitz), vs. nature (snow, hunger), vs. society (the law of club and fang, the drivers' clubs), vs. self (civilized morals vs. wild instincts).
  2. What does London mean by "the law of club and fang"?
    Answer: In the North, survival depends on force: men's clubs and dogs' teeth make the rules.
  3. Explain how Buck's theft of food shows both adaptation and loss.
    Answer: It helps him survive (adaptation) but marks the decay of his moral nature (loss).
  4. Give one example each of direct and indirect characterization of Buck.
    Answer: Direct: "He was fit, that was all." Indirect: he digs a sleeping hole after watching Billee.
  5. Why is the conflict between Buck and Spitz so serious?
    Answer: Only one can lead the team, and in the North, losing a fight can mean death.
  6. Use the roots: what do mortal, survive and metamorphosis have to do with mort, viv and morph?
    Answer: mortal: will die (death); survive: stay alive (life); metamorphosis: change of form (shape).
