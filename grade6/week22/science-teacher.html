Unit 5: Ecosystems · Week 22 · Life Science · Teacher Plan

Food Chains, Food Webs and Energy

Lesson length: 60 minutes live, plus the after-class interactive lesson

Objectives

  • Classify organisms as producers, consumers (herbivores, carnivores, omnivores) and decomposers.
  • Construct food chains and food webs that show the direction of energy flow.
  • Explain why only about 10 percent of energy passes from one level to the next, using an energy pyramid.
  • Predict how removing or adding a species affects a food web.
  • Explain biomagnification using the example of mercury in the Everglades.

Standards

  • SC.7.L.17.1: Explain and illustrate the roles of and relationships among producers, consumers, and decomposers in the process of energy transfer in a food web.
  • SC.7.L.17.3: Describe and investigate various limiting factors in the local ecosystem and their impact on native populations, including food, shelter, water, space, disease, parasitism, predation, and nesting sites.
  • SC.8.L.18.4: Cite evidence that living systems follow the Laws of Conservation of Mass and Energy (preview).
  • NGSS MS-LS2-3: Develop a model to describe the cycling of matter and flow of energy among living and nonliving parts of an ecosystem.

Materials

  • The interactive lesson
  • A ball of yarn, index cards, a marker, tape or safety pins
  • 4 clear cups, a pitcher or 4-cup measuring cup, water, a 1/4 teaspoon or dropper
  • Printable practice page

Vocabulary

  • producer: An organism that makes its own food, usually by photosynthesis.
  • consumer: An organism that gets energy by eating other organisms.
  • decomposer: An organism, such as a fungus or bacterium, that breaks down dead matter and returns nutrients to the soil and water.
  • food chain: A single path showing how energy passes from one organism to another.
  • food web: Many connected food chains in an ecosystem.
  • trophic level: A feeding level in a food chain, such as producers or primary consumers.
  • biomagnification: The buildup of a toxin in organisms at higher levels of a food chain.

Key questions to ask

  • Where does this organism get its energy?
  • Which way does the arrow point, and why?
  • Where did the other 90 percent go?
  • What happens if this species disappears?

Common mistakes to watch for

  • Arrows point from the eater to the food.
  • Energy is recycled like matter.
  • Top predators have the most energy.
  • Decomposers aren't important.

Support (if the student is struggling)

  • Color-code trophic levels on the food web.
  • Use the phrase "arrow means 'is eaten by' / 'gives energy to.'"
  • Pre-fill some yarn-web cards.

Challenge (if the student is ready for more)

  • Research the snail kite's recovery and how it has adapted to eating invasive island apple snails.
  • Calculate how much producer energy is needed to support one panther, using the 10% rule over four levels.

Background for the teacher

Florida NGSSS places food webs in Grade 7 (SC.7.L.17.1); KCE teaches them here per the Grade 6 scope. A panther found dead in the Everglades in 1989 had its death attributed to probable mercury poisoning (Florida Fish and Wildlife Conservation Commission; Roelke and others). Dorcas and colleagues (2012, PNAS) reported declines of 99 percent in raccoon sightings, nearly 99 percent in opossums and 87 percent in bobcats in road surveys in Everglades National Park, with marsh rabbits and foxes effectively disappearing from surveys; the lesson rounds to "about 90 percent or more." Male panther home ranges average roughly 200 square miles. The 10 percent figure is a commonly used average (Lindeman, 1942); real ecological efficiencies vary. Fish consumption advisories: Florida Department of Health.

Sources

  • Florida Fish and Wildlife Conservation Commission, Florida Panther and Burmese Python pages.
  • Dorcas, M. E., et al. (2012), Severe mammal declines coincide with proliferation of invasive Burmese pythons in Everglades National Park, PNAS 109(7).
  • Florida Department of Health, Fish Consumption Advisories.

Answer key: interactive lesson

Reconnect

  1. Is sunlight a biotic or abiotic factor?
    Answer: abiotic
    Today: the energy source for almost every food web.
  2. All the populations living together in an area form a ___.
    Answer: community
    A food web maps the feeding links in a community.
  3. In Unit 2 you learned the process plants use to make sugar from sunlight, water and carbon dioxide. What is it called?
    Answer: photosynthesis
    It starts every food chain in this lesson.
  4. Last week you learned about limiting factors. How could food act as a limiting factor for alligators in the Everglades?
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: If prey (fish, turtles, birds) is scarce, alligators can't all get enough to eat, so their population can't grow
    Model answer: If there aren't enough fish, turtles and birds to eat, not all alligators can get enough food, so their population stops growing.

Learn 1: Producers, Consumers and Decomposers

  1. Classify each organism.
    Answer: producer; herbivore; omnivore; decomposer
    Mushrooms are fungi that break down dead matter.
  2. In Week 14 you predicted a world without decomposers. Now place them in the Everglades web: why would an arrow from every organism, including the alligator and panther, point to decomposers, and where does the energy in a dead alligator finally go?
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Every organism dies or produces waste, so all feed decomposers; Decomposers use the energy and most is lost as heat; nutrients return for producers
    Model answer: Dead plants and animals would pile up without rotting. The nutrients in them would stay locked away instead of returning to the soil and water, so producers couldn't grow well, and everything that depends on producers would suffer too.

Learn 2: Food Chains and Food Webs

  1. Put this Everglades food chain in order, starting with the producer.
    Answer: periphyton → apple snail → snail kite
    Energy flows from the producer to the herbivore to the carnivore.
  2. In a food chain diagram, the arrow points from the food toward the ___.
    Answer: the eater (the consumer)
    Arrows show the direction energy flows.
  3. Use the food web. Snail kites eat almost only apple snails. Why is the snail kite more at risk than the great egret if a drought dries up the marsh?
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Drought reduces apple snails; Snail kites depend on one food source; egrets eat many kinds of prey, so they can switch
    Model answer: A drought would kill off many apple snails. Since snail kites eat almost nothing else, they would starve. Great egrets eat many kinds of fish, crayfish and frogs, so they can switch to other food.

Learn 3: Energy Pyramids: The 10 Percent Rule

  1. Producers in a pond capture 50,000 units of energy. Using the 10% rule, about how many units reach the secondary consumers?
    Answer: 500 units
    50,000 → 5,000 (primary) → 500 (secondary).
  2. Explain, using the energy pyramid, why there are far fewer alligators than mosquitofish in the Everglades.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Only about 10% of energy passes to each higher level; Less energy at the top supports fewer organisms; alligators are several levels above mosquitofish
    Model answer: Only about 10 percent of the energy at each level moves up to the next. Alligators are higher up the pyramid than mosquitofish, so much less energy reaches them, and that smaller amount can only support a few alligators.

Learn 4: Changes in the Web and Biomagnification

  1. Burmese pythons eat marsh rabbits and raccoons. Use the food web to predict two ripple effects of pythons spreading through the Everglades.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Prey mammals decline; Two ripple effects (native predators like panthers lose food; plants or prey that mammals ate could increase or change; competition with alligators)
    Model answer: With fewer marsh rabbits and raccoons, Florida panthers would have less food and might struggle. Also, the things raccoons eat, like crayfish and turtle eggs, might increase because fewer raccoons are eating them.
  2. Why does mercury become more concentrated at higher levels of a food chain, while energy becomes less?
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Explains what happens to energy at each level; Explains why mercury builds up instead
    Model answer: Energy gets used up and lost as heat at each step, so less moves up. Mercury isn't used up; it stays stored in the body. Each predator eats many prey and keeps all of their mercury, so it piles up higher in the chain.

Hands-On

  1. Describe your yarn web. When one organism disappeared, which organisms lost connections, and what does that show about food webs?
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Describes the web and the removed organism; Names affected organisms correctly; Explains interdependence: one change affects many
    Model answer: The sun connected to sawgrass and algae, which connected to snails, rabbits and fish, then up to kites, egrets, alligators and the panther. When the apple snail dropped out, the snail kite lost its only connection, and the algae lost an eater. It showed that one missing species can affect many others.
  2. Record how much water was in each cup. How does the model show the 10 percent rule, and why would a food chain rarely have more than four or five levels?
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Amounts: 1,000, 100, 10, 1 mL (or proportional); Explains that energy shrinks quickly, so there isn't enough left to support more levels
    Model answer: Cup 1 had 1,000 mL, cup 2 had 100, cup 3 had 10 and cup 4 had just 1 mL, about a quarter teaspoon. The energy shrinks so fast that by the fifth level there would be almost nothing left, so there isn't enough energy to support many levels.

Think Deeper

  1. Some people say that eating lower on the food chain (more plants, less meat) uses less of the Earth's energy and land. Use the 10 percent rule to explain the reasoning behind this claim. Then name one thing the rule alone doesn't tell us about people's food choices.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Explains: about 90% of energy is lost at each level, so feeding animals plants to eat the animals uses more plant energy and land than eating plants directly; Names a limit (nutrition needs, culture and tradition, land not suited to crops, health, cost, personal choice); Even-handed tone
    Model answer: Each step up the food chain loses about 90 percent of the energy. So if cows eat 100 units of plant energy, people who eat the beef get only about 10 units. Eating plants directly gets much more of the original energy, so it can take less land to feed people. But the rule doesn't tell us everything: people need certain nutrients, some land can only grow grass for grazing, and food is tied to culture, health and family traditions.
  2. Florida advises people to limit how often they eat some large fish, like largemouth bass from certain lakes, and sets looser limits, or none, for small fish like sunfish. Explain why, using biomagnification.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Bass are higher in the food chain (they eat smaller fish); Mercury builds up at each level, so bass carry more mercury than small fish; Eating fish high in mercury too often could harm health
    Model answer: Largemouth bass are predators that eat many smaller fish, which ate even smaller organisms. Mercury builds up at every level, so a big bass can carry much more mercury than a small sunfish lower in the chain. Eating high-mercury fish too often could harm a person's health, so the advice is to eat them less.

Exit Check

  1. What do we call an organism that makes its own food?
    Answer: a producer
    Usually by photosynthesis.
  2. If producers have 20,000 units of energy, about how many reach primary consumers?
    Answer: 2000 units
    10% of 20,000.
  3. What is the buildup of a toxin at higher levels of a food chain called?
    Answer: biomagnification
    Like mercury in Everglades panthers.
  4. Why is a food web a better model of an ecosystem than a single food chain?
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Most organisms eat and are eaten by more than one thing; a web shows all those connections
    Model answer: Most animals eat several kinds of food and are eaten by several predators, so a web shows all those connections, while a chain shows only one.

Answer key: printable page

  1. Define producer, consumer and decomposer, with an Everglades example of each.
    Answer: Producer: makes food (sawgrass). Consumer: eats others (alligator). Decomposer: breaks down dead matter (fungi, bacteria).
  2. Write a four-step Everglades food chain with arrows.
    Answer: algae → mosquitofish → largemouth bass → alligator.
  3. Producers capture 30,000 units of energy. How much reaches the tertiary consumers?
    Answer: 30,000 → 3,000 → 300 → 30 units.
  4. Why do food chains rarely have more than four or five levels?
    Answer: Only about 10% of energy moves up each level, so too little remains to support more levels.
  5. Explain biomagnification using mercury and panthers.
    Answer: Mercury is stored, not used; each predator collects mercury from many prey; it builds up to high levels in panthers.
  6. Predict one effect of Burmese pythons on the Everglades food web.
    Answer: Fewer raccoons and marsh rabbits; panthers lose food; prey of those mammals may increase.
