Unit 5: Ecosystems · Week 25 · Life Science · Teacher Plan

Unit 5 Checkpoint and Ecosystem Study Project

Lesson length: 60 minutes live, plus the after-class interactive lesson

Objectives

  • Explain how biotic and abiotic factors, energy flow, matter cycles and interactions work together in one ecosystem.
  • Use evidence to predict how a change ripples through an ecosystem.
  • Interpret food webs, cycles and population graphs.
  • Complete a field study of a local ecosystem.
  • Demonstrate mastery of Unit 5 on the checkpoint (85 or higher).

Standards

  • SC.7.L.17.1: Explain and illustrate the roles of and relationships among producers, consumers, and decomposers in the process of energy transfer in a food web.
  • SC.7.L.17.2: Compare and contrast the relationships among organisms such as mutualism, predation, parasitism, competition, and commensalism.
  • SC.7.L.17.3: Describe and investigate various limiting factors in the local ecosystem and their impact on native populations, including food, shelter, water, space, disease, parasitism, predation, and nesting sites.
  • SC.8.L.18.3: Construct a scientific model of the carbon cycle to show how matter and energy are continuously transferred within and between organisms and their physical environment.
  • SC.6.E.7.2: Investigate and apply how the cycling of water between the atmosphere and hydrosphere has an effect on weather patterns and climate.
  • SC.8.L.18.4: Cite evidence that living systems follow the Laws of Conservation of Mass and Energy.
  • SC.6.N.1.1: Define a problem from the sixth grade curriculum, use appropriate reference materials to support scientific understanding, plan and carry out scientific investigation of various types, such as systematic observations or experiments, identify variables, collect and organize data, interpret data in charts, tables, and graphics, analyze information, make predictions, and defend conclusions.

Materials

  • The interactive lesson
  • A shoebox or box lid, index cards, scissors, a marker, yarn in 3 colors, tape
  • The student's field notes
  • The Unit 5 projects page and rubric
  • The Unit 5 checkpoint

Vocabulary

  • ecosystem: All the living and nonliving things in an area and how they interact.
  • energy pyramid: A model showing how energy decreases at each level of a food chain.
  • biomagnification: The buildup of a toxin at higher levels of a food chain.
  • symbiosis: A close, long-term relationship between two species.
  • field study: Scientific observations made in a real natural setting.

Key questions to ask

  • Is this energy or matter, and does it flow or cycle?
  • What limits this population?
  • Who benefits in this relationship?
  • If one part changes, what else changes?

Common mistakes to watch for

  • Energy is recycled in ecosystems.
  • Food web arrows point from eater to food.
  • Plants don't respire.
  • Predators wipe out their prey.

Support (if the student is struggling)

  • Keep the concept map visible during review (not during the checkpoint).
  • Use the shoebox model to answer ripple questions aloud first.
  • Provide a field data sheet with columns for date, time, weather, counts and notes.

Challenge (if the student is ready for more)

  • Compare your study site with a site in a different Florida ecosystem.
  • Graph your three visits' data and explain any differences.

Background for the teacher

Checkpoint totals: Part A 10, Part B 10, Part C 10, Part D 11, total 41; mastery is about 35 of 41, and open items are scored with the rubric after submission. Florida places most of these benchmarks in Grades 7 and 8; KCE teaches them here per the Grade 6 scope. Field visits should be supervised; avoid touching wildlife, stay back from water where alligators may be present, and check public land rules before collecting anything.

Answer key: interactive lesson

Reconnect

  1. Week 21: Sunlight is not alive. What term names a nonliving factor in an ecosystem?
    Answer: abiotic
    It is not alive.
  2. Week 22: If producers capture 30,000 units of energy, about how many reach the primary consumers?
    Answer: 3000 units
    About 10 percent.
  3. Week 23: Which process releases carbon dioxide every time a living thing breaks down sugar for energy?
    Answer: cellular respiration
    Plants do it too.
  4. Week 24: A tick feeding on a deer is an example of which type of symbiosis?
    Answer: parasitism
    The tick benefits; the deer is harmed.

Learn 1: Review: Parts and Energy

  1. In a food chain of sawgrass → marsh rabbit → panther, the sawgrass captures 50,000 units of energy. About how many reach the panther?
    Answer: 500 units
    50,000 → 5,000 → 500.
  2. Explain how one abiotic factor decides which organisms can live in a Florida spring but not in the Gulf of Mexico.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Names a specific abiotic factor; Explains how it affects which organisms can live there
    Model answer: Salt is the key abiotic factor. Spring water is fresh, so freshwater plants like eelgrass and fish like bass live there, while ocean fish that need salt water can't survive.

Learn 2: Review: Cycles and Interactions

  1. A manatee eats eelgrass. Explain what happens to the energy and what happens to the carbon in the eelgrass.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Explains what happens to the energy; Explains where the carbon goes and how it returns; Contrasts flowing energy with cycling matter
    Model answer: Most of the energy in the eelgrass is used by the manatee to live and is lost as heat; it doesn't come back. The carbon becomes part of the manatee's body and returns to the water and air when the manatee breathes out or when it dies and decomposes, so the carbon can be used again.
  2. Give one Florida example each of mutualism, commensalism and parasitism from this unit, and explain who benefits in each.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: A correct example of each type; Explains who benefits, who is harmed and who is unaffected
    Model answer: Mutualism: the neon goby cleans a grouper; both benefit. Commensalism: Spanish moss on a live oak; the moss benefits and the oak is unaffected. Parasitism: a tick on a deer; the tick benefits and the deer is harmed.

Learn 3: Finishing the Ecosystem Study Project

  1. Name the place you are studying and describe how you will collect data on three visits: what you will measure, with what tools, and how you'll keep conditions fair.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Names a real study site; Specific measurements and tools; Explains how visits will be comparable
    Model answer: I'm studying the retention pond behind our church. On three Saturday mornings at 9 a.m., I'll measure air temperature with a thermometer, count birds and turtles for 15 minutes, and survey a 1-meter quadrat in the grass well back from the water. Going at the same time and spot each visit keeps it fair.

Hands-On

  1. Describe the change you tested and the ripple effects you predicted for at least three other parts of your ecosystem. Which connection surprised you?
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Names the change tested; At least three ripple effects traced through the model; Reflects on a surprising connection
    Model answer: We tested a fertilizer spill into our pond. Algae would grow quickly and block sunlight from underwater plants; when the algae died, decomposers would use up oxygen, so fish could die; then herons and turtles would lose food. The surprise was that more food for algae could end up meaning less food for everything else.

Think Deeper

  1. Write a short explanation for a younger child of why "everything in an ecosystem is connected." Use at least one example from each week of the unit (parts, energy, matter and interactions).
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Explains connection in kid-friendly language; An accurate example from each of the four weeks; Ties the examples together
    Model answer: In nature, everything needs something else. Plants need sunlight and water, which are nonliving parts. Rabbits eat the plants to get energy, and panthers eat the rabbits, but most energy gets used up along the way, so there are only a few panthers. When animals breathe and when they die, their carbon goes back into the air and soil for new plants. And some animals help each other, like little fish that clean big fish. If one piece disappears, the others feel it.

Checkpoint: Part A: Parts of an ecosystem

  1. A Florida pine forest has longleaf pines, sandy soil, gopher tortoises, wiregrass and summer lightning. Name the two abiotic factors.
    Answer: sandy soil and lightning
    Soil and lightning are not alive.
  2. Put the levels of ecology in order from smallest to largest.
    Answer: organism → population → community → ecosystem → biosphere
    Each level includes the one before it.
  3. A pond's frog population grows for three years and then levels off. Explain why, using the terms limiting factor and carrying capacity.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric (4 points toward the checkpoint).
    Rubric: Uses limiting factor correctly with an example; Uses carrying capacity correctly; Explains the leveling off
    Model answer: The population levels off because it reached the pond's carrying capacity, the largest number of frogs the pond can support. Limiting factors such as the number of insects to eat and places to lay eggs keep the population from growing any larger.

Checkpoint: Part B: Energy in food webs

  1. In a food web, what do the arrows show?
    Answer: the direction energy flows (from food to eater)
    Arrows point from the food to the organism that eats it.
  2. Algae capture 80,000 units of energy. About how many reach a secondary consumer?
    Answer: 800 units
    80,000 → 8,000 → 800.
  3. Explain why a largemouth bass from a polluted lake usually has much more mercury in its body than the small fish it eats.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric (4 points toward the checkpoint).
    Rubric: Mercury is stored in the body, not used up; The bass eats many small fish, so mercury builds up at each level (biomagnification)
    Model answer: Mercury isn't used up or lost; it is stored in the body. A bass eats many small fish over its life, collecting the mercury from each one, so the amount builds up at each level of the food chain. This is biomagnification.

Checkpoint: Part C: Cycles of matter

  1. Put these steps of the water cycle in order, starting with water in a lake.
    Answer: evaporation → condensation → precipitation → infiltration into the aquifer
    Then groundwater can flow out at a spring.
  2. Which process do producers use to remove carbon dioxide from the air?
    Answer: photosynthesis
    They make sugar from carbon dioxide and water.
  3. Explain why burning fossil fuels increases the carbon dioxide in the air even though that carbon was once part of living things.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric (4 points toward the checkpoint).
    Rubric: The carbon was stored underground for millions of years, outside the active cycle; Burning releases it quickly, faster than plants and oceans can absorb it
    Model answer: The carbon in coal, oil and gas was locked underground for millions of years, out of the active cycle. Burning releases all that stored carbon as carbon dioxide very quickly, faster than plants and the ocean can take it back in, so the amount in the air rises.

Checkpoint: Part D: Interactions

  1. Classify each relationship.
    Answer: mutualism; commensalism; competition
    +/+; +/0; −/−.
  2. In a forest, hare numbers peak in 2010 and lynx numbers peak in 2012. Explain why the lynx peak comes after the hare peak, and predict what happens to the hares after 2012.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric (4 points toward the checkpoint).
    Rubric: Explains the time lag (more food lets lynx reproduce; kittens take time to grow); Predicts the hares decline after 2012 because there are many predators
    Model answer: With lots of hares, lynx have plenty of food and raise more kittens, but it takes time for the lynx population to grow, so it peaks two years later. After 2012 the many lynx eat so many hares that the hare population falls, and later the lynx will decline too.
  3. Burmese pythons have spread through the Everglades. Use two relationship types and the idea of energy flow to predict two effects on the ecosystem.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric (4 points toward the checkpoint).
    Rubric: Uses two relationship types correctly; Uses energy flow correctly; Two reasonable predicted effects
    Model answer: Pythons are predators of raccoons and marsh rabbits, so those populations drop. They also compete with native predators like panthers and alligators for the same prey, so those predators have less energy flowing to them and may decline. Plants and animals the mammals once ate could increase.

Answer key: printable page

  1. Name two biotic and two abiotic factors in a Florida ecosystem.
    Answer: Biotic: cypress tree, alligator; abiotic: water, sunlight.
  2. Explain the 10 percent rule.
    Answer: Only about 10 percent of energy passes to the next level; the rest is used or lost as heat.
  3. Describe how water moves from the Gulf to a Florida spring.
    Answer: Evaporation, condensation, precipitation over land, infiltration into the aquifer, flow out at the spring.
  4. Name the five types of relationships and give an example of each.
    Answer: Predation: a panther eats a deer; competition: two anole species want the same perch; mutualism: bees pollinate orange blossoms; commensalism: cattle egrets eat insects stirred up by cows; parasitism: a tick feeds on a deer.
  5. Predict one ripple effect if decomposers disappeared from a pond.
    Answer: Dead matter piles up and nutrients stay locked away, so producers and the whole web suffer.
