Unit 5: Novel Study · Week 25 · ELA · Teacher Plan

Unit 5 Checkpoint and Literary Response Project

Lesson length: 60 minutes live, plus the after-class interactive lesson

Objectives

  • Explain how setting, conflict and character change work together across a whole novel.
  • Trace a theme from beginning to end using symbols, foils and craft choices as evidence.
  • Analyze an unfamiliar passage from the novel for character, tone and craft.
  • Write a literary analysis with a clear claim, accurate quotations and explanation.
  • Demonstrate mastery of Unit 5 on the checkpoint (85 or higher).

Standards

  • ELA.6.R.1.1: Analyze how the interaction between characters contributes to the development of a plot in a literary text.
  • ELA.6.R.1.2: Analyze the development of stated or implied theme(s) throughout a literary text.
  • ELA.6.R.1.3: Explain the influence of multiple narrators and/or shifts in point of view in a literary text.
  • ELA.6.R.3.1: Explain how figurative language contributes to tone and meaning in text(s).
  • ELA.6.C.1.4: Write expository texts to explain and/or analyze information from multiple sources, using a logical organizational structure, relevant elaboration, and varied transitions.
  • ELA.K12.EE.1.1: Cite evidence to explain and justify reasoning.

Materials

  • The interactive lesson
  • The Call of the Wild (the whole novel)
  • 8 to 10 sheets of paper or sticky notes, markers, painter's tape
  • The student's project draft
  • The Unit 5 projects page and rubric
  • The Unit 5 checkpoint

Vocabulary

  • literary analysis: Writing that makes a claim about how a text works and supports it with evidence.
  • character arc: The change a character goes through from the beginning of a story to the end.
  • thematic statement: A complete sentence stating a theme, without naming characters.
  • textual evidence: Quotations or specific details from a text that support a claim.
  • commentary: Your explanation of how evidence supports your claim.

Key questions to ask

  • Is this a claim or a summary?
  • Does your commentary explain how, not just what?
  • Is the quotation exact?
  • What does the novel get right, and what does it leave out?

Common mistakes to watch for

  • Literary analysis means retelling the plot.
  • One quotation proves a claim by itself, without commentary.
  • Criticizing a book means you didn't like it.
  • A theme can be one word.

Support (if the student is struggling)

  • Provide a claim-evidence-commentary paragraph frame.
  • Let the student mark passage evidence with colored pencils before writing.
  • Read the passage aloud together before Part A (reading support does not change the scoring).

Challenge (if the student is ready for more)

  • Compare The Call of the Wild with London's White Fang (1906), which reverses Buck's journey.
  • Write a short literary analysis of London's ending from the point of view of a Tr'ondëk Hwëch'in reader.

How the lesson runs

During the live lesson, the student shares their screen and answers the Reconnect questions and the Learn checks live while you teach. The Hands-On activity, Think Deeper and Exit Check are completed after class.

Scoring

The interactive lesson scores itself: full points on the first try, half points on the second try, then the answer and explanation appear. Mastery is 85. Written responses arrive in your results email with the score; score them with the rubric below before the student moves on.

Checkpoint scoring

Checkpoint questions are scored separately from the lesson. The student gets one try per question and the answers are not shown, so the checkpoint measures independent mastery and a retake stays fair. Your results email shows the auto-scored checkpoint points, a list of each item marked correct or MISSED, and the points available for the written items. Score the written items with their rubrics, then: checkpoint score = (auto points + rubric points) / total possible × 100. Mastery is 85. Below 85: teach a targeted review lesson on the parts missed (A to D), then the student retakes the checkpoint. Record both attempts in the Weekly Score Log.

Answer key: interactive lesson

Reconnect

  1. Week 21: What event in the Yukon in 1896 created the demand for strong sled dogs?
    Answer: the Klondike Gold Rush (the gold strike)
    Men needed dogs to haul supplies north.
  2. Week 22: The title of Chapter II names a law of the North. Complete it: the law of club and ___.
    Answer: fang
    Force rules in the North.
  3. Week 23: A symbol that keeps returning through a novel is called a ___.
    Answer: motif
    Like the club or the call.
  4. Week 24: An author's word choice is called ___.
    Answer: diction
    Diction creates tone.

Learn 1: Review: Setting, Conflict and Character Change

  1. Put Buck's character arc in order.
    Answer: pampered pet at Judge Miller's → beaten into obedience by the man in the red sweater → lead dog after defeating Spitz → devoted companion to John Thornton → leader of the wolf pack
    Each conflict pushes him to the next stage.
  2. Choose one conflict from the novel and explain how the Klondike setting created it. Name the conflict type.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Names a specific conflict and its type; Explains how the setting caused or shaped it
    Model answer: Buck's struggle to sleep in the snow is character versus nature. In California he slept warm in the Judge's house, but the Yukon winter forced him to learn to dig a hole in the snow like the other dogs, or freeze.

Learn 2: Review: Symbols, Theme and Craft

  1. Pick one symbol or motif and trace how its meaning changes from an early chapter to a late chapter. What theme does that change support?
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Names a symbol and its early meaning, with a chapter; Names its later meaning, with a chapter; Connects the change to a theme stated as a sentence
    Model answer: The call starts as a faint pull Buck can't name and grows louder until, after Thornton's death, Buck answers it in Chapter VII. That change supports the theme that the wild part of a creature can be buried by comfort but never erased.
  2. Name one craft choice London makes (diction, narrator, foreshadowing or structure) and explain how it affects you as a reader.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Names a specific craft choice with an example; Explains its effect on the reader
    Model answer: London's foreshadowing with the call made me nervous every time Buck wandered into the woods. I knew that only Thornton was holding him back, so when Thornton died I already felt where the story was going.

Learn 3: Finishing the Literary Response Project

  1. This body paragraph is weak: "Buck changes a lot. In Chapter III he fights Spitz. Then he becomes lead dog. This shows he changes." Rewrite it with a real claim, one piece of evidence and commentary.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: A claim about how or why Buck changes, not a summary; Evidence introduced with context; Commentary that explains how the evidence supports the claim
    Model answer: Buck's victory over Spitz shows that the North rewards cunning more than strength. London calls Buck "the dominant primordial beast who had made his kill." Spitz is the stronger fighter, but Buck wins by faking one attack and striking another. London's word primordial shows that this cunning comes from deep, ancient instincts, which is why Buck can rise to lead dog in a world where the old rules no longer work.

Hands-On

  1. Write your claim and your strongest piece of evidence. What "So what?" question did your visitor ask, and how did you revise your commentary to answer it?
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Claim and evidence written out accurately; The visitor's question; A specific revision to the commentary
    Model answer: Claim: London uses foils to show that knowledge of nature matters more than wealth. Evidence: Hal, Charles and Mercedes "did not know how to do anything." My visitor asked, "So what if they didn't know things?" I revised my commentary to explain that their ignorance kills the dogs and finally themselves, while François and Perrault, who are not rich, survive because they know the trail.

Think Deeper

  1. Look back at the whole novel study. Which reading tool from this unit (setting, conflict, symbols, theme, craft or reading as a critic) changed how you understood The Call of the Wild the most? Explain with an example, and name one tool you want to use on your next book.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Names a tool and explains how it changed your understanding; A specific example from the novel; Names a tool for the next book, with a reason
    Model answer: Reading as a critic changed my understanding most. At first I just cheered for Buck in Chapter VII, but after comparing the Yeehats with the real Tr'ondëk Hwëch'in, I noticed London never gave them names or reasons. On my next book I want to use symbols, because tracking the call showed me how one image can carry a whole theme.

Checkpoint: Part A: Analyze a passage

  1. From Chapter VI of The Call of the Wild (1903). Thornton has bet that Buck can start a sled loaded with a thousand pounds of flour and pull it a hundred yards. Thornton knelt down by Buck's side. He took his head in his two hands and rested cheek on cheek. He did not playfully shake him, as was his wont, or murmur soft love curses; but he whispered in his ear. "As you love me, Buck. As you love me," was what he whispered. Buck whined with suppressed eagerness. The crowd was watching curiously. The affair was growing mysterious. It seemed like a conjuration. As Thornton got to his feet, Buck seized his mittened hand between his jaws, pressing in with his teeth and releasing slowly, half-reluctantly. It was the answer, in terms, not of speech, but of love. Thornton stepped well back. "Now, Buck," he said. Buck tightened the traces, then slacked them for a matter of several inches. It was the way he had learned. "Gee!" Thornton's voice rang out, sharp in the tense silence. Buck swung to the right, ending the movement in a plunge that took up the slack and with a sudden jerk arrested his one hundred and fifty pounds. The load quivered, and from under the runners arose a crisp crackling. "Haw!" Thornton commanded. Buck duplicated the manœuvre, this time to the left. The crackling turned into a snapping, the sled pivoting and the runners slipping and grating several inches to the side. The sled was broken out. Men were holding their breaths, intensely unconscious of the fact. wont: habit. conjuration: a magic spell. traces: harness straps. Gee and Haw: commands to turn right and left. arrested: stopped. manœuvre: the 1903 spelling of maneuver. broken out: freed from the ice frozen around the runners. What does this passage reveal about the relationship between Buck and Thornton? Support your answer with two quotations from the passage.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric (5 points toward the checkpoint).
    Rubric: A clear statement about the relationship; Two accurate quotations from the passage; Explanation of how each quotation shows the relationship
    Model answer: The passage shows that Buck works for Thornton out of love, not fear. Thornton whispers, "As you love me, Buck," instead of commanding him, and Buck answers by gently holding Thornton's hand in his jaws, which London calls "the answer, in terms, not of speech, but of love." Unlike the man with the club, Thornton gets Buck's best effort through trust.
  2. Reread the last two paragraphs of the passage. How does London's diction create suspense? Name two words or phrases and explain their effect.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric (4 points toward the checkpoint).
    Rubric: Two specific words or phrases from the passage; Explains how each builds tension or suspense
    Model answer: London uses sounds that grow louder: a "crisp crackling" turns into "a snapping," so readers hear the ice breaking little by little. Then "Men were holding their breaths" makes the reader hold their breath too, waiting to see if Buck can move the sled.
  3. Compare this scene with the scene in Chapter I where Buck faces the man in the red sweater. How has the source of Buck's obedience changed, and what does that change show about the novel's themes?
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric (5 points toward the checkpoint).
    Rubric: Describes the Chapter I source of obedience; Describes the Chapter VI source of obedience; Connects the change to a theme
    Model answer: In Chapter I, Buck obeys because the club beats him into it; the man rules by force. In Chapter VI, Buck strains with all his strength because he loves Thornton. The change shows a theme of the novel: force can make a creature obey, but only love and respect earn its full loyalty.
  4. In the passage, Buck "tightened the traces, then slacked them for a matter of several inches. It was the way he had learned." Which conflict type from Week 22 is Buck overcoming in the whole scene: the frozen load is character versus ___.
    Answer: nature
    The ice frozen around the runners is part of nature.

Checkpoint: Part B: The whole novel

  1. Explain how the historical setting of the Klondike Gold Rush makes Buck's story possible. Include one fact about the real history you learned in Week 21.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric (4 points toward the checkpoint).
    Rubric: Explains the connection between the gold rush and Buck's kidnapping or journey; One accurate historical fact from Week 21
    Model answer: The gold rush created a huge demand for strong dogs to haul supplies, which is why Manuel steals Buck and sells him north. In real history, the strike was made in 1896 on a creek off the Klondike River, and the Klondike was the homeland of the Tr'ondëk Hwëch'in.
  2. Choose one pair of foils in the novel. Explain what the contrast reveals about a theme, and state that theme as a complete sentence.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric (4 points toward the checkpoint).
    Rubric: Names a valid pair of foils with a specific difference; A thematic statement as a full sentence that names no characters; Explains how the contrast reveals it
    Model answer: Thornton and Hal are foils. Thornton treats his dogs "as if they were his own children," while Hal beats Buck when he refuses to move. The contrast reveals the theme that kindness earns loyalty, while cruelty only destroys.
  3. Explain how the point of view shifts at the end of the novel and why that shift fits Buck's final change.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric (4 points toward the checkpoint).
    Rubric: Describes the shift from inside Buck to a legend told from outside; Explains why it fits his change into a wild animal
    Model answer: Most of the novel stays close to Buck's thoughts, but at the end the narrator steps back and tells Buck's story as a legend the Yeehats tell about a Ghost Dog. The shift fits because Buck has left the human world; we can no longer see inside his mind, just as people can't understand a wild wolf.
  4. A friend says, "The Call of the Wild is a great adventure, so there's nothing to criticize." Respond with one strength of the novel's craft and one problem a critical reader should notice, using specific examples.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric (5 points toward the checkpoint).
    Rubric: One specific strength of craft, with an example; One specific problem, with an example; A balanced response to the friend
    Model answer: It is a great adventure: London's diction, like calling Buck "a live hurricane of fury," makes every scene feel alive. But a critical reader should notice that the Yeehats have no names, motives or point of view and are described only as attackers, a stereotype that hides the real First Nations of the Klondike. A book can be exciting and still deserve criticism.

Checkpoint: Part C: Write an analysis paragraph

  1. Write one analysis paragraph (claim, evidence, commentary) answering this question: Is Buck's ending a triumph, a loss or both? Use at least one quotation from Chapter VII and one from an earlier chapter.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric (8 points toward the checkpoint).
    Rubric: A clear, arguable claim that answers the question; At least two accurate quotations, one from Chapter VII and one earlier; Commentary that explains how each quotation supports the claim; Organized, with a concluding sentence
    Model answer: Buck's ending is both a triumph and a loss. In Chapter VII he finally answers the call and runs "at the head of the pack," free and powerful, a triumph over every man who tried to rule him with a club. Yet the ending also costs him the only love he ever knew. In Chapter VI London says that "Love, genuine passionate love, was his for the first time," and that love dies with Thornton. Buck becomes a legend, but he returns each summer to Thornton's valley to howl "long and mournfully." London shows that freedom and loss can arrive together: Buck wins the wild only after losing the one bond that held him to people.

Answer key: printable page

  1. List Buck's five stages of change in order.
    Answer: Pampered pet; beaten into obedience; lead dog; devoted to Thornton; leader of the wolf pack.
  2. Name the four types of conflict and give one example of each from the novel.
    Answer: Versus nature (snow, ice); versus character (Spitz); versus society (law of club and fang); versus self (civilized versus wild).
  3. Choose one symbol and explain what it stands for.
    Answer: The club: force and human power; the fire: ancestry; the call: the wild.
  4. Write a thematic statement for the novel and give two pieces of evidence.
    Answer: Model: Comfort can bury the wild in a creature, but cannot erase it; Buck's dreams by the fire; Buck answering the call.
  5. Explain the parts of an analysis paragraph.
    Answer: Claim (a point about how the text works), evidence (an exact quotation with context), commentary (explanation of how the evidence proves the claim).
