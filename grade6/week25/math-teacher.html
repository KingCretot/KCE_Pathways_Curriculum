Unit 5: Expressions and Equations · Week 25 · Math · Teacher Plan

Unit 5 Review and Checkpoint

Lesson length: 60 minutes live, plus the after-class interactive lesson

Objectives

  • Choose the right algebra tool for a problem: evaluate, simplify, solve or compare.
  • Use exponents and the order of operations, including simple interest.
  • Write and evaluate expressions and cost formulas.
  • Solve one-step equations and test values in inequalities, including budget problems.
  • Demonstrate mastery of Unit 5 on the checkpoint (85 or higher).

Standards

  • MA.6.NSO.3.3: Evaluate positive rational numbers and integers with natural number exponents.
  • MA.6.AR.1.3: Evaluate algebraic expressions using substitution and order of operations.
  • MA.6.AR.1.4: Apply the properties of operations to generate equivalent algebraic expressions with integer coefficients.
  • MA.6.AR.1.1: Given a mathematical or real-world context, translate written descriptions into algebraic expressions and translate algebraic expressions into written descriptions.
  • MA.6.AR.2.1: Given an equation or inequality and a specified set of integer values, determine which values make the equation or inequality true or false.
  • MA.6.AR.2.2: Write and solve one-step equations in one variable within a mathematical or real-world context using addition and subtraction, where all terms and solutions are integers.
  • MA.6.AR.2.3: Write and solve one-step equations in one variable within a mathematical or real-world context using multiplication and division, where all terms and solutions are integers.
  • MA.6.AR.2.4: Determine the unknown decimal or fraction in an equation involving any of the four operations, relating three numbers, with the unknown in any position.
  • Financial literacy: budgeting and simple interest (Jump$tart Spending and Saving; Credit and Borrowing).

Materials

  • The interactive lesson
  • 4 station sheets, index cards, a marker, a timer, optional painter's tape
  • Printable practice page
  • The Unit 5 checkpoint

Vocabulary

  • exponent: Tells how many times the base is used as a factor.
  • expression: Numbers, variables and operations with no equal sign.
  • equation: Two expressions set equal; a solution makes it true.
  • inequality: Two expressions compared with , ≤ or ≥.
  • inverse operations: Operations that undo each other.
  • simple interest: Interest earned only on the principal: I = P × r × t.

Key questions to ask

  • Am I evaluating, simplifying, solving or comparing?
  • What operation undoes this one?
  • Is the boundary included?
  • Does my answer make sense with whole items?

Common mistakes to watch for

  • 2³ means 2 × 3.
  • Solving means doing the same operation that appears.
  • Inequalities have one answer.
  • Budget answers are always rounded to the nearest whole number.

Support (if the student is struggling)

  • Provide the toolkit chart during review (not during the checkpoint).
  • Use the balance model for equations.
  • Let the student explain a problem aloud before writing.

Challenge (if the student is ready for more)

  • Plan a full fair budget with five cost lines and a booth fee that raises at least $50 profit.
  • Solve 4n + 2.50 = 14.50 two different ways.

How the lesson runs

During the live lesson, the student shares their screen and answers the Reconnect questions and the Learn checks live while you teach. The Hands-On activity, Think Deeper and Exit Check are completed after class.

Scoring

The interactive lesson scores itself: full points on the first try, half points on the second try, then the answer and explanation appear. Mastery is 85. Written responses arrive in your results email with the score; score them with the rubric below before the student moves on.

Checkpoint scoring

Checkpoint questions are scored separately from the lesson. The student gets one try per question and the answers are not shown, so the checkpoint measures independent mastery and a retake stays fair. Your results email shows the auto-scored checkpoint points, a list of each item marked correct or MISSED, and the points available for the written items. Score the written items with their rubrics, then: checkpoint score = (auto points + rubric points) / total possible × 100. Mastery is 85. Below 85: teach a targeted review lesson on the parts missed (A to D), then the student retakes the checkpoint. Record both attempts in the Weekly Score Log.

Answer key: interactive lesson

Reconnect

  1. Week 21: 18 − 2³ × 2 = ?
    Answer: 2
    2³ = 8; 8 × 2 = 16; 18 − 16 = 2.
  2. Week 22: Evaluate 4m + 3n when m = 5 and n = 2.
    Answer: 26
    20 + 6 = 26.
  3. Week 23: Solve: x/4 = 9
    Answer: 36
    Multiply both sides by 4.
  4. Week 24: Write an inequality for "no more than 30 people." Use p. Write the symbol in words (for example, "x is less than 5").
    Answer: p ≤ 30 (p is less than or equal to 30)
    No more than means 30 or fewer.

Learn 1: Review: Exponents, Order of Operations and Interest

  1. Evaluate: (7 − 3)² + 12 ÷ 4
    Answer: 19
    4² = 16; 12 ÷ 4 = 3; 16 + 3 = 19.
  2. The co-op puts $600 in a savings account earning 3% simple interest. How much interest does it earn in 2 years?
    Answer: 36 dollars
    600 × 0.03 × 2 = 36.

Learn 2: Review: Expressions and Cost Formulas

  1. Write a cost formula: face painting kits cost $6 each, plus $10 shipping. Use k.
    Answer: 6k + 10
    $6 per kit plus a $10 fixed fee.
  2. Simplify: 4(2x + 3) − 5x
    Answer: 3x + 12
    8x + 12 − 5x = 3x + 12.

Learn 3: Review: Equations and Inequalities

  1. Twelve identical prizes cost $54. What is the price of one prize?
    Answer: 4.5 dollars
    12p = 54, so p = $4.50.
  2. Explain when you would write an equation and when you would write an inequality for a fair-planning problem. Give one example of each.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Explains when to use an equation; Explains when to use an inequality; One fair example of each
    Model answer: Use an equation when the amount is exact, such as finding the price of one prize when 12 cost $54: 12p = 54. Use an inequality for a limit or goal, such as buying balloons at $0.40 each with at most $20: 0.40b ≤ 20.

Learn 4: Money: Planning the Fair Budget

  1. Using the $300 budget, how much money is left after the pavilion and signs?
    Answer: 185 dollars
    300 − 75 − 40 = 185.
  2. If 18 booths sign up instead of 15, what is the lowest whole-dollar booth fee that raises at least $300?
    Answer: 17 dollars
    18f ≥ 300, f ≥ 16.67, so $17.
  3. The team wants to add a $45 bounce house. Use an inequality to decide how many craft kits they could still buy within the $300 budget, and give your advice on whether to add it.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: A correct inequality including the bounce house; Correct greatest number of kits; A recommendation with a reason
    Model answer: 75 + 40 + 45 + 3.50k ≤ 300, so 3.50k ≤ 140 and k ≤ 40. They could still buy 40 craft kits. I'd add the bounce house if 40 kits is enough for the expected crowd, since it draws families to the fair.

Hands-On

  1. Which station was easiest and which was hardest for you? Show one problem from your hardest station, solved and checked, and explain what you'll review before the checkpoint.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Names the easiest and hardest stations; One worked and checked problem from the hardest station; A specific review plan
    Model answer: Station 2 was easiest. Station 4 was hardest, especially deciding when to round up. Problem: save at least $95 at $12 a week: 12w ≥ 95, w ≥ 7.92, so 8 weeks; check: 12 × 8 = 96, which is at least 95. Before the checkpoint I'll review the rounding rule for goals and limits.

Think Deeper

  1. Unit 5 moved from evaluating expressions to solving equations to working with inequalities. Explain how these three ideas are connected, using one example that uses all three.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Explains how expressions are the building blocks of equations and inequalities; Explains how solving and testing are related; One example that uses all three ideas
    Model answer: An expression like 5n + 20 can describe the cost of n tickets plus parking. Setting it equal to a total, 5n + 20 = 60, makes an equation with one solution, n = 8. Comparing it to a limit, 5n + 20 ≤ 60, makes an inequality with many solutions, 8 tickets or fewer. All three use the same expression; the equal sign or inequality symbol decides what kind of answer you get.

Checkpoint: Part A: Exponents, order of operations and interest

  1. Evaluate: 6² − 4 × 5
    Answer: 16
    36 − 20 = 16.
  2. Evaluate: 3 × (2 + 4)² ÷ 9
    Answer: 12
    6² = 36; 3 × 36 = 108; 108 ÷ 9 = 12.
  3. Write 7 × 7 × 7 × 7 × 7 in exponent form.
    Answer: 7; 5
    Seven used as a factor five times.
  4. $1,200 is borrowed at 6% simple interest for 3 years. How much interest is owed?
    Answer: 216 dollars
    1,200 × 0.06 × 3 = 216.

Checkpoint: Part B: Expressions

  1. Write an expression for "8 less than three times a number n."
    Answer: 3n − 8
    Three times n, then subtract 8.
  2. Evaluate 2x² + 3y when x = 4 and y = 5.
    Answer: 47
    2(16) + 15 = 47.
  3. Simplify: 5(y + 2) + 3y − 4
    Answer: 8y + 6
    5y + 10 + 3y − 4 = 8y + 6.
  4. A bike shop charges $15 per hour plus a $12 helmet fee to rent a bike. Write a cost formula, find the cost for 4 hours, and explain what each part of the formula means.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric (4 points toward the checkpoint).
    Rubric: Correct formula 15h + 12; Correct cost $72; Explains the rate and the fixed fee
    Model answer: C = 15h + 12. For 4 hours: 15(4) + 12 = 60 + 12 = $72. The 15 is the hourly rate, which changes with time, and the 12 is the fixed helmet fee, paid once.

Checkpoint: Part C: One-step equations

  1. Solve: x + 27 = 63
    Answer: 36
    Subtract 27.
  2. Solve: 9m = 108
    Answer: 12
    Divide by 9.
  3. Solve: (2/3)k = 16
    Answer: 24
    Multiply by 3/2.
  4. A receipt shows 5 identical notebooks for a total of $13.50. Write an equation for the price of one notebook, solve it, and check it.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric (4 points toward the checkpoint).
    Rubric: Correct equation; Correct price $2.70; A correct check
    Model answer: 5n = 13.50. Divide both sides by 5: n = $2.70. Check: 5 × 2.70 = 13.50.

Checkpoint: Part D: Inequalities

  1. Write an inequality for "A ride's minimum height is 42 inches." Use h. Write the symbol in words (for example, "x is less than 5").
    Answer: h ≥ 42 (h is greater than or equal to 42)
    At least means 42 or more.
  2. Test x = 3, 4, 5 and 6 in 7x − 5 > 23. Which is the smallest of these that makes it true?
    Answer: 5
    7(4) − 5 = 23, not greater than 23; 7(5) − 5 = 30.
  3. A number line has an open circle at −2 and is shaded to the left. Write the inequality for variable n, with the symbol in words (for example, "x is less than 5").
    Answer: n < −2 (n is less than −2)
    Open circle: −2 not included; shaded left: smaller numbers.
  4. You have $36 to spend on jars of jam for a gift basket. Each jar costs $4.75. Write an inequality, solve it, and explain the greatest number of jars you can buy.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric (4 points toward the checkpoint).
    Rubric: Correct inequality; Correct solving steps; Correct answer with rounding explained
    Model answer: 4.75j ≤ 36, so j ≤ 7.58. I can buy at most 7 jars, rounding down because 8 jars would go over the $36 limit.

Answer key: printable page

  1. Evaluate: (a) 4³ (b) 50 − 3² × 4 (c) (10 − 6)² ÷ 2
    Answer: (a) 64 (b) 14 (c) 8
  2. How much simple interest does $900 earn at 4% for 5 years?
    Answer: 900 × 0.04 × 5 = $180.
  3. Write a cost formula: $7 per pizza plus a $4 delivery fee. Find the cost of 6 pizzas.
    Answer: 7p + 4; 7(6) + 4 = $46.
  4. Solve and check: (a) y − 4.6 = 9.4 (b) 15w = 165
    Answer: (a) 14 (b) 11
  5. Write and graph: you must be at least 16 to drive.
    Answer: a ≥ 16; closed circle at 16, shaded right.
  6. You have $60. Movie tickets cost $11.50 each. What is the greatest number you can buy?
    Answer: 11.50t ≤ 60; t ≤ 5.2; 5 tickets.
