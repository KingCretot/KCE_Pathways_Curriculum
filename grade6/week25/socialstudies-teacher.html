Unit 5: Empires and Global Trade · Week 25 · World and American History · Teacher Plan

Unit 5 Checkpoint and Trade Route Project

Lesson length: 60 minutes live, plus the after-class interactive lesson

Objectives

  • Compare the trans-Saharan and Indian Ocean trade networks.
  • Explain how geography and trade shaped Ghana, Mali, Songhai, the Swahili Coast and Great Zimbabwe.
  • Analyze primary sources and archaeology about African history.
  • Explain how historians corrected myths that denied African achievement.
  • Demonstrate mastery of Unit 5 on the checkpoint (85 or higher).

Standards

  • SS.6.G.2.1: Explain how major physical characteristics, natural resources, climate, and absolute and relative locations have influenced settlement, interactions, and the economies of ancient civilizations of the world.
  • SS.6.G.2.6: Explain the concept of cultural diffusion, and identify the influences of different ancient cultures on one another.
  • SS.6.E.3.4: Describe the relationship among civilizations that engage in trade, including the benefits and drawbacks of voluntary trade.
  • SS.6.W.1.3: Interpret primary and secondary sources.
  • SS.6.W.1.5: Describe the roles of historians and recognize varying historical interpretations (historiography).

Materials

  • The interactive lesson
  • The student's trade route game or project
  • Index cards, a marker, a die, small items as goods
  • The Unit 5 projects page and rubric
  • The Unit 5 checkpoint

Vocabulary

  • trade network: A system of routes, cities and merchants that moves goods over long distances.
  • primary source: A source created by someone who was there at the time, such as a traveler's account.
  • archaeology: The study of the past through objects, buildings and other remains.
  • cultural diffusion: The spread of ideas, beliefs and customs from one group to another.

Key questions to ask

  • How did geography shape this empire or city?
  • What moved along this route besides goods?
  • Who wrote this source, and what can archaeology add?
  • Who benefited from leaving Africa out of history?

Common mistakes to watch for

  • Africa was isolated before Europeans arrived.
  • Africa had no written history.
  • Outsiders built Africa's great monuments.
  • The empires existed at the same time (they followed one another).

Support (if the student is struggling)

  • Keep the review map visible during review (not during the checkpoint).
  • Provide a timeline card from Ghana to Great Zimbabwe.
  • Let the student answer one item aloud before writing.

Challenge (if the student is ready for more)

  • Compare the trade networks of Unit 5 with the Silk Road.
  • Research the Kilwa coins found in northern Australia and what historians think they show.

Scoring

The interactive lesson scores itself: full points on the first try, half points on the second try, then the answer and explanation appear. Mastery is 85. Written responses arrive in your results email with the score; score them with the rubric below before the student moves on.

Checkpoint scoring

Checkpoint questions are scored separately from the lesson. The student gets one try per question and the answers are not shown, so the checkpoint measures independent mastery and a retake stays fair. Your results email shows the auto-scored checkpoint points, a list of each item marked correct or MISSED, and the points available for the written items. Score the written items with their rubrics, then: checkpoint score = (auto points + rubric points) / total possible × 100. Mastery is 85. Below 85: teach a targeted review lesson on the parts missed (A to D), then the student retakes the checkpoint. Record both attempts in the Weekly Score Log.

Answer key: interactive lesson

Reconnect

  1. Week 21: Which animal made it possible to cross the Sahara with heavy loads?
    Answer: the camel
    Camels can go many days without water.
  2. Week 22: Which ruler of Mali made a famous hajj to Mecca in 1324?
    Answer: Mansa Musa
    His gold lowered its value in Cairo.
  3. Week 23: Which Songhai ruler divided the empire into provinces?
    Answer: Askia Muhammad
    He ruled from 1493 to 1528.
  4. Week 24: Which people's ancestors built Great Zimbabwe?
    Answer: the Shona
    A Bantu-speaking people of Zimbabwe.

Learn 1: Review: West African Empires

  1. Put these events in order.
    Answer: Ghana taxes the gold-salt trade → Sundiata founds Mali → Mansa Musa's hajj → Askia Muhammad organizes Songhai → Battle of Tondibi
    Ghana, then Mali, then Songhai.
  2. Choose two of the West African empires and compare how each gained and kept its wealth.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Two empires named; How each gained wealth, with specifics; One similarity or difference
    Model answer: Ghana grew rich by taxing the gold and salt that passed through its lands, without controlling the gold mines themselves. Mali controlled the gold fields and the trade cities directly, so it grew even richer, as Mansa Musa's hajj showed. Both depended on the trans-Saharan trade.

Learn 2: Review: East Africa and the Indian Ocean

  1. Give one example of cultural diffusion from the trans-Saharan trade and one from the Indian Ocean trade.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: A correct example from each network; Explains what spread and how
    Model answer: Across the Sahara, Islam and Arabic writing spread to West Africa with Muslim traders and scholars, helping Timbuktu become a center of learning. On the Indian Ocean, Arabic words entered the Swahili language, and Chinese porcelain became a prized decoration in Swahili homes.

Learn 3: Review: How We Know

  1. Choose one written source and one piece of archaeological evidence from Unit 5. Explain what each tells historians and one limit of each.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: One written source with what it shows and one limit; One archaeological find with what it shows and one limit
    Model answer: Ibn Battuta's account of Mali tells us how safe and just the empire seemed to a visitor, but he judged some customs by his own culture. Great Zimbabwe's walls show the builders' skill and wealth, but stones can't tell us the rulers' names or what people believed.

Hands-On

  1. Describe your playtest. Which event card taught the most history, which rule did you revise and why, and what did your family member learn?
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Names an event card and the history it teaches; Describes a rule revision and the reason; Reports what the player learned
    Model answer: The best card was "Monsoon reverses: you must wait in port until April," because it showed why merchants stayed for months. My sister found that the camel route was too easy, so I added a water rule: caravans must stop at an oasis every three spaces. She said she never knew African cities traded with China.

Think Deeper

  1. Many history books once left Africa out of world history before 1500. Using at least three examples from Unit 5, explain why that was a serious mistake.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: A clear claim; At least three accurate examples from Unit 5; Explains what is lost when Africa is left out
    Model answer: Leaving Africa out was a serious mistake because African empires were at the center of world trade. Mali's gold was so plentiful that Mansa Musa's visit changed prices in Cairo. Timbuktu was a world center of learning with scholars and libraries. Kilwa and Great Zimbabwe traded with Arabia, India and China. Without Africa, the story of medieval gold, Islam and the Indian Ocean makes no sense.

Checkpoint: Part A: West African empires

  1. Which two goods were traded for each other across the Sahara?
    Answer: gold and salt
    Gold came from the south; salt from Saharan mines.
  2. Who founded the Mali Empire, according to the epic preserved by griots?
    Answer: Sundiata Keita
    He defeated Sumanguru at Kirina.
  3. Explain one cause and two effects of Mansa Musa's hajj.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric (4 points toward the checkpoint).
    Rubric: A correct cause; Two correct effects
    Model answer: He made the hajj because he was a devout Muslim and the pilgrimage is a duty for those able to go. His gold made its value fall in Cairo, and the journey put Mali on European maps like the Catalan Atlas.
  4. Explain two causes of Songhai's fall in 1591.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric (4 points toward the checkpoint).
    Rubric: Two correct causes, explained
    Model answer: Morocco's army had guns and cannons that Songhai's spears and bows could not match, and Songhai had been weakened by a civil war between rival princes in the late 1580s.

Checkpoint: Part B: Timbuktu and learning

  1. Leo Africanus wrote that in Timbuktu, written books "are sold for more money than any other merchandize." What does this tell historians about Timbuktu, and why should they be careful with this source?
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric (4 points toward the checkpoint).
    Rubric: What the quotation shows about Timbuktu; A reason for caution about the source
    Model answer: It shows that learning was so valued in Timbuktu that books were the most profitable goods. Historians should be careful because Leo was a visitor writing years later, and we read him through translations.
  2. Who was Ahmad Baba, and why is he remembered?
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric (4 points toward the checkpoint).
    Rubric: Identifies him as a Timbuktu scholar exiled by Morocco; Explains his argument about race and slavery or his scholarship
    Model answer: Ahmad Baba was a famous Timbuktu scholar whom the Moroccans exiled to Marrakesh after 1591. He is remembered for his books and for arguing in 1615 that skin color was no reason for slavery.
  3. Explain how Timbuktu's manuscripts have been protected, from the 1500s to 2013.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric (4 points toward the checkpoint).
    Rubric: Protection in earlier centuries; The 2012 to 2013 rescue
    Model answer: Families kept manuscripts in private libraries, hiding them in chests and even burying them after 1591. In 2012, librarians smuggled more than 350,000 manuscripts to Bamako to save them from armed groups.

Checkpoint: Part C: The Indian Ocean world

  1. What seasonal winds carried ships between East Africa and Arabia and India?
    Answer: the monsoon winds
    They reverse about every six months.
  2. Describe Swahili culture, explaining what was African and what came from contact with traders.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric (4 points toward the checkpoint).
    Rubric: African roots (Bantu language, African people); Influences from contact (Islam, Arabic words, imported goods)
    Model answer: Swahili people were African and spoke a Bantu language, but centuries of trade added Arabic words, Islam as the main religion, and imported goods like Chinese porcelain set into their homes and tombs.
  3. Explain how Great Zimbabwe was connected to the Indian Ocean trade, and what evidence proves it.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric (4 points toward the checkpoint).
    Rubric: Explains the gold route to the coast; Names archaeological evidence of the connection
    Model answer: Great Zimbabwe's rulers controlled gold that was carried to Sofala and Kilwa and shipped across the Indian Ocean. Archaeologists found Chinese porcelain, Persian pottery, Indian glass beads and a coin from Kilwa at the site.

Checkpoint: Part D: Myth and evidence

  1. Explain the colonial myth about who built Great Zimbabwe, why it was spread, and how archaeologists disproved it.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric (5 points toward the checkpoint).
    Rubric: Describes the myth; Explains the motive; Names evidence or archaeologists who disproved it
    Model answer: Colonizers claimed that outsiders such as Phoenicians or the Queen of Sheba built Great Zimbabwe, because admitting Africans built it would undercut their claim to be bringing civilization. Archaeologists David Randall-MacIver in 1905 and Gertrude Caton-Thompson in 1929 showed it was built by the ancestors of the Shona.
  2. Compare the trans-Saharan and Indian Ocean trade networks: give one similarity and two differences.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric (5 points toward the checkpoint).
    Rubric: One correct similarity; Two correct differences, with specifics
    Model answer: Both carried African gold to distant markets and spread Islam. But trans-Saharan trade used camels across a desert while Indian Ocean trade used dhows on monsoon winds, and the Sahara trade exchanged gold for salt while the Indian Ocean trade brought cloth and porcelain from India and China.

Answer key: printable page

  1. Put in order and describe in one sentence each: Ghana, Mali, Songhai.
    Answer: Ghana taxed the gold-salt trade; Mali controlled gold and grew under Sundiata and Mansa Musa; Songhai became largest under Askia Muhammad.
  2. Why was Timbuktu important?
    Answer: A center of trade and Islamic learning with scholars, mosques and manuscripts.
  3. How did the monsoon winds shape Swahili trade?
    Answer: Ships sailed to Africa November to March and back April to September.
  4. Who built Great Zimbabwe, and how was the colonial myth disproved?
    Answer: Ancestors of the Shona; archaeologists Randall-MacIver and Caton-Thompson.
  5. Name one written source and one archaeological find from Unit 5.
    Answer: Ibn Battuta's account; Kilwa's Great Mosque (or Great Zimbabwe's walls).
