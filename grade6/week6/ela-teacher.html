Unit 2: Explaining the World (Informational) · Week 6 · ELA · Teacher Plan

Central Idea and Supporting Details

Lesson length: 60 minutes live, plus the after-class interactive lesson

Objectives

  • Explain how informational texts differ from narratives.
  • Determine the central idea of a text and state it as a complete sentence.
  • Identify supporting details (facts, examples, statistics) and explain how each supports the central idea.
  • Tell the difference between a topic, a central idea and a minor detail.
  • Use the roots geo, terr, hydr and aqua, and write an informational paragraph with a central idea and support.

Standards

  • ELA.6.R.2.2: Analyze the central idea(s), implied or explicit, and its development throughout a text.
  • ELA.6.R.2.1: Analyze how the structure of a text contributes to the meaning and/or purpose (preview).
  • ELA.6.C.1.4: Write expository texts to explain and/or analyze information from multiple sources, using relevant information and a logical organizational pattern.
  • ELA.6.V.1.2: Apply knowledge of Greek and Latin roots and affixes to determine meanings of words and phrases in grade-level content.
  • ELA.K12.EE.2.1: Read and comprehend grade-level complex texts proficiently.

Materials

  • The interactive lesson
  • The article printed for annotating
  • Two highlighters
  • A paper plate, paper strips and tape
  • Printable practice page

Vocabulary

  • informational text: Writing that explains real facts and ideas, such as articles, textbooks and reports.
  • topic: The subject of a text, usually a word or phrase (the Great Green Wall).
  • central idea: The most important point the author makes about the topic, stated as a complete sentence.
  • supporting detail: A fact, example, statistic, quotation or explanation that proves or develops the central idea.
  • minor detail: Interesting information that does not directly support the central idea.
  • statistic: A fact given as a number, such as 18 million hectares.
  • geo / terr: Roots meaning earth or land: geography, geology, territory, terrain.
  • hydr / aqua: Roots meaning water: hydrant, dehydrated, aquarium, aquatic.

Lesson flow

Key questions to ask

  • What is the topic? What does the author say about it?
  • Which ideas come up in most paragraphs?
  • Does this detail hold up the central idea, or is it just interesting?
  • Where is your proof?

Common mistakes to watch for

  • Writing the topic (one phrase) as the central idea.
  • Choosing the first sentence or one interesting fact as the central idea.
  • Thinking every true detail supports the central idea.
  • Slipping into story mode in informational writing.

Support (if the student is struggling)

  • Provide sentence frames: This article is mostly about ___. The author shows that ___.
  • Highlight repeated words to find the central idea.
  • Limit the paragraph to three details with a frame.

Challenge (if the student is ready for more)

  • Find a second article about the Great Green Wall and compare its central idea with this one.
  • Rewrite paragraph 3 so it supports a different central idea, then explain the change.

Background for the teacher

The article is original to KCE and its facts were checked against current sources. The African Union launched the Great Green Wall for the Sahara and the Sahel Initiative in 2007. Targets for 2030: restore 100 million hectares, sequester 250 million tonnes of carbon and create 10 million jobs, across an 8,000-km belt from Senegal to Djibouti. The UNCCD's 2020 status report estimated about 18 million hectares restored (later estimates differ). Farmer-managed natural regeneration (Niger) and zaï planting pits (Burkina Faso) are well-documented local methods. The quoted closing line is a paraphrase, not a direct quotation from a person.

Sources

  • UNCCD: The Great Green Wall, Implementation Status and Way Ahead to 2030 (September 2020).
  • National Geographic Education: Great Green Wall.
  • Mongabay (2023 and 2025) reporting on Great Green Wall progress.

How the lesson runs

During the live lesson, the student shares their screen and answers the Reconnect questions and the Learn checks live while you teach. The Hands-On activity, Think Deeper and Exit Check are completed after class.

Answer key: interactive lesson

Reconnect

  1. In Unit 1 you wrote theme statements. How is a theme in a story like a central idea in an article, and how is it different?
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Alike: both are the big message, stated as a complete sentence; Different: a theme is a lesson about life shown through characters; a central idea is the main point about a real topic
    Model answer: Both are the big idea of the text, written as a complete sentence. But a theme is a lesson about life that the reader figures out from characters and events, while a central idea is the author's main point about a real topic, usually supported with facts.
  2. Name two narrative techniques from Unit 1 that you would NOT usually find in a news article, and explain why.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Two narrative techniques (dialogue between characters, a slowed-down climax, first-person reflection, sensory scene-building); Reason: informational texts aim to explain facts clearly
    Model answer: A slowed-down climax and invented dialogue, because an article's job is to explain real facts clearly, not to build suspense in a story.
  3. Using roots from Unit 1, what does biography mean?
    Answer: writing about a person's life
    bio (life) + graph (write).

Learn 1: Topic vs. Central Idea

  1. A student says the central idea is: "The Great Green Wall was started in 2007." Explain why that is not the central idea.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: It is a single fact or detail; Most of the article is not about the start date; the central idea must cover the whole text
    Model answer: That is just one fact from paragraph 2. The article is mostly about how the project changed and what it teaches about healing land, so the start date is a supporting detail, not the central idea.
  2. Which paragraph most directly states the article's big lesson? Type the paragraph number.
    Answer: 6
    The last paragraph pulls the article together: land heals when communities lead the work and plan for the long run.
  3. Write the central idea of the article in your own words in one complete sentence.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Complete sentence; Covers the whole article, not one detail; In the student's own words
    Model answer: African countries are learning that the desert can be pushed back when local people lead the work and use both traditional methods and science, even though the project is far from finished.

Learn 2: Supporting Details

  1. Explain how the detail about zaï pits in paragraph 4 supports the central idea.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Zaï pits are a traditional local method; Shows that local knowledge helps the land heal, which is the central idea
    Model answer: Zaï pits are an old, low-cost method that local farmers use to catch rain and compost. This supports the central idea that the project works best when it uses local knowledge and communities lead the work.
  2. About what fraction of the 100-million-hectare goal had been restored by 2020, according to paragraph 5? Type a fraction in simplest form.
    Answer: 18/100 = 9/50 (less than one fifth)
    18 million out of 100 million = 18/100 = 9/50, which is less than 1/5 (20/100).
  3. Sort these into supporting details and minor details, and explain one choice: (a) the first plan was 15 kilometers wide; (b) many young trees died because no one cared for them; (c) war has slowed work in some countries; (d) Djibouti is near the Red Sea.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Supporting: (b) and (c); Minor: (a) and (d); Explains one choice clearly
    Model answer: Supporting: (b) and (c), because they explain why progress has been slow and why communities need to lead. Minor: (a) and (d). The exact width and Djibouti's location are true but do not help prove the central idea.

Learn 3: Roots: Earth and Water

  1. The Aswan High Dam produces hydroelectric power. Using the root, what kind of power is that?
    Answer: electricity made from moving water
    hydr means water.
  2. Complete each sentence with a root word.
    Answer: geologist / aquatic / terrain
    geologist (earth + one who studies), aquatic (water), terrain (land).
  3. A plant that is dehydrated has lost what?
    Answer: water
    de (remove) + hydr (water).

Hands-On

  1. Type your central idea and each supporting detail (with paragraph numbers), labeled fact, example or statistic. Then write the minor detail you left short and why.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Central idea as a complete sentence; At least three supporting details with paragraph numbers; Each labeled correctly; A minor detail with a reason
    Model answer: Central idea: Land in the Sahel can heal when local people lead the work and mix traditional methods with science, but progress is slow. Fact (2): the African Union started the project in 2007. Example (4): farmers in Niger let old tree roots sprout again. Example (4): zaï pits catch rain and compost. Statistic (5): about 18 million hectares were restored by 2020. Minor detail: Djibouti is near the Red Sea. It tells where the Sahel ends but does not prove how the land heals.

Think Deeper

  1. Write an informational paragraph of 120 to 180 words about the Nile flood (use your Social Studies lesson). Begin with a sentence that states your central idea, include at least three supporting details (one must be a number), and end with a concluding sentence. Do not tell a story.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Opening sentence states a clear central idea; Three or more accurate supporting details, including a statistic; Every detail supports the central idea; no minor details; Concluding sentence; Formal informational tone, no narrative
    Model answer: The yearly flood of the Nile made it possible for a great civilization to grow in the middle of a desert. Almost no rain falls in most of Egypt, but each summer, monsoon rains in the Ethiopian Highlands filled the Blue Nile and sent a flood north. When the water went down, it left a layer of rich black silt, which is why the Egyptians called their country Kemet, the Black Land. Farmers built walled basins to trap the floodwater and planted wheat and barley in the season called Peret. Officials measured the flood with nilometers marked in cubits; a flood of about 16 cubits meant a good harvest. Because the flood could be predicted and managed, Egypt produced extra grain year after year. The Nile's flood, and the people who learned to control it, turned a desert into one of the longest-lasting civilizations in history.
  2. Paragraph 5 reports that progress has been slow. Why might the author include bad news in an article about a hopeful project? What does that do for the reader's trust?
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Honest, balanced reporting; Builds trust because the author is not hiding problems; Connects to the central idea that the work is long-term
    Model answer: The author includes the bad news to be honest and balanced. If the article only praised the project, readers might not trust it. Showing the problems also supports the central idea that healing land takes communities and a long time.

Exit Check

  1. What is the difference between a topic and a central idea? Give an example of each for an article about the Nile.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Topic is a word or phrase; central idea is a complete sentence about the topic; Accurate Nile examples
    Model answer: A topic is a word or phrase, like the Nile flood. A central idea is a complete sentence about it, like: The Nile's yearly flood made farming possible in the desert.
  2. "About 18 million hectares had been restored by 2020" is what kind of supporting detail?
    Answer: statistic
    A fact given as a number is a statistic.
  3. What does the root terr mean?
    Answer: land or earth
    terr means land or earth, as in territory.
  4. What does the root aqua mean?
    Answer: water
    aqua means water, as in aquarium.
  5. Write one supporting detail from the article and explain in one sentence how it supports the central idea.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Accurate detail; Clear connection to the central idea
    Model answer: Farmers in Niger let old tree roots sprout again. This shows that local, traditional methods help the land heal, which is the central idea.

Answer key: printable page

  1. What is the topic of the article? What is its central idea?
    Answer: Topic: the Great Green Wall. Central idea: land in the Sahel can heal when communities lead and use local knowledge with science, though progress is slow.
  2. List one fact, one example and one statistic from the article, with paragraph numbers.
    Answer: Fact: launched in 2007 (2). Example: farmers in Niger protect old roots (4). Statistic: about 18 million hectares restored by 2020 (5).
  3. Write one minor detail from the article and explain why it is minor.
    Answer: For example: Djibouti is near the Red Sea; it gives location but does not prove the central idea.
  4. Write a word with each root and its meaning: geo ____ terr ____ hydr ____ aqua ____
    Answer: geography (earth); territory (land); hydrant (water); aquarium (water)
  5. Write a central idea sentence for a paragraph about why people need clean water.
    Answer: For example: Clean water is essential for health, farming and daily life everywhere in the world.
  6. Explain how a central idea is like the top of a table.
    Answer: The central idea is what the whole text holds up; each supporting detail is a leg that supports it.
