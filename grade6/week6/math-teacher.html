Unit 2: Fractions and Ratios · Week 6 · Math · Teacher Plan

Multiplying Fractions

Lesson length: 60 minutes live, plus the after-class interactive lesson

Objectives

  • Find a fraction of a whole number and of a fraction, using models and the algorithm.
  • Simplify before multiplying using common factors (the GCF from Unit 1).
  • Multiply mixed numbers by changing them to improper fractions.
  • Predict whether a product will be greater or less than a factor.
  • Split a shared cost fairly using fractions.

Standards

  • MA.6.NSO.2.2: Extend previous understanding of multiplication and division to compute products and quotients of positive fractions by positive fractions, including mixed numbers, with procedural fluency.
  • MA.6.NSO.2.3: Solve multi-step real-world problems involving any of the four operations with positive multi-digit decimals or positive fractions, including mixed numbers.
  • MA.K12.MTR.2.1: Demonstrate understanding by representing problems in multiple ways (area models).
  • MA.K12.MTR.7.1: Apply mathematics to real-world contexts.
  • Financial literacy: sharing costs fairly and budgeting for a group (Jump$tart Spending and Saving).

Materials

  • The interactive lesson
  • Graph paper or a printed rectangle for area models
  • Measuring cups (1, 1/2, 1/3, 1/4) and dry rice
  • Printable practice page

Vocabulary

  • numerator: The top number of a fraction: how many parts are counted.
  • denominator: The bottom number: how many equal parts make the whole.
  • improper fraction: A fraction whose numerator is greater than or equal to its denominator, such as 7/4.
  • mixed number: A whole number and a fraction together, such as 1 3/4.
  • simplest form: A fraction whose numerator and denominator have no common factor except 1.
  • reciprocal: Two numbers whose product is 1, such as 3/4 and 4/3 (used next week).
  • fair share: A portion that matches each person's agreed part of the use or the cost.

Key questions to ask

  • What is the whole?
  • Will the answer be more or less than the starting amount? Why?
  • Can you simplify before you multiply?
  • What does fair mean in this situation?

Common mistakes to watch for

  • Multiplying whole parts and fraction parts of mixed numbers separately.
  • Thinking multiplication always makes numbers bigger.
  • Forgetting to simplify, or simplifying across addition.
  • Adding denominators when multiplying.

Support (if the student is struggling)

  • Use fraction strips or paper folding for fraction-of-a-fraction.
  • Provide a step card: change, simplify, multiply, change back.
  • Begin with unit fractions (1/3 of 1/2) before others.

Challenge (if the student is ready for more)

  • Find two different fractions whose product is 3/8.
  • Plan a reunion budget of $500 using at least four fractional categories that total the whole.

Background for the teacher

Engine note: fraction answers may be typed as fractions (3/4), mixed numbers (7 1/2) or exact decimals (0.75). All names and amounts are illustrative. This week begins the Unit 2 money thread on splitting costs fairly; Week 7 connects fractions to recipes, servings and grocery costs.

How the lesson runs

During the live lesson, the student shares their screen and answers the Reconnect questions and the Learn checks live while you teach. The Hands-On activity, Think Deeper and Exit Check are completed after class.

Scoring

The interactive lesson scores itself: full points on the first try, half points on the second try, then the answer and explanation appear. Mastery is 85. Written responses arrive in your results email with the score; score them with the rubric below before the student moves on.

Answer key: interactive lesson

Reconnect

  1. What is the GCF of 18 and 30?
    Answer: 6
    18 = 2 × 3 × 3, 30 = 2 × 3 × 5. Shared: 2 × 3 = 6.
  2. Use the GCF to write 18/30 in simplest form. Type it as a fraction, like 2/7.
    Answer: 3/5
    Divide both by 6: 18/30 = 3/5.
  3. What is the LCM of 4 and 6?
    Answer: 12
    Multiples of 6: 6, 12. 12 is also a multiple of 4.
  4. Write 0.375 as a fraction in simplest form.
    Answer: 3/8
    375/1000; divide both by 125 to get 3/8.
  5. Explain the difference between a problem that needs the GCF and one that needs the LCM. Give a one-sentence example of each.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: GCF: splitting into the largest equal groups, with an example; LCM: when things line up again or the smallest shared amount, with an example
    Model answer: The GCF splits things into the largest equal groups, like packing 36 muffins and 48 cookies into identical bags. The LCM finds when things line up, like two buses that leave every 15 and 20 minutes leaving together again.

Learn 1: A Fraction of a Whole Number

  1. 3/8 of the 40 relatives at the reunion are children. How many children are there?
    Answer: 15 children
    40 ÷ 8 = 5, and 3 × 5 = 15.
  2. Without calculating, is 7/8 × 56 more or less than 56? Explain using what multiplying by a fraction less than 1 means.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Less; Explains that 7/8 is less than 1, so the product is only part of 56
    Model answer: Less, because 7/8 is less than 1, so I am taking only part of 56 (it is 49).

Learn 2: A Fraction of a Fraction

  1. Kwame takes 1/3 of the 3/4 pan of cornbread that is left. What fraction of the whole pan does he take?
    Answer: 1/4
    1/3 × 3/4 = 3/12 = 1/4.
  2. 5/6 × 9/10 = ? Type the answer in simplest form.
    Answer: 3/4
    Simplify first: 5 and 10 share 5; 9 and 6 share 3. (1 × 3)/(2 × 2) = 3/4.
  3. 4/9 × 3/8 = ? Type the answer in simplest form.
    Answer: 1/6
    Simplify first: (1 × 1)/(3 × 2) = 1/6.
  4. Draw (or describe) an area model for 3/5 × 1/2. How many parts are in the whole, how many are in the overlap, and what is the product?
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Whole has 10 parts; Overlap has 3 parts; Product 3/10
    Model answer: I split a rectangle into 5 columns and shaded 3, then split it into 2 rows and shaded 1. The whole has 10 parts and the overlap has 3, so 3/5 × 1/2 = 3/10.

Learn 3: Multiplying Mixed Numbers

  1. The recipe uses 2/3 cup of buttermilk per pan. How much for 2 1/2 pans? Type a mixed number.
    Answer: 1 2/3 cups
    2/3 × 5/2 = 10/6 = 5/3 = 1 2/3 cups.
  2. 2 1/4 × 1 1/3 = ?
    Answer: 3
    9/4 × 4/3 = 36/12 = 3.
  3. A rectangular picnic blanket is 2 1/2 yards long and 1 3/5 yards wide. What is its area in square yards?
    Answer: 4 square yards
    5/2 × 8/5 = 40/10 = 4 square yards.
  4. Jada says 2 1/2 × 2 1/2 = 4 1/4 because 2 × 2 = 4 and 1/2 × 1/2 = 1/4. Find her mistake, give the correct product, and explain how an estimate could have warned her.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Correct product 6 1/4; Explains she skipped the cross parts (2 × 1/2 twice) or should use improper fractions; Estimate: 2 1/2 × 2 1/2 is more than 2 × 3 = 6
    Model answer: She multiplied only the whole parts and only the fraction parts, so she left out 2 × 1/2 and 1/2 × 2. Using improper fractions: 5/2 × 5/2 = 25/4 = 6 1/4. An estimate warns her: 2 1/2 × 2 is already 5, so the answer must be more than 5, and 4 1/4 is too small.
  5. An Egyptian royal cubit was divided into 7 palms. A nilometer mark reads 15 3/4 cubits. How many palms is that? Type a mixed number.
    Answer: 110 1/4 palms
    15 3/4 × 7 = 63/4 × 7 = 441/4 = 110 1/4 palms.

Learn 4: Splitting Costs Fairly

  1. How much does Uncle Desmond's household pay?
    Answer: $160
    1/3 × 480 = $160.
  2. What fraction of the guests does Aunt Lena's household bring?
    Answer: 1/6
    1 − 1/2 − 1/3 = 6/6 − 3/6 − 2/6 = 1/6.
  3. How much does Aunt Lena's household pay?
    Answer: $80
    1/6 × 480 = $80. Check: 240 + 160 + 80 = 480.
  4. Aunt Lena cooked all the food and spent 6 hours doing it. Is splitting only by number of guests still fair? Suggest a fairer plan and explain it with numbers.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Takes a reasoned position; Proposes a specific plan with numbers that still total $96; Explains why it is fairer
    Model answer: Not completely, because her work is worth something too. One plan: count her cooking as worth her $80 share, so she pays $0, and the other two households split her $80 by their guest fractions. Amara's household pays 3/5 of it ($48) and Uncle Desmond's pays 2/5 ($32), for totals of $288 and $192, which still add to $480.

Hands-On

  1. Type the original amounts, your scale factor, and each scaled amount with the work shown.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: At least four ingredients; Correct multiplication with improper fractions; Simplified answers; Units included
    Model answer: Scale factor: 2 1/2. Flour 1 3/4 cups × 5/2 = 35/8 = 4 3/8 cups. Sugar 1/3 cup × 5/2 = 5/6 cup. Milk 3/4 cup × 5/2 = 15/8 = 1 7/8 cups. Salt 1/2 teaspoon × 5/2 = 5/4 = 1 1/4 teaspoons.
  2. Which scaled amount was hardest to measure with real cups, and how did you do it?
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Names a hard amount; Explains a real measuring strategy
    Model answer: 5/6 cup was hardest because I have no 1/6 cup. I used 1/2 cup plus 1/3 cup, because 3/6 + 2/6 = 5/6.

Think Deeper

  1. Explain why multiplying the numerators and the denominators works, using an area model or an example from the lesson. Then explain why 3/4 × 2/3 gives the same answer as 2/3 × 3/4.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Explains the denominators make the size of each small part; Explains the numerators count the overlapping parts; Explains the commutative property with the model rotated
    Model answer: In the area model, multiplying the denominators (3 × 4 = 12) tells how many equal small parts the whole is cut into. Multiplying the numerators (2 × 3 = 6) counts the parts in the overlap, so the product is 6/12. If I switch the order, I am just turning the rectangle sideways, so the overlap is still 6 of 12 parts. That is the commutative property.
  2. The reunion committee has $240. They spend 3/8 on food and 1/4 on renting the pavilion, and save 1/5 of what is left for next year. How much is saved? Show every step.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Food $90 and pavilion $60; Left $90; Saved 1/5 of 90 = $18; Clear steps
    Model answer: Food: 3/8 × 240 = $90. Pavilion: 1/4 × 240 = $60. Spent: $150, so $90 is left. Saved: 1/5 × 90 = $18.

Exit Check

  1. 5/8 of $72 = ?
    Answer: $45
    72 ÷ 8 = 9, and 5 × 9 = 45.
  2. 7/12 × 3/14 = ? Type in simplest form.
    Answer: 1/8
    Simplify first: 7 and 14 share 7; 3 and 12 share 3. (1 × 1)/(4 × 2) = 1/8.
  3. 3 1/3 × 1 1/5 = ?
    Answer: 4
    10/3 × 6/5 = 60/15 = 4.
  4. A trail is 4 1/2 miles long. Dev has hiked 2/3 of it. How many miles has he hiked?
    Answer: 3 miles
    2/3 × 9/2 = 18/6 = 3 miles.
  5. Three roommates split a $1,350 rent. One has the big room and pays 2/5. The other two split the rest equally. How much does each of the other two pay?
    Answer: $405
    2/5 × 1,350 = $540. 1,350 − 540 = $810, and 810 ÷ 2 = $405 each.

Answer key: printable page

  1. A 4 1/2-pound bag of rice: the family uses 2/3 of it at the reunion and 1/4 of what is left the next day. How much rice is left?
    Answer: 4 1/2 × 1/3 = 1 1/2 lb left after the reunion; 1/4 of that is 3/8 lb; 1 1/2 − 3/8 = 1 1/8 lb remains.
  2. 3/8 × 4/9 = ____ 6/7 × 14/15 = ____
    Answer: 1/6; 4/5
  3. 1 1/2 × 2 2/3 = ____ 3 3/4 × 1 3/5 = ____
    Answer: 4; 6
  4. Draw an area model for 2/5 × 3/4 and write the product.
    Answer: 5 rows by 4 columns = 20 parts; overlap 6; 6/20 = 3/10
  5. Four friends rent a beach cabin for $360. Two of them stay the whole time and agree to pay 1/3 each. The other two split the rest. How much does each pay?
    Answer: 1/3 × 360 = $120 each for the first two; $120 left, so $60 each for the other two.
  6. Without calculating, circle the products less than 12: 3/4 × 12, 5/4 × 12, 1 1/2 × 12, 2/3 × 12. Explain your rule.
    Answer: 3/4 × 12 and 2/3 × 12; multiplying by a fraction less than 1 gives less than 12.
