Unit 2: Cells, the Building Blocks of Life · Week 6 · Life Science · Teacher Plan

Cell Theory and the Microscope

Lesson length: 60 minutes live, plus the after-class interactive lesson

Objectives

  • State the three parts of the cell theory.
  • Explain how the microscope made the cell theory possible, and describe the work of Hooke, Leeuwenhoek, Schleiden, Schwann, Remak and Virchow.
  • Name the parts of a compound light microscope and calculate total magnification.
  • Compare the sizes of cells with everyday objects using metric units.
  • Explain how the cell theory was built from evidence and changed over time.

Standards

  • SC.6.L.14.2: Investigate and explain the components of the scientific theory of cells (cell theory): all organisms are composed of cells (single-celled or multi-cellular), all cells come from pre-existing cells, and cells are the basic unit of life.
  • SC.6.N.3.1: Recognize and explain that a scientific theory is a well-supported and widely accepted explanation of nature and is not simply a claim posed by an individual.
  • SC.6.N.2.2: Explain that scientific knowledge is durable because it is open to change as new evidence or interpretations are encountered.
  • SC.6.N.2.3: Recognize that scientists who make contributions to scientific knowledge come from all kinds of backgrounds and possess varied talents, interests, and goals.
  • MA.6 connection: multiplying and dividing decimals for magnification and size.

Materials

  • The interactive lesson
  • A clear plastic sheet, dropper and water
  • An onion, salt and printed text
  • A magnifying glass or microscope if available
  • Printable practice page

Vocabulary

  • cell: The smallest unit that can carry out all the processes of life.
  • cell theory: The scientific theory that all living things are made of cells, the cell is the basic unit of life, and all cells come from existing cells.
  • organism: A living thing.
  • unicellular: Made of one cell, such as a bacterium.
  • multicellular: Made of many cells, such as a plant or a person.
  • microscope: A tool that uses lenses to make small objects look larger.
  • magnification: How many times larger an object appears. Total magnification = eyepiece power × objective power.
  • scientific theory: A well-tested explanation supported by a large body of evidence from many scientists.

Key questions to ask

  • How did a new tool change what scientists could know?
  • What evidence supports each part of the cell theory?
  • What makes a scientific theory different from a guess?
  • Who gets credit, and why does it matter?

Common mistakes to watch for

  • Thinking Hooke saw living cells.
  • Believing a scientific theory is a guess.
  • Thinking only animals are made of cells, or that cells are all the same size.
  • Adding instead of multiplying lens powers.

Support (if the student is struggling)

  • Provide a labeled microscope diagram to keep.
  • Use a timeline card with one fact per scientist.
  • Model the magnification formula with two examples.

Challenge (if the student is ready for more)

  • Research how electron microscopes go beyond light microscopes and what they revealed.
  • Find out how Leeuwenhoek's microscopes were built and why they were so powerful for single lenses.

Background for the teacher

Hooke published Micrographia in 1665. Leeuwenhoek began reporting microorganisms to the Royal Society in the 1670s; his best surviving lens magnifies about 270 times. Schleiden (1838) and Schwann (1839) proposed that plants and animals are composed of cells. Robert Remak published evidence that cells arise by division in 1852; Virchow's 1855 essay popularized omnis cellula e cellula without crediting Remak, who faced antisemitic barriers to advancement. Ernest Everett Just (1883 to 1941) earned his PhD at the University of Chicago in 1916, received the first Spingarn Medal in 1915, and published The Biology of the Cell Surface in 1939. Human cell count estimates are about 30 trillion (Sender, Fuchs and Milo, 2016). Sizes on the scale chart are typical values.

Answer key: interactive lesson

Reconnect

  1. A scientist looks at pond water and sees tiny moving specks. Write one observation and one inference.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Observation uses the senses only; Inference is a possible explanation
    Model answer: Observation: tiny green specks are moving quickly in the water. Inference: the specks might be living things that can swim.
  2. Convert 0.5 millimeters to micrometers. (1 mm = 1,000 micrometers)
    Answer: 500 micrometers
    0.5 × 1,000 = 500 micrometers. Micrometers are the unit scientists use for cells.
  3. Convert 2.5 centimeters to millimeters.
    Answer: 25 mm
    2.5 × 10 = 25 mm.
  4. In Week 5 you learned that scientists replicate each other's work. Why would that matter for a big claim like "every living thing is made of cells"?
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Many scientists checking many organisms builds trust; One person's observations could be wrong or limited
    Model answer: A claim about every living thing needs evidence from many scientists looking at many kinds of organisms. If only one person checked, they might have made a mistake or looked at too few living things.

Learn 1: The Scientists Who Saw Cells

  1. Hooke named cells, but he never saw a living one. Explain why.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Cork is dead plant tissue; He saw only empty cell walls
    Model answer: Cork comes from dead bark, so the cells were empty. Hooke saw only the walls of dead plant cells, not the living material inside.
  2. Who was the first person to describe living single-celled organisms?
    Answer: Antonie van Leeuwenhoek
    In the 1670s Leeuwenhoek described tiny living organisms he called animalcules.
  3. Robert Remak published the idea that cells come from cells before Rudolf Virchow, but Virchow got most of the credit. Why does it matter who gets credit in science, and what does this story teach us?
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Credit rewards the people who did the work and builds trust; Recognizes that scientists can be treated unfairly (Remak faced discrimination as a Jewish scientist); A lesson about honesty or citing sources
    Model answer: Credit matters because it rewards the people who did the work and lets others find and check the original evidence. Remak, a Jewish scientist, faced discrimination and did not get the credit he earned. The story teaches that scientists must be honest and cite the people whose work they use.

Learn 2: The Three Parts of the Cell Theory

  1. Write the three parts of the cell theory in your own words.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: All living things are made of cells; The cell is the basic unit of life; Cells come from other cells
    Model answer: Every living thing is made of at least one cell. The cell is the smallest unit that does the jobs of life. New cells only come from cells that already exist.
  2. A friend says, "The cell theory is just a theory, so it's only a guess." How would you respond?
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Explains that a scientific theory is a well-tested explanation; Mentions large amounts of evidence from many scientists over many years
    Model answer: In science, a theory is not a guess. It is an explanation that has been tested and supported by huge amounts of evidence from many scientists over almost 200 years, like Schleiden, Schwann and Virchow, and everyone who has looked at cells since.
  3. Is a rock alive? Use the cell theory to explain your answer.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Not alive; It is not made of cells
    Model answer: No. A rock is not made of cells, and according to the cell theory, all living things are made of cells.

Learn 3: Using a Microscope

  1. A microscope has a 10× eyepiece and a 4× objective. What is the total magnification?
    Answer: 40 ×
    10 × 4 = 40×.
  2. Leeuwenhoek's best lenses magnified about 270×. How many times more powerful is a modern 10× eyepiece with a 100× objective?
    Answer: about 3.7 times
    10 × 100 = 1,000×. 1,000 ÷ 270 ≈ 3.7.
  3. A cell looks 4 mm wide at 400×. How wide is it really, in millimeters?
    Answer: 0.01 mm
    4 ÷ 400 = 0.01 mm (10 micrometers).
  4. Why should you always start focusing with the lowest-power objective?
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Easier to find the object (wider view); Avoids crashing the lens into the slide
    Model answer: The low-power lens shows a wider area, so it is easier to find what you are looking for, and it sits higher above the slide so you will not crack the slide with the lens.

Learn 4: Scientist Spotlight: Ernest Everett Just

  1. What part of the cell did Ernest Everett Just show was important, and what barrier did he face as a scientist?
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: The cell surface (in fertilization and development); Racism kept him from positions at major U.S. research universities
    Model answer: He showed that the cell's surface plays a key role in fertilization and how an egg develops. Racism kept him out of major American research universities, so he did much of his work in summers at Woods Hole and later in Europe.

Hands-On

  1. Which drop size magnified more, and how do you know? Estimate how many times larger the letters looked.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Smaller, more curved drop magnifies more; Explains how they compared; Gives a reasonable estimate
    Model answer: The smaller drop magnified more. A letter that was 2 mm tall looked about 6 mm tall under the small drop, so about 3 times larger. The bigger drop was flatter and only made letters a little bigger.
  2. Describe what you saw in two of the items, and explain what a real microscope would let you see that your water drop could not.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Two specific observations; Recognizes the limits of a water lens; Connects to individual cells needing higher magnification
    Model answer: Salt grains looked like tiny clear cubes, and the onion skin looked like a thin sheet with faint lines. A real microscope at 100× or 400× would show the onion's individual cells as boxes with walls and a dark nucleus in each, which my water drop was not strong enough to show.

Think Deeper

  1. "New tools lead to new evidence, and new evidence leads to new theories." Use the history of the cell theory to explain this statement, naming at least three scientists.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Explains the microscope as the new tool; Names three scientists and what each contributed; Shows how evidence built up into the theory over time
    Model answer: Before microscopes, no one could see cells. Better lenses let Hooke see and name cells in cork in 1665 and let Leeuwenhoek see living single-celled organisms in the 1670s. Over the next 150 years, microscopes improved, and Schleiden and Schwann used them to show that plants and animals are both made of cells. Remak then observed cells dividing, and Virchow spread the idea that every cell comes from a cell. Each new tool gave new evidence, and the evidence slowly built the cell theory.
  2. Long ago, many people believed that mice could appear from piles of old rags and grain. How does the third part of the cell theory challenge that idea? Design a simple test that could check it.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Cells only come from cells, so mice must come from parent mice; A fair test: sealed versus open containers of rags and grain; Predicts mice appear only where mice can get in
    Model answer: The cell theory says all cells come from existing cells, so a mouse, made of cells, must come from other mice, not from rags. To test it, I would put rags and grain in two containers, one sealed so mice cannot get in and one open. If mice appear only in the open one, it shows they came in from outside, not from the rags.

Exit Check

  1. State the three parts of the cell theory.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Living things are made of cells; The cell is the basic unit of life; Cells come from existing cells
    Model answer: All living things are made of cells, the cell is the basic unit of life, and all cells come from other cells.
  2. What is the total magnification with a 15× eyepiece and a 40× objective?
    Answer: 600 ×
    15 × 40 = 600×.
  3. Which scientist first named cells after looking at cork?
    Answer: Robert Hooke
    Robert Hooke, in 1665.
  4. Is a single bacterium unicellular or multicellular?
    Answer: unicellular
    A bacterium is one cell.
  5. A cell is 0.02 mm wide. How many of them would fit side by side across 1 millimeter?
    Answer: 50 cells
    1 ÷ 0.02 = 50 cells.

Answer key: printable page

  1. Write the three parts of the cell theory.
    Answer: All living things are made of cells; the cell is the basic unit of life; all cells come from existing cells.
  2. Match each scientist to the discovery: Hooke, Leeuwenhoek, Schwann, Remak.
    Answer: Hooke: named cells in cork; Leeuwenhoek: saw living single-celled organisms; Schwann: animals are made of cells; Remak: cells come from dividing cells.
  3. Find the total magnification: 10× eyepiece with 4×, 10× and 40× objectives.
    Answer: 40×, 100×, 400×
  4. An image of a cell is 6 mm wide at 600×. How wide is the real cell?
    Answer: 6 ÷ 600 = 0.01 mm
  5. Explain the difference between a scientific theory and a guess.
    Answer: A scientific theory is a well-tested explanation supported by lots of evidence from many scientists; a guess has not been tested.
  6. Name one thing Ernest Everett Just discovered and one barrier he faced.
    Answer: The importance of the cell surface in fertilization and development; racism kept him out of major U.S. research universities.
