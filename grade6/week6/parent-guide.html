Week 6: Unit 2 Begins: Rivers, Cells, Fractions and Facts

Unit 2 opens this week. Your child travels down the Nile, from its sources in East Africa and Ethiopia to the Mediterranean, to see how a river flowing north through the desert built the kingdom of Kemet, looks into the invisible world of cells, multiplies fractions to scale recipes and split costs, and learns to find the central idea of an informational article.

How scoring works: each lesson scores itself. Your child gets two tries per question: full points on the first try, half points on the second, then the answer and explanation appear. A score of 85 or higher means the lesson is mastered. Written answers go to Jonathan, who scores them with a rubric.

Time this week: About 60 minutes of live lessons per subject, plus 30 to 45 minutes of after-class work per subject.

Gather this week: A baking pan, sand, dark potting soil or coffee grounds, water, a clear cup, a ruler, a marker, a towel and optional bean seeds (Social Studies); a clear page protector, a dropper or straw, printed text, salt, sugar, a leaf, a hair, a fabric scrap, an onion, a millimeter ruler and a magnifying glass if you have one (Science); measuring cups and spoons, dry rice, a family recipe and graph paper (Math); a printed copy of the article, two highlighters, a paper plate, paper strips, tape, scissors and a marker (ELA).

Heads up: The floodplain model and the water-drop magnifier can be a little messy; a towel underneath helps. Unit 1 project feedback comes back this week: if a project scored below 85, your child revises it once using the feedback.

ELA: Central Idea and Supporting Details

Your child reads an article about Africa's Great Green Wall, a project to restore land across the Sahel, then learns to state its central idea and prove it with facts, examples and statistics. They write an informational paragraph about the Nile flood.

Ask at dinner: "What was that article mostly about, in one sentence? What's your proof?"

Try it together: Pick a news story or a cereal box and ask your child to find the central idea and two supporting details.

Signs it's clicking: They state the central idea as a full sentence, not just a topic, and explain how each detail supports it. Signs they need help: They pick one interesting fact as the central idea, or slip into storytelling in their paragraph.

Math: Multiplying Fractions

Your child multiplies fractions and mixed numbers using area models and the standard method, simplifies first using common factors, and uses fractions to scale a recipe and split a family cookout cost fairly.

Ask at dinner: "If we make 2 1/2 batches of this recipe, how much of each ingredient do we need?"

Try it together: Scale a real family recipe together and measure the new amounts.

Signs it's clicking: They estimate first and change mixed numbers to improper fractions before multiplying. Signs they need help: They multiply the whole numbers and the fractions separately in mixed numbers. Ask them to estimate first.

Life Science: Cell Theory and the Microscope

Your child learns how the microscope revealed cells, follows the scientists who built the cell theory (including Robert Remak, who deserved more credit than he got), calculates microscope magnification, and meets cell biologist Ernest Everett Just. They build a simple water-drop magnifier.

Ask at dinner: "What does it mean that every cell comes from another cell?"

Try it together: Look at salt, a leaf and onion skin together through the water-drop magnifier or a magnifying glass.

Signs it's clicking: They can state the three parts of the cell theory and explain that a scientific theory is not a guess. Signs they need help: They add the eyepiece and objective numbers instead of multiplying them.

World and American History: The Nile: Geography and the Rise of Kemet

Your child maps the Nile from Lake Victoria and the Ethiopian Highlands to the Mediterranean, learns why the south was called Upper Egypt, sees how the flood created Kemet (the Black Land), and learns that the kingdom was unified from the south around 3100 BCE.

Ask at dinner: "Why would a farmer in Egypt care about the rain in Ethiopia?"

Try it together: Build the floodplain model together and watch where the dark soil settles.

Signs it's clicking: They explain that the Nile flows north and that people had to work together to manage the flood. Signs they need help: They place Upper Egypt in the north because it is at the top of the map. Remind them: upstream is south.
