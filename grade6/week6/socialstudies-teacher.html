Unit 2: The Nile Valley and Ancient Africa · Week 6 · World and American History · Teacher Plan

The Nile: Geography and the Rise of Kemet (Egypt)

Lesson length: 60 minutes live, plus the after-class interactive lesson

Objectives

  • Locate the Nile, its two main sources, the cataracts, Upper and Lower Egypt and Nubia on a map.
  • Explain how the yearly flood created the Black Land and shaped the farming year.
  • Describe how people used basin irrigation and the nilometer to manage the flood.
  • Explain how the drying of the Sahara brought peoples to the Nile Valley.
  • Describe how the kingdom of Kemet was unified from the south around 3100 BCE.

Standards

  • SS.6.G.2.4: Explain how the geographical location of ancient civilizations contributed to the culture and politics of those societies.
  • SS.6.G.2.1: Explain how major physical characteristics, natural resources, climate, and absolute and relative locations have influenced settlement, interactions, and the economies of ancient civilizations of the world.
  • SS.6.W.2.2: Describe how the developments of agriculture and metallurgy related to settlement, population growth, and the emergence of civilization.
  • SS.6.W.2.4: Compare the economic, political, social, and religious institutions of ancient river civilizations (preview).
  • C3 Framework D2.Geo.4.6-8: Explain how cultural patterns and economic decisions influence environments and the daily lives of people in both nearby and distant places.

Materials

  • The interactive lesson
  • A baking pan, sand, dark soil and water
  • A clear cup, ruler and marker
  • A world map or globe
  • Printable practice page

Vocabulary

  • Kemet: The ancient Egyptians' own name for their country, meaning the Black Land.
  • Deshret: The Red Land: the desert on either side of the Nile Valley.
  • inundation: The yearly flood of the Nile.
  • silt: Fine, rich soil carried by flowing water and left behind when a flood goes down.
  • cataract: A stretch of rocky rapids in a river. The Nile has six main cataracts between Aswan and Khartoum.
  • basin irrigation: Trapping floodwater in walled fields (basins) so it soaks into the soil.
  • nilometer: A staircase or marked pillar used to measure the height of the Nile's flood.
  • delta: The wide, triangle-shaped area where a river splits into branches before reaching the sea.
  • unification: Joining separate regions into one country under one government.

Key questions to ask

  • Why is the south called Upper Egypt?
  • Where did the floodwater come from?
  • What work did people do to turn the flood into food?
  • Why might a king want to be shown wearing both crowns?

Common mistakes to watch for

  • Thinking Upper Egypt is in the north because it is at the top of the map.
  • Believing the flood came from rain in Egypt.
  • Picturing Egypt as separate from the rest of Africa.
  • Treating the Narmer Palette as an exact news report rather than royal art.

Support (if the student is struggling)

  • Give a blank Nile map to label while teaching.
  • Use a seasons card with pictures.
  • Pair the cubit problem with a ruler: about two hand-spans.

Challenge (if the student is ready for more)

  • Research how ancient Egyptians used the rising of the star Sirius to predict the flood.
  • Compare the Nile with the Mississippi or the Ganges: how does each river's flood affect farming?

Background for the teacher

The Nile is about 6,650 km long. The Blue Nile and Atbara supply most of the floodwater and sediment reaching Egypt. The Egyptian civil year had three seasons: Akhet (inundation), Peret (emergence) and Shemu (harvest), with approximate modern months given in the lesson. The cubit used for nilometer readings is the royal cubit, about 52.3 to 52.5 cm. Kmt (Kemet) is attested as a name for Egypt; most Egyptologists relate it to the dark Nile soil, while Cheikh Anta Diop argued it referred to the people; Week 7 examines this debate with evidence. Archaeologists such as Maria Gatto and Stuart Tyson Smith describe close cultural ties between predynastic Upper Egypt, Lower Nubia and the eastern Sahara. Narmer is generally dated to about 3100 BCE; the Narmer Palette, discovered at Hierakonpolis in 1897 to 1898 by James Quibell and Frederick Green, is in the Egyptian Museum in Cairo. Its scenes may record a historical conquest or express the idea of unification symbolically.

Answer key: interactive lesson

Reconnect

  1. What word means extra food that can be stored or traded, which allowed some people to become specialists?
    Answer: surplus
    A surplus lets some people do work other than farming.
  2. In Week 4 you read that the Green Sahara dried after about 5,000 years ago. Predict what herders living there would have done, and why.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: They moved toward water: the Nile, oases or the Sahel; Because grass and water disappeared
    Model answer: They probably moved toward reliable water, like the Nile Valley or farther south, because the grass for their cattle and the lakes dried up.
  3. On a map, which direction is usually at the top?
    Answer: north
    Most maps put north at the top, which matters for this week's surprise.
  4. The Narmer Palette is a carved stone object from about 3100 BCE. Is it a primary or secondary source, and what is one question a historian should ask about it?
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Primary source; A good historian's question (who made it, for whom, what message, is it propaganda)
    Model answer: It is a primary source because it was made at the time. A historian should ask who made it and what message the king wanted people to believe.

Learn 1: Where the Water Comes From

  1. Name the two main branches of the Nile and where each begins.
    Answer: white / blue
    The White Nile comes from the Lake Victoria region; the Blue Nile from Lake Tana in Ethiopia.
  2. Explain why a rainy summer in Ethiopia mattered so much to a farmer in Egypt, more than 1,500 kilometers away.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Ethiopian monsoon rains fed the Blue Nile and the flood; The flood watered Egypt's fields and brought fertile silt; Egypt itself gets almost no rain
    Model answer: Almost no rain falls in Egypt, so farmers depended on the flood. The flood came from monsoon rains in the Ethiopian Highlands, which filled the Blue Nile and carried water and black silt all the way to Egypt's fields.
  3. If you sail from Aswan to Memphis, are you traveling upstream or downstream?
    Answer: downstream
    Memphis is north of Aswan, and the Nile flows north, so you go downstream.

Learn 2: The Black Land and the Red Land

  1. Complete the sentence.
    Answer: kemet / deshret
    Kemet was the dark, silt-covered farmland; Deshret was the desert.
  2. Herodotus called Egypt "the gift of the river." Agree or disagree: was the Nile's wealth a free gift? Use one piece of evidence from the lesson.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Takes a position; Explains the role of the flood; Explains the human work needed (irrigation, planning, measuring)
    Model answer: I partly disagree. The flood brought water and silt for free, but people had to build basin walls, dig canals, measure the flood and plan their planting to turn that gift into food.

Learn 3: Managing the Flood

  1. In which season did farmers plant their crops in the black silt?
    Answer: Peret (Emergence)
    After the flood drained, farmers planted in Peret.
  2. A nilometer reading shows a flood of 16 cubits. If one cubit is about 52 centimeters, about how many meters is that? Round to the nearest tenth.
    Answer: 8.3 m
    16 × 52 = 832 cm = 8.32 m, about 8.3 meters.
  3. Explain why both a very low flood and a very high flood were dangerous, and why measuring the flood helped the government.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Low flood: not enough water for fields, hunger; High flood: destroyed villages, walls and canals; Measuring helped predict harvests, store food and set taxes
    Model answer: A low flood could not reach the higher fields, so crops failed and people went hungry. A very high flood washed away mud-brick villages and canals. Measuring the flood let officials predict the harvest, store extra grain and set fair taxes.

Learn 4: From Villages to a Kingdom

  1. Which crown stood for Upper Egypt, in the south?
    Answer: the White Crown
    The White Crown stood for Upper Egypt; the Red Crown for Lower Egypt.
  2. How many years ago was 3100 BCE, counting to the year 2026?
    Answer: about 5,125 years (5,126 by simple adding)
    3,100 + 2,026 = 5,126, or 5,125 exactly because there is no year 0.
  3. The Narmer Palette was made for a king. What message did it send to people who saw it? Why should a historian be careful using it as evidence?
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Message: the king is powerful, has united the Two Lands, defeated enemies; Caution: made to praise the king (propaganda); may exaggerate or be symbolic
    Model answer: It told people the king was powerful, had defeated his enemies and now ruled both lands. A historian should be careful because it was made to praise the king, so it shows his side and may exaggerate or be symbolic rather than an exact record.

Hands-On

  1. Describe where the dark silt settled in your model and explain how this connects to the names Kemet and Deshret.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Silt settled on the flat floodplain near the channel; Sand away from the channel stayed light; Connects to Black Land and Red Land
    Model answer: The dark soil settled in a strip along both sides of the channel where the water spread out and slowed down. The higher sand stayed light. That is just like Kemet, the black farmland along the river, and Deshret, the red desert beyond it.
  2. Record your three nilometer readings. If 1 cm on your cup stood for 1 cubit, which flood would be best for farming, and why?
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Three readings with units; Chooses a middle reading; Explains too low and too high
    Model answer: My readings were 4 cm, 8 cm and 12 cm. The 8 cm flood would be best: 4 cm would be too low to reach the higher fields, and 12 cm might wash away the basin walls and villages.

Think Deeper

  1. Some people say geography made Egyptian civilization; others say the people did. Write a paragraph arguing which mattered more. Use at least three pieces of evidence from this lesson and answer the other side.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Clear claim; Three accurate pieces of evidence (flood, silt, Ethiopia rains, irrigation, nilometers, unification); Answers the opposing view; Organized with a conclusion
    Model answer: Geography gave the opportunity, but people made Egyptian civilization. The Nile flood, fed by rains in Ethiopia, brought water and black silt to a desert. Yet the flood alone did not feed millions: people built basin walls and canals, measured the flood with nilometers, and planned the farming year around Akhet, Peret and Shemu. Leaders from the south then united the valley around 3100 BCE. Some argue the river did everything, and without the Nile there would be no Kemet. That is true, but other rivers flood too, and not every river valley produced a kingdom that lasted 3,000 years. It took organization, knowledge and cooperation to turn the gift of the river into a civilization.
  2. Today the Aswan High Dam (finished in 1970) stops the yearly flood. Predict one benefit and one problem this might cause for farmers, and explain your reasoning.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: A plausible benefit (no destructive floods, water stored for dry years, electricity, year-round irrigation); A plausible problem (no new silt, need for fertilizer, salt buildup, people displaced); Reasoning connects to the flood's role
    Model answer: One benefit is that farmers can get water all year and no longer fear a destructive flood. One problem is that the silt now stays behind the dam, so fields no longer get new black soil each year, and farmers must buy fertilizer to replace it.

Exit Check

  1. In which direction does the Nile flow?
    Answer: north
    From Central and East Africa north to the Mediterranean.
  2. Complete the sentence.
    Answer: south / north
    Upper means upstream, which is south on the Nile.
  3. What did the ancient Egyptians call their country?
    Answer: Kemet
    Kemet, the Black Land.
  4. Explain in one or two sentences how the flood made farming possible in a desert.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Flood brought water; Left fertile silt
    Model answer: The flood soaked the fields with water and left a layer of rich black silt, so crops could grow even though almost no rain fell.
  5. Who is believed to have united Egypt around 3100 BCE, and from which part of Egypt did he come?
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Narmer; From Upper Egypt (the south), Hierakonpolis
    Model answer: Narmer, a ruler from Upper Egypt in the south, around Hierakonpolis.

Answer key: printable page

  1. Label on a sketch map: the White Nile, the Blue Nile, Khartoum, Aswan, Memphis, the Delta, Upper Egypt and Lower Egypt.
    Answer: White Nile from Lake Victoria; Blue Nile from Ethiopia; they meet at Khartoum; Aswan at the First Cataract; Memphis near the Delta; Upper Egypt in the south, Lower Egypt in the north.
  2. Explain why the south of Egypt is called Upper Egypt.
    Answer: The Nile flows north, so the south is upstream, or up the river.
  3. Name the three seasons of the Egyptian year and what farmers did in each.
    Answer: Akhet: flood, repair tools and build; Peret: plant in the silt; Shemu: harvest and store grain.
  4. A flood reaches 14 cubits. About how many meters is that if a cubit is 52 cm?
    Answer: 14 × 52 = 728 cm, about 7.3 m.
  5. What do the White Crown, the Red Crown and the Double Crown stand for?
    Answer: White: Upper Egypt; Red: Lower Egypt; Double: the two lands united under one king.
  6. Why did Egyptian farmers in the north depend on rain that fell in Ethiopia?
    Answer: The monsoon rains in the Ethiopian Highlands fed the Blue Nile, which caused the flood that watered Egypt's fields.
