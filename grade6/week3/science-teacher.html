Unit 1: Thinking Like a Scientist · Week 3 · Life Science · Teacher Plan

Measuring and Recording Data

Lesson length: 60 minutes live, plus the after-class interactive lesson

Objectives

  • Explain why scientists around the world use the metric system (SI).
  • Convert between metric units by multiplying and dividing by powers of ten.
  • Measure length, volume, mass and temperature with the correct tools and units, including reading a meniscus.
  • Explain the difference between accuracy and precision.
  • Organize measurements in a clear data table with labeled units.

Standards

  • SC.6.N.1.1: Define a problem from the sixth grade curriculum, use appropriate reference materials to support scientific understanding, plan and carry out scientific investigation of various types, such as systematic observations or experiments, identify variables, collect and organize data, interpret data in charts, tables, and graphics, analyze information, make predictions, and defend conclusions.
  • SC.6.N.1.4: Discuss, compare and negotiate methods used, results obtained and explanations among groups of students conducting the same investigation (for a homeschool student: compare results with a parent or a KCE classmate).
  • SC.8.P.8.2 (preview): Differentiate between weight and mass recognizing that weight is the amount of gravitational pull on an object and is distinct from, though proportional to, mass.
  • MA.6 connection: multiplying and dividing by powers of ten (Math Week 3).
  • NGSS Science and Engineering Practices 3 and 5: planning investigations; using mathematics and computational thinking.

Materials

  • The interactive lesson
  • Ruler with millimeters, kitchen scale in grams, measuring cup in milliliters
  • A clear glass and water to show a meniscus on camera
  • Thermometer if available
  • Printable practice page

Vocabulary

  • metric system (SI): The International System of Units, a base-ten measurement system used by scientists worldwide.
  • meter (m): The base unit of length.
  • gram (g) and kilogram (kg): Units of mass. The kilogram is the official SI base unit; 1 kg = 1,000 g.
  • liter (L): A unit of volume. 1 L = 1,000 mL.
  • degrees Celsius (°C): The metric temperature scale. Water freezes at 0 °C and boils at 100 °C at sea level.
  • mass: The amount of matter in an object, measured with a balance or scale.
  • volume: The amount of space something takes up.
  • meniscus: The curved surface of a liquid in a narrow container. Read the volume at the bottom of the curve.
  • accuracy: How close a measurement is to the true value.
  • precision: How close repeated measurements are to each other.

Key questions to ask

  • What unit goes with that number?
  • Are you going up or down the staircase, so will you multiply or divide?
  • Where should your eye be when you read the cylinder?
  • Is that tool giving you accuracy, precision, both or neither?

Common mistakes to watch for

  • Multiplying when converting to a larger unit (should divide).
  • Reading the top of the meniscus.
  • Using mass and weight as if they mean the same thing.
  • Thinking precise measurements must be accurate.

Support (if the student is struggling)

  • Give a printed metric staircase to keep beside the work.
  • Use a place-value chart to show decimal points moving.
  • Practice reading a ruler using a printed enlarged ruler.

Challenge (if the student is ready for more)

  • Ask the student to research the 2019 redefinition of the kilogram, which replaced a metal cylinder kept near Paris with a definition based on a constant of nature.
  • Have the student convert a family recipe from cups and ounces to milliliters and grams.

Background for the teacher

The Mars Climate Orbiter was lost on September 23, 1999. The NASA investigation board found that ground software produced thruster impulse data in pound-force seconds while the navigation software expected newton-seconds. The orbiter's cost is commonly reported as about $125 million (about $327.6 million for the whole Mars Surveyor 98 program, which also included the Mars Polar Lander). Since 2019 the kilogram has been defined using the Planck constant.

Answer key: interactive lesson

Reconnect

  1. A student tests whether the color of a cup changes how fast ice melts in the sun. What is the independent variable?
    Answer: the color of the cup
    The color is what the student changes.
  2. Name two different variables the student must control in that test.
    Answer: For example: the size of the ice cube, the type and size of cup, the amount of sun, the starting temperature
    Everything except cup color must stay the same.
  3. Four trials took 8, 10, 9 and 13 minutes. What is the average?
    Answer: 10 minutes
    8 + 10 + 9 + 13 = 40, and 40 ÷ 4 = 10 minutes.
  4. Write a hypothesis for the cup color test in if, then, because form.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Has if, then and because; Names cup color as the change and melting time as the result; Gives a scientific reason
    Model answer: If I put the ice in a black cup, then it will melt faster than in a white cup, because dark colors absorb more energy from sunlight.
  5. What is the difference between a control group and controlled variables? Use the bean-plant example if it helps.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Control group: the group that gets no change, used for comparison; Controlled variables: conditions kept the same for every group
    Model answer: The control group is the group that gets no change, like the bean plants with plain water, so we can compare the others to it. Controlled variables are the conditions kept the same for every group, like the same pot, soil and sunlight.

Learn 1: Why the Metric System?

  1. Why did the Mars Climate Orbiter fail?
    Answer: Two teams used different units (customary and metric) and nobody converted.
    A unit mismatch sent the spacecraft too close to Mars.
  2. Which tool and unit would you use to measure the mass of an apple? Answer like this: tool, unit.
    Answer: a scale (or balance), grams
    Mass is measured with a scale or balance, in grams.
  3. Why is the metric system easier to convert than inches, feet and yards?
    Answer: Because it is based on powers of ten, so you just move the decimal point.
    12 inches in a foot and 3 feet in a yard do not follow a pattern; metric always uses tens.

Learn 2: Climbing the Metric Staircase

  1. 2,500 mL = ? L
    Answer: 2.5 L
    milli to base is 3 steps up: 2,500 ÷ 1,000 = 2.5 L.
  2. 0.75 kg = ? g
    Answer: 750 g
    kilo to base is 3 steps down: 0.75 × 1,000 = 750 g.
  3. 68 mm = ? cm
    Answer: 6.8 cm
    milli to centi is 1 step up: 68 ÷ 10 = 6.8 cm.
  4. A trail is 4.6 km long. How many meters is that?
    Answer: 4,600
    4.6 × 1,000 = 4,600 m.

Learn 3: Reading Tools Correctly

  1. How long is the leaf on the ruler, in centimeters?
    Answer: 6.4 cm
    6 whole centimeters plus 4 millimeters = 6.4 cm.
  2. Read the graduated cylinder. What is the volume, in mL? (Each small line is 1 mL.)
    Answer: 32 mL
    The bottom of the meniscus sits at the 32 mL line, 2 marks above 30.
  3. An astronaut travels to the Moon. Does her mass change? Does her weight change? Explain both.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Mass stays the same (same amount of matter); Weight decreases (weaker gravity, about 1/6)
    Model answer: Her mass stays the same because she is made of the same amount of matter. Her weight drops to about one sixth because the Moon's gravity is weaker.
  4. A thermometer in a glass of ice water should read about how many degrees Celsius?
    Answer: 0 °C
    Ice water is at the freezing point of water: about 0 °C.

Learn 4: Accuracy, Precision and Data Tables

  1. What is the average dissolving time at 25 °C?
    Answer: 60 s
    62 + 58 + 60 = 180, and 180 ÷ 3 = 60 seconds.
  2. What is the average dissolving time at 50 °C?
    Answer: 31 s
    31 + 29 + 33 = 93, and 93 ÷ 3 = 31 seconds.
  3. What pattern does the data show?
    Answer: As water temperature increases, sugar dissolves faster (in less time).
    98 s at 10 °C, 60 s at 25 °C, 31 s at 50 °C: warmer water, less time.
  4. Your kitchen scale reads 5 g when nothing is on it, every single time. Are its measurements accurate, precise, or both? Type precise, accurate or both.
    Answer: precise (but not accurate)
    It is consistent (precise) but always 5 g off (not accurate). Press the tare button to zero it.

Think Deeper

  1. A classmate writes the result of an experiment as "The plant grew 15." Explain why this is a problem, and connect your answer to the Mars Climate Orbiter.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Explains that a number without a unit cannot be interpreted; Gives possible units (mm, cm) to show the confusion; Connects to the orbiter's unit mismatch; Suggests the correct way to write it
    Model answer: Without a unit, nobody knows whether the plant grew 15 millimeters or 15 centimeters, which is ten times more. The Mars Climate Orbiter was destroyed because two teams used different units and never checked. The classmate should write "The plant grew 15 cm" and label the units in the table heading.
  2. Design the data table you would use for the fair paper towel test from Week 2. Write the column headings with units, and explain why you arranged it that way.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Independent variable (paper type) in the first column; Columns for repeated trials and an average; Units in the headings (mL or g); Explains the arrangement
    Model answer: Columns: Paper type | Trial 1 (mL absorbed) | Trial 2 (mL) | Trial 3 (mL) | Average (mL). The independent variable goes first because it is what I chose to change, the trials go next so I can see how consistent they are, and the average goes last because I calculate it from the trials. Putting mL in the headings means I do not have to write it in every cell.

Exit Check

  1. 5.3 m = ? cm
    Answer: 530 cm
    Base to centi is 2 steps down: 5.3 × 100 = 530 cm.
  2. 1,250 g = ? kg
    Answer: 1.25 kg
    Base to kilo is 3 steps up: 1,250 ÷ 1,000 = 1.25 kg.
  3. 0.4 L = ? mL
    Answer: 400 mL
    0.4 × 1,000 = 400 mL.
  4. What tool and unit would you use to measure 50 milliliters of vinegar precisely? Answer like this: tool, unit.
    Answer: a graduated cylinder, milliliters
    A graduated cylinder measures liquid volume precisely in milliliters.
  5. When you read a graduated cylinder, which part of the meniscus do you read?
    Answer: the bottom of the curve
    For water, read the bottom of the meniscus at eye level.
  6. Explain the difference between accuracy and precision using an example from your own kitchen or yard.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Accuracy = close to the true value; Precision = repeated results close together; A real example
    Model answer: If my bathroom scale always says I weigh 3 pounds less than the doctor's scale, it is precise because it gives the same number every time, but not accurate because the number is wrong.

Answer key: printable page

  1. Convert: 7.2 km = ______ m 350 mL = ______ L 46 mm = ______ cm 0.08 kg = ______ g
    Answer: 7,200 m; 0.35 L; 4.6 cm; 80 g
  2. Name the tool and metric unit for each: the length of a pencil; the mass of a potato; the volume of juice in a glass; the temperature of a pool.
    Answer: ruler, cm; scale, g; measuring cup or graduated cylinder, mL; thermometer, °C
  3. Explain how to read a graduated cylinder correctly.
    Answer: Put your eye level with the liquid and read the bottom of the meniscus.
  4. Explain the difference between mass and weight. Would your mass change on Mars?
    Answer: Mass is the amount of matter; weight is the pull of gravity. Mass would not change on Mars; weight would be less.
  5. A student measures a book three times: 412 g, 413 g, 412 g. The true mass is 450 g. Are the measurements accurate, precise, both or neither? Explain.
    Answer: Precise (close together) but not accurate (far from 450 g).
  6. Draw a data table for testing how the height of a ramp changes how far a toy car rolls. Include headings with units and 3 trials.
    Answer: Ramp height (cm) | Trial 1 distance (cm) | Trial 2 (cm) | Trial 3 (cm) | Average (cm)
