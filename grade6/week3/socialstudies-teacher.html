Unit 1: How We Know the Past · Week 3 · World and American History · Teacher Plan

Human Beginnings in Africa

Lesson length: 60 minutes live, plus the after-class interactive lesson

Objectives

  • Explain how scientists date the deep past using fossils, layers of earth and dating methods.
  • Describe key evidence of early human ancestors and the first stone tools in Africa.
  • Explain why scientists now think Homo sapiens emerged across Africa about 300,000 years ago.
  • Trace how people spread from Africa to every continent.
  • Use genetic evidence to explain why all humans are one family, and why skin color varies.

Standards

  • SS.6.W.2.1: Compare the lifestyles of hunter-gatherers with those of settlers of early agricultural communities (preview for Week 4; today establishes the hunter-gatherer world).
  • SS.6.W.1.4: Describe the methods of historical inquiry and how history relates to the other social sciences.
  • SS.6.W.1.5: Describe the roles of historians and recognize varying historical interpretations (historiography).
  • SS.6.G.4.2: Use maps to trace significant migrations, and analyze their results.
  • C3 Framework D2.His.16.6-8: Organize applicable evidence into a coherent argument about the past.

Materials

  • The interactive lesson
  • A world map or globe
  • Dig box materials (prepared by a family member before the after-class activity)
  • Printable practice page

Vocabulary

  • fossil: The preserved remains or traces of a living thing from long ago.
  • hominin: Humans and our closest extinct relatives that walked upright.
  • Homo sapiens: Our own species. The name means wise human.
  • stratigraphy: The study of layers of rock and soil to learn about the past.
  • law of superposition: In undisturbed layers, the deepest layers are the oldest.
  • migration: The movement of people from one place to another to live.
  • DNA: The material inside our cells that carries the instructions for how living things grow and work, passed from parents to children.
  • melanin: The natural pigment that gives skin its color and protects it from the sun's ultraviolet (UV) rays.
  • symbolic thinking: Using one thing to stand for another, such as patterns, art or beads, which shows complex minds.

Lesson flow

6 minReconnect
Run the Reconnect questions. Have the student find Lake Turkana and Morocco on a real map of Africa.
6 minHook
Ask the student where their own family comes from. Then reveal that every family tree, followed far enough, leads to Africa.
10 minDating the past
Use the layer diagram. Ask what could disturb layers. Explain natural clocks with the hourglass comparison.

Locate each site on the map. Discuss Lucy and the meaning of Dinkinesh, and the 3.3-million-year-old Lomekwi tools.
12 minFirst Homo sapiens
Compare the old East Africa story with the new pan-African evidence. Emphasize that evidence changed the conclusion.
12 minSpreading out and one family
Use the spread timeline. Discuss the 99.9 percent DNA figure and the science of skin color. Make clear that skin color is about sunlight, not ability.
4 minAssign
Assign the dig box, Think Deeper and the Exit Check.

Key questions to ask

  • How can we know about people who left no writing?
  • What could make a layer lie about its age?
  • Why did the Jebel Irhoud dates change the story?
  • If we share 99.9 percent of our DNA, what does that say about the idea of separate races?
  • Why might older textbooks have ignored or shrunk Africa's role?

Common mistakes to watch for

  • Thinking humans evolved from chimpanzees. Humans and chimpanzees share a common ancestor that lived millions of years ago.
  • Thinking Lucy was a human. Her species was an earlier hominin.
  • Thinking skin color reflects ability or that races are separate biological groups.
  • Thinking people moved out of Africa in a single trip; it happened in waves over tens of thousands of years.

Support (if the student is struggling)

  • Give a simple timeline card with four dates: 3.3 million (tools), 3.2 million (Lucy), 300,000 (Homo sapiens), 65,000 (leaving Africa).
  • Use the Africa map to color-code sites by age.

Challenge (if the student is ready for more)

  • Research the Leakey family's discoveries at Olduvai Gorge and Lake Turkana and present one.
  • Investigate the debate over the White Sands footprints and explain what evidence each side uses.

Background for the teacher

Key facts: Lucy (A. afarensis, AL 288-1) was found at Hadar, Ethiopia, in 1974 and dates to about 3.2 million years ago. The Lomekwi 3 tools (Kenya, published 2015) date to about 3.3 million years ago. Jebel Irhoud (Morocco) Homo sapiens fossils were dated to about 300,000 years ago (Hublin et al., Nature, 2017); Florisbad (South Africa) to about 260,000 years; Omo Kibish (Ethiopia) to about 233,000 years (revised in 2022). Blombos Cave engraved ochre dates to about 73,000 to 77,000 years. Dates for reaching Australia (50,000 to 65,000 years) and the Americas (at least 15,000 years; White Sands footprints about 21,000 to 23,000 years) are still being debated, so the lesson gives conservative figures. Any two humans share about 99.9 percent of their DNA.

Sources

Answer key: interactive lesson

Reconnect

  1. Lake Turkana in Kenya is at about 3°N, 36°E. Which two hemispheres is it in?
    Answer: Northern and Eastern
    N is north of the equator; E is east of the prime meridian.
  2. Africa is about 30.4 million km² and the United States about 9.8 million km². About how many times larger is Africa? Round to the nearest whole number.
    Answer: 3 times
    30.4 ÷ 9.8 ≈ 3.1, so about 3.
  3. Which map projection makes Africa look much smaller than it really is, compared with places near the poles?
    Answer: Mercator
    Mercator enlarges places far from the equator.
  4. A stone tool dug up by archaeologists is a primary or secondary source?
    Answer: primary
    It was made and used at the time.
  5. How many years passed from 4800 BCE (Nabta Playa) to 3000 BCE (Stonehenge)?
    Answer: 1,800
    Both BCE: 4800 − 3000 = 1,800 years.
  6. Why do historians corroborate evidence?
    Answer: To confirm it with other sources so they can trust it
    Corroboration checks one source against others.

Learn 1: How Do We Know How Old It Is?

  1. In the diagram, which tool is the oldest? Type only its name.
    Answer: the simple chopping tool (deepest layer)
    It is in the deepest layer, so it is the oldest.
  2. A pottery shard is found above a stone blade in undisturbed layers. Which object is older? Type only its name.
    Answer: the stone blade
    Lower layers are older, so the blade is older than the pottery above it.
  3. Why do scientists say undisturbed layers? Describe something that could mix up the layers and make the rule unreliable.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Names a real disturbance (digging, animals burrowing, earthquakes, floods, burials); Explains that it could put younger objects below older ones
    Model answer: An animal digging a burrow or people digging a grave could push a newer object down into an older layer, so it would look older than it really is.

Learn 2: Our Earliest Ancestors

  1. About how many million years old is Lucy?
    Answer: 3.2 million years
    Lucy is about 3.2 million years old.
  2. What does Lucy's skeleton show about how her species moved?
    Answer: They walked upright on two legs.
    Her hip and leg bones show she walked upright.
  3. In which country were the oldest known stone tools found?
    Answer: Kenya
    At Lomekwi, near Lake Turkana, Kenya, about 3.3 million years old.
  4. What does Lucy's Ethiopian name, Dinkinesh, mean?
    Answer: you are marvelous
    Dinkinesh means "you are marvelous" in Amharic.

Learn 3: The First Homo sapiens

  1. About how old are the Jebel Irhoud fossils, in years?
    Answer: 300,000
    About 300,000 years old, the oldest known Homo sapiens fossils.
  2. How many years older are the Jebel Irhoud fossils than the Omo Kibish fossils (233,000 years)?
    Answer: 67,000
    300,000 − 233,000 = 67,000 years.
  3. Jebel Irhoud is in Morocco, Omo Kibish in Ethiopia and Florisbad in South Africa. What does finding early Homo sapiens in all three places suggest?
    Answer: That Homo sapiens emerged across the whole African continent, not in just one region.
    The sites are thousands of kilometers apart, in the north, east and south of Africa.
  4. What did people at Blombos Cave make that shows symbolic thinking?
    Answer: engraved ochre patterns and shell beads
    Patterns and beads stand for ideas, which shows complex, symbolic minds.

Learn 4: Spreading Across the World, and One Human Family

  1. About what percent of their DNA do any two humans share?
    Answer: 99.9 %
    About 99.9 percent.
  2. Why does Africa have the greatest human genetic diversity?
    Answer: Because humans have lived there the longest
    Populations that have existed longer have had more time to develop genetic variety.
  3. How did the first people reach Australia?
    Answer: By crossing open water in boats
    Australia was never connected to Asia by land during this time, so people needed boats.
  4. Explain, in two or three sentences, why human skin color varies, and what skin color does and does not tell us about a person.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Explains UV protection near the equator (melanin); Explains vitamin D and lighter skin far from the equator; States that skin color says nothing about intelligence, ability or worth
    Model answer: Skin color varies because of sunlight. People whose ancestors lived near the equator have more melanin, which protects against strong UV rays, while lighter skin helped people farther from the equator make vitamin D from weaker sunlight. Skin color tells us about where our ancestors lived, not about anyone's intelligence, character or worth.

Hands-On

  1. Record each layer from top to bottom: material, depth in cm and artifact. Then list the artifacts from oldest to youngest.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Completes every step of the activity; Includes specific details, numbers or observations from the activity; Explains thinking in complete sentences
  2. Did your order match the secret list? If something seemed out of place, what could explain it? How is your dig like the work at Olduvai Gorge?
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Completes every step of the activity; Includes specific details, numbers or observations from the activity; Explains thinking in complete sentences
    Model answer: Yes, my order matched, except the bottle cap was in a deeper layer than it should have been. My mom had pushed it down like a burrow, so the layers were disturbed there. Like scientists at Olduvai Gorge, I dug one layer at a time, measured the depth and used superposition to decide which objects were older.

Think Deeper

  1. Before 2017, many textbooks said our species began about 200,000 years ago in East Africa. Explain how the Jebel Irhoud discovery changed that story, and why changing conclusions is a strength of science and history, not a weakness.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Explains the new date (300,000 years) and location (Morocco, North Africa); Explains the new idea of a pan-African origin; Explains why updating conclusions with new evidence is a strength; Connects to corroboration or evidence from Week 1
    Model answer: The Jebel Irhoud fossils in Morocco turned out to be about 300,000 years old, about 100,000 years older than the East African fossils textbooks used. Because early Homo sapiens fossils were also found in South Africa, scientists now think our species emerged across the whole continent. Changing the story is a strength, because it shows scientists follow the evidence instead of protecting old ideas, just as historians corroborate and correct sources.
  2. Why does it matter that every human being shares African ancestors and 99.9 percent of their DNA? Explain what this evidence means for how people should think about race and about Africa's place in world history.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Uses the 99.9 percent DNA and African origin evidence; Explains that humans are one family and that races are not separate biological groups; Explains that Africa is central to world history, not on its edge; Clear claim and reasoning
    Model answer: If all humans share African ancestors and 99.9 percent of our DNA, then we are one family, and the differences people use to divide each other are tiny. Africa is not on the edge of world history; it is where human history begins. Knowing this should make people question anyone who claims one group is naturally better than another.

Exit Check

  1. In undisturbed layers, where are the oldest objects found, and why? Name one thing that could disturb the layers.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Bottom; Layers pile up over time; Names a real disturbance (burrowing animals, digging, floods, earthquakes)
    Model answer: At the bottom, because layers pile up on top of each other over time. A burrowing animal or a flood could mix the layers.
  2. In what country were Lucy's bones found?
    Answer: Ethiopia
    At Hadar, Ethiopia, in 1974.
  3. Where were the oldest known Homo sapiens fossils, about 300,000 years old, found? Name the site or the country.
    Answer: Jebel Irhoud, Morocco
    Jebel Irhoud, Morocco.
  4. How many years passed between the oldest stone tools (3,300,000 years ago) and Lucy (3,200,000 years ago)?
    Answer: 100,000
    3,300,000 − 3,200,000 = 100,000 years. The tools are older than Lucy.
  5. What pigment protects skin from the sun's UV rays?
    Answer: melanin
    Melanin gives skin its color and protects it.
  6. Name one piece of evidence that early Homo sapiens had symbolic thinking.
    Answer: engraved ochre or shell beads from Blombos Cave
    Patterns and beads represent ideas.

Answer key: printable page

  1. Explain the law of superposition and one thing that could make it unreliable.
    Answer: In undisturbed layers the deepest is oldest; digging, burrowing animals, floods or earthquakes can mix layers.
  2. Put in order from oldest to youngest: Homo sapiens at Jebel Irhoud; Lomekwi stone tools; Lucy; Blombos Cave beads.
    Answer: Lomekwi tools (3.3 million), Lucy (3.2 million), Jebel Irhoud (300,000), Blombos beads (over 70,000)
  3. How many years older is Lucy than the Jebel Irhoud fossils? (3,200,000 and 300,000 years) Show your work.
    Answer: 3,200,000 − 300,000 = 2,900,000 years
  4. Why do scientists now think Homo sapiens emerged across all of Africa, not in one region?
    Answer: Early Homo sapiens fossils have been found far apart, in Morocco, Ethiopia and South Africa.
  5. Explain why human skin color varies.
    Answer: More melanin protects against strong UV near the equator; lighter skin helps make vitamin D where sunlight is weaker.
  6. What does the fact that all humans share about 99.9 percent of their DNA tell us?
    Answer: All humans are one closely related family; the differences between groups are very small.
