Grade 6 · Week 3 · Parent Guide

Week 3: Big Ideas and Deep Time

This week your child finds the message inside a famous poem, multiplies decimals to check a paycheck, measures like a scientist, and traces the human story back 300,000 years to Africa.

How scoring works: each lesson scores itself. Your child gets two tries per question: full points on the first try, half points on the second, then the answer and explanation appear. A score of 85 or higher means the lesson is mastered. Written answers go to Jonathan, who scores them with a rubric.

Time this week: About 60 minutes of live lessons per subject, plus 30 to 45 minutes of after-class work per subject.

Gather this week: 7 index cards and a staircase (or cards on the floor); a kitchen scale and three pieces of produce; a ruler with centimeters, a measuring cup with milliliter marks, five household objects and a thermometer if you have one; a shoebox or container, three or four layering materials (damp sand or soil, rice or oatmeal), four small objects to bury and a ruler.

Heads up: For Social Studies, please build the dig box yourself without letting your child see the order of the layers, and write down the order secretly. Personal narrative project: your child now has a story, a story mountain and an opening paragraph; next week they draft the whole story.

ELA: Theme in Story and Poem

Your child reads Langston Hughes's 1922 poem "Mother to Son," learns the difference between a topic and a theme, explores how Hughes compares life to a staircase, and discusses why he wrote in dialect. They write their first full theme paragraph with quotations.

Ask at dinner: "What would your own staircase look like? What have been the tacks and splinters, and the landings?"

Try it together: Listen to your child recite the poem from the top of your stairs. Ask what the mother wants for her son.

Signs it's clicking: They write a theme as a full sentence about life, not one word, and support it with exact quotes. Signs they need help: They summarize the poem instead of explaining its message, or call dialect bad grammar. Remind them Hughes chose it on purpose to honor how people really spoke.

Math: Multiplying Decimals

Your child models decimal multiplication on a grid, uses the standard algorithm, multiplies by powers of ten, and calculates a teenager's paycheck from an hourly wage, then figures out why his own total is one cent off from the paycheck.

Ask at dinner: "If you earned $15.75 an hour for 6.5 hours, about how much would you make?"

Try it together: At the store or with produce at home, have your child find the cost of fruit priced by the pound.

Signs it's clicking: They predict whether a product will be bigger or smaller before they calculate. Signs they need help: They line up decimal points as if adding, or believe multiplying always makes a number bigger.

Life Science: Measuring and Recording Data

Your child learns why scientists use the metric system (and how a unit mix-up cost NASA a $125 million Mars spacecraft), converts metric units, reads rulers and graduated cylinders, and learns the difference between accuracy and precision.

Ask at dinner: "What unit goes with that number?"

Try it together: Do the Kitchen Metric Lab together: estimate, then measure lengths in centimeters, masses in grams and volumes in milliliters.

Signs it's clicking: They always include units and know whether to multiply or divide when converting. Signs they need help: They multiply when converting to a larger unit. A printed metric staircase helps.

World and American History: Human Beginnings in Africa

Your child learns how scientists date the deep past, meets Lucy (Dinkinesh), sees the 3.3-million-year-old Lomekwi stone tools, learns that our species emerged across Africa about 300,000 years ago, and uses DNA evidence to understand that all humans are one family and why skin color varies.

Ask at dinner: "If everyone's ancestors came from Africa, what does that mean about the idea of separate races?"

Try it together: Build the dig box secretly, then watch your child dig layer by layer and put the artifacts in order.

Signs it's clicking: They explain that deeper layers are usually older and that new evidence can change scientific conclusions. Signs they need help: They think humans came from chimpanzees. Explain that humans and chimpanzees share a very distant common ancestor.
