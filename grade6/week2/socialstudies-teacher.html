Unit 1: How We Know the Past · Week 2 · World and American History · Teacher Plan

Maps, Projections and the True Size of the World

Lesson length: 60 minutes live, plus the after-class interactive lesson

Objectives

  • Locate places using latitude and longitude, and name the hemispheres.
  • Explain why every flat map of a round Earth distorts size, shape, distance or direction.
  • Compare the Mercator, Gall-Peters, Robinson and Winkel Tripel projections and what each is good for.
  • Use real land-area data to describe the true size of Africa.
  • Analyze a map as a source with a point of view.

Standards

  • SS.6.G.1.1: Use latitude and longitude coordinates to understand the relationship between people and places on the Earth.
  • SS.6.G.1.2: Analyze the purposes of map projections (political, physical, special purpose) and explain the applications of various types of maps.
  • SS.6.G.1.4: Utilize tools geographers use to study the world.
  • SS.6.W.1.3: Interpret primary and secondary sources.
  • C3 Framework D2.Geo.2.6-8: Use maps, satellite images, photographs and other representations to explain relationships between the locations of places and regions and changes in their environmental characteristics.

Materials

  • The interactive lesson
  • A globe if you have one
  • An orange and a permanent marker
  • Optional: thetruesize.com, a free website that lets students drag countries to compare true sizes
  • Printable practice page

Vocabulary

  • latitude: Imaginary lines that run east and west around Earth, measuring distance north or south of the equator in degrees.
  • longitude: Imaginary lines that run from pole to pole, measuring distance east or west of the prime meridian in degrees.
  • equator: The line of 0° latitude, dividing Earth into the Northern and Southern Hemispheres.
  • prime meridian: The line of 0° longitude, which runs through Greenwich, England, dividing the Eastern and Western Hemispheres.
  • hemisphere: Half of Earth: Northern, Southern, Eastern or Western.
  • map projection: A method of showing the round Earth on a flat surface. Every projection distorts something.
  • distortion: A change in the true size, shape, distance or direction of places on a map.
  • Mercator projection: A 1569 map made for sailors that keeps directions and local shapes true but greatly enlarges places near the poles.
  • equal-area projection: A projection, such as Gall-Peters, that shows every place at its true relative size but stretches shapes.

Lesson flow

6 minReconnect
Run the Week 1 Reconnect questions.
6 minHook
Put a Mercator world map on screen. Ask the student to guess how many Greenlands fit in Africa before revealing about 14.
12 minLatitude and longitude
Use the grid. Practice reading coordinates for Lakeland, Cairo, Timbuktu and Nairobi. Emphasize latitude first.
10 minProjections
Compare the four projections. If possible, show images of each side by side. Ask what each is good and bad for.
10 minTrue size
Work the area problems together. If online, have the student drag Greenland to the equator on thetruesize.com and watch it shrink.
10 minMaps have a point of view
Show a south-up map. Ask how it feels and why. Connect to bias from Week 1.
6 minOrange peel live
Do the orange peel together on camera, or start it and let the student finish after class.

Key questions to ask

  • Who made this map, when and why?
  • What does this projection keep true, and what does it give up?
  • Why is the Mercator map still used for online street maps?
  • What might a student in Nigeria or Brazil think of a map centered on Europe?
  • What would change if the map put the Pacific Ocean in the center?

Common mistakes to watch for

  • Thinking Mercator is simply wrong. It is accurate for direction and was built for navigation.
  • Mixing up latitude and longitude. Latitude lines are flat like the rungs of a ladder (latitude, ladder).
  • Thinking north-up is a scientific fact.
  • Believing Africa is a country rather than a continent of 54 countries.

Support (if the student is struggling)

  • Use a globe with the orange-peel activity to make the distortion concrete.
  • Provide a coordinate practice card with four cities.
  • Memory trick: latitude rungs are flat like a ladder; longitude lines are long, from pole to pole.

Challenge (if the student is ready for more)

  • Have the student find the coordinates of their own home and of a place where their family has roots.
  • Ask the student to research the AuthaGraph projection (Japan, 2016) and compare it with Winkel Tripel.

Background for the teacher

Land areas (approximate): Africa 30.37 million km², United States 9.83 million km² (including water), China 9.6 million km², India 3.29 million km², Greenland 2.17 million km². Gerardus Mercator published his projection in 1569. Arthur Robinson's projection dates to 1963; National Geographic used it from 1988 and adopted Winkel Tripel in 1998. Stuart McArthur's Universal Corrective Map of the World (south-up) was published in 1979. Al-Idrisi completed his Tabula Rogeriana for King Roger II of Sicily in 1154; the Kangnido (Honil Gangni Yeokdae Gukdo Jido) was made in Korea in 1402. The 1884 International Meridian Conference chose Greenwich as the prime meridian. Africa has 54 United Nations member states; the African Union has 55 members because it includes the Sahrawi Arab Democratic Republic. Boston Public Schools began introducing Peters projection maps in 2017.

Answer key: interactive lesson

Reconnect

  1. A map drawn by a Spanish sailor in 1529. Primary or secondary source?
    Answer: primary
    It was created at the time by someone who was there.
  2. What do we call West African keepers of oral history?
    Answer: griots
    Griots preserve history through speech and song.
  3. How many years passed from 2600 BCE to 1300 BCE?
    Answer: 1,300
    Both BCE: 2600 − 1300 = 1,300 years.
  4. In what century was the year 1619?
    Answer: 17th century
    The 1600s are the 17th century.
  5. In Week 1, which word revealed bias in the Spanish account of Florida?
    Answer: discovered
    "Discovered" ignored the Calusa and other nations who already lived there.
  6. What does it mean to corroborate evidence?
    Answer: To confirm it with another source that agrees
    Corroborating means confirming with an independent source.

Learn 1: Latitude and Longitude

  1. Point A on the grid is a city at about 28°N, 82°W. Which two hemispheres is it in? Type both names.
    Answer: Northern and Western
    N means north of the equator; W means west of the prime meridian.
  2. Use the coordinates to name the city at each point: Cairo (30°N, 31°E), Timbuktu (17°N, 3°W) or Nairobi (1°S, 37°E).
    Answer: B = cairo, C = timbuktu, D = nairobi
    B is north and east (Cairo). C is just west of the prime meridian and north of the equator (Timbuktu). D is just south of the equator and east (Nairobi).
  3. What is the latitude of the equator, in degrees?
    Answer: 0 degrees
    The equator is 0° latitude.
  4. Timbuktu, in Mali, is at about 17°N, 3°W. In which hemispheres is it located?
    Answer: Northern and Western
    North of the equator and just west of the prime meridian. Much of West Africa is in the Western Hemisphere.

Learn 2: Why Every Flat Map Is Wrong Somewhere

  1. What four things can a flat map distort? Name all four.
    Answer: size (area), shape, distance and direction
    Every projection distorts at least one of size, shape, distance and direction.
  2. What was the Mercator projection originally designed for?
    Answer: Navigation by sailors
    Straight lines on it are constant compass directions, which helped sailors.
  3. Which projection would you use to compare the true sizes of Brazil and Alaska?
    Answer: Gall-Peters (an equal-area projection)
    An equal-area projection shows true relative size.
  4. Why does Greenland look so large on the Mercator map?
    Answer: It is near the North Pole, and Mercator stretches places more the farther they are from the equator.
    Mercator enlarges areas more and more toward the poles.

Learn 3: The True Size of Africa

  1. What is the total area of the United States, China and India together, in millions of square kilometers?
    Answer: 22.7 million km²
    9.8 + 9.6 + 3.3 = 22.7 million km² (a decimal skill from Math Week 2).
  2. After fitting those three countries inside Africa, how much area is left over?
    Answer: 7.7 million km²
    30.4 − 22.7 = 7.7 million km².
  3. About how many times larger than Greenland is Africa? Divide and round to the nearest whole number.
    Answer: 14 times
    30.4 ÷ 2.2 ≈ 13.8, which rounds to 14.

Learn 4: Maps Have a Point of View

  1. Give one piece of evidence from this lesson that putting north at the top is a tradition, not a scientific requirement.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Uses evidence such as al-Idrisi's south-up map, McArthur's map, or that space has no up
    Model answer: In 1154 al-Idrisi drew his world map with south at the top, so north-up was a choice later mapmakers made, not a rule of science.
  2. In what year did Boston Public Schools begin adding Gall-Peters maps to classrooms?
    Answer: 2017
    2017, to show students the true relative sizes of continents.
  3. Using what you learned about bias in Week 1, explain how a map can show a point of view. Give a specific example.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Explains that mapmakers make choices (projection, center, orientation, labels); Gives a specific example from the lesson; Connects to bias or point of view
    Model answer: A mapmaker chooses what to put in the center and which projection to use. The Kangnido map put Korea and China at the center and made them large, and many European maps put Europe in the center and used Mercator, which shrinks Africa. Those choices make some places look more important, just as a word like discovered shows a writer's bias.

Hands-On

  1. Describe what happened when you flattened the peel. Where did it tear or stretch the most? Measure your equator line again on the flattened peel and compare it with your first measurement. How does this explain why the Mercator map enlarges Greenland?
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Completes every step of the activity; Includes specific details, numbers or observations from the activity; Explains thinking in complete sentences

Think Deeper

  1. Your homeschool co-op has money for one large world map for its classroom. Which projection should it buy: Mercator, Gall-Peters or Winkel Tripel? Make a claim and support it with at least two pieces of evidence from this lesson. Then name one weakness of your choice.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Clear claim naming one projection; At least two pieces of evidence from the lesson (data, purpose of the projection, distortion); Acknowledges a weakness of the chosen projection; Considers what students should learn from the map
    Model answer: The co-op should buy a Winkel Tripel map. It has much less size distortion than Mercator, so students would see that Africa is about 14 times larger than Greenland, and it keeps shapes looking more natural than Gall-Peters. National Geographic has used it since 1998 for world maps. Its weakness is that nothing on it is perfectly accurate, so the co-op should keep a globe nearby.
  2. How might growing up with maps that shrink Africa affect the way people think about Africa and its history? Connect your answer to the Week 1 idea that sources have a point of view.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Explains a possible effect on how people view Africa's importance; Connects to point of view and bias; Uses the true-size data as evidence
    Model answer: If Africa always looks small on the wall, people may think it is less important than Europe or North America, even though it is larger than the United States, China and India combined. Like the word discovered in Week 1, a map can carry a point of view without saying it out loud, so students should ask who made the map and why.

Exit Check

  1. What is the name of the line of 0° latitude?
    Answer: equator
    The equator divides the Northern and Southern Hemispheres.
  2. What is the name of the line of 0° longitude?
    Answer: prime meridian
    The prime meridian divides the Eastern and Western Hemispheres.
  3. Nairobi, Kenya is at about 1°S, 37°E. Which two hemispheres is it in?
    Answer: Southern and Eastern
    S means south of the equator; E means east of the prime meridian.
  4. Which projection keeps compass directions true but greatly enlarges places near the poles?
    Answer: Mercator
    Mercator was built for navigation.
  5. Africa is about 30.4 million km² and India is about 3.3 million km². About how many times larger is Africa? Round to the nearest whole number.
    Answer: 9 times
    30.4 ÷ 3.3 ≈ 9.2, which rounds to 9.
  6. Why can't a flat map show the whole Earth perfectly?
    Answer: Because Earth is a sphere, and flattening a curved surface always stretches or tears it.
    Like the orange peel, a sphere cannot lie flat without distortion.

Answer key: printable page

  1. Explain the difference between latitude and longitude, and name the 0° line of each.
    Answer: Latitude measures north and south of the equator (0°); longitude measures east and west of the prime meridian (0°).
  2. Write the hemispheres for each place: Cairo (30°N, 31°E) ________ Lakeland (28°N, 82°W) ________ Lima, Peru (12°S, 77°W) ________
    Answer: Northern and Eastern; Northern and Western; Southern and Western
  3. Name one thing the Mercator projection keeps true and one thing it distorts.
    Answer: Keeps direction (and local shape) true; distorts size near the poles.
  4. Africa is about 30.4 million km². The United States is about 9.8 million km². About how many United States would fit inside Africa? Show your work.
    Answer: 30.4 ÷ 9.8 ≈ 3.1, so about 3
  5. Why did your orange peel tear and stretch when you tried to flatten it? What does that show about flat maps?
    Answer: A curved surface cannot lie flat without stretching or tearing; every flat map distorts something.
  6. Explain how a world map can have a point of view. Give one example.
    Answer: Mapmakers choose the projection, center and orientation; for example, Mercator enlarges Europe and North America and shrinks Africa, or maps put Europe in the center.
