Unit 1: Thinking Like a Scientist · Week 2 · Life Science · Teacher Plan

Designing a Fair Test: Variables

Lesson length: 60 minutes live, plus the after-class interactive lesson

Objectives

  • Identify the independent, dependent and controlled variables in an investigation.
  • Explain why a fair test changes only one variable at a time and uses a control group.
  • Write a hypothesis in if, then, because form.
  • Explain why scientists repeat trials and average the results.
  • Design and carry out a fair test at home.

Standards

  • SC.6.N.1.1: Define a problem from the sixth grade curriculum, use appropriate reference materials to support scientific understanding, plan and carry out scientific investigation of various types, such as systematic observations or experiments, identify variables, collect and organize data, interpret data in charts, tables, and graphics, analyze information, make predictions, and defend conclusions.
  • SC.6.N.1.2: Explain why scientific investigations should be replicable.
  • MA.6 connection: finding the mean of a data set (formally taught in Math Unit 6).
  • NGSS Science and Engineering Practice 3: planning and carrying out investigations.

Materials

  • The interactive lesson
  • Paper towels, napkins, three identical cups, a measuring cup in milliliters, a timer
  • Printable practice page

Vocabulary

  • variable: Any factor that can change in an investigation.
  • independent variable: The one variable the scientist changes on purpose.
  • dependent variable: The variable that is measured; it may change because of the independent variable.
  • controlled variables: Everything kept the same so the test is fair (also called constants).
  • control group: The group that does not get the change being tested, used for comparison.
  • fair test: An investigation in which only one variable is changed at a time.
  • hypothesis: A testable prediction, often written as If..., then..., because...
  • trial: One run of an experiment. Scientists repeat trials to make results reliable.
  • data: Facts and measurements collected during an investigation.

Lesson flow

6 minReconnect
Run the Week 1 Reconnect questions. The Turner question bridges to independent variables.
6 minHook
Read Jaylen's test. Ask the student to list everything Jaylen changed. Count them together.
12 minVariables
Use the sugar diagram. Then have the student label variables in three quick examples you invent.
10 minControl groups and trials
Work the Brand A and Brand B averages. Discuss why Brand B's results vary and what one trial would have told us.
8 minHypothesis
Model one if, then, because hypothesis. The student writes one for the paper towel test.
8 minReal-world fair tests
Read about citrus greening. Ask: why would a citrus scientist need hundreds of trees instead of two?
10 minStart the paper towel test live
If materials are ready, do Trial 1 together on camera so the student sees the procedure, then they finish the trials after class.

Key questions to ask

  • What exactly are you changing?
  • What will you measure, and with what tool?
  • What could accidentally change that you need to control?
  • How many trials will you run, and why?
  • What would it mean if your hypothesis turns out wrong?

Common mistakes to watch for

  • Changing more than one variable at a time.
  • Confusing the control group with controlled variables (a group versus things kept the same).
  • Thinking a wrong hypothesis means the experiment failed.
  • Thinking one trial is enough.

Support (if the student is struggling)

  • Use a three-column variables organizer: I change / I measure / I keep the same.
  • Provide sentence frames for the hypothesis.
  • Reduce the hands-on test to two kinds of paper with three trials each.

Challenge (if the student is ready for more)

  • Have the student graph their paper towel averages as a bar graph (preview of Week 4).
  • Ask the student to research one real citrus greening treatment being tested and identify its variables.

Background for the teacher

Citrus greening (huanglongbing, or HLB) was first detected in Florida in 2005. It is caused by bacteria spread by the Asian citrus psyllid and has sharply reduced Florida's orange production. The University of Florida's Institute of Food and Agricultural Sciences (UF/IFAS) runs many controlled field trials of possible treatments.

Sources

  • USDA Animal and Plant Health Inspection Service: Citrus greening.

Answer key: interactive lesson

Reconnect

  1. "The plant grew 4 centimeters in a week." Observation or inference?
    Answer: observation
    It was measured directly.
  2. "The plant grew because it had more sunlight" is an inference. Write one observation that could support it.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: A direct observation or measurement; Connected to sunlight or growth
    Model answer: The plant by the window grew 6 cm in two weeks, while the plant in the hallway grew 2 cm.
  3. "The water is cloudy." Qualitative or quantitative?
    Answer: qualitative
    It describes a quality with no number.
  4. Is this testable? Do heavier paper airplanes fly shorter distances? Type yes or no.
    Answer: yes
    You can change the plane's mass and measure the distance it flies.
  5. A testable question names what you change and what you ___.
    Answer: measure
    Change one thing, measure the result.
  6. What did Charles Henry Turner change in his honeybee experiments?
    Answer: The color of the cards the honey dishes sat on
    He changed the card colors and observed which ones the bees chose. Today you will learn the name for that kind of variable.

Learn 1: Three Kinds of Variables

  1. In a fair version of Jaylen's test, what should the independent variable be?
    Answer: the brand (type) of paper towel
    The question is about brands, so brand is the only thing that should change.
  2. What should Jaylen measure (the dependent variable)?
    Answer: the amount of water each towel absorbs
    The question asks how much each towel absorbs.
  3. Name two variables Jaylen must keep the same.
    Answer: For example: the amount of water, the soaking time, the container, and the size of the towel
    Water amount, soaking time, container and sheet size must all be controlled.
  4. A student tests whether the height a ball is dropped from changes how high it bounces. What is the dependent variable?
    Answer: how high the ball bounces
    Bounce height is measured; drop height is changed.

Learn 2: Control Groups and Repeated Trials

  1. What is the average for Brand A?
    Answer: 42 mL
    42 + 45 + 39 = 126, and 126 ÷ 3 = 42 mL.
  2. What is the average for Brand B?
    Answer: 43.33 (about 43.3)
    38 + 51 + 41 = 130, and 130 ÷ 3 = 43.33 mL.
  3. Look at Brand B's trials. Why is it a good thing the student did not stop after Trial 1?
    Answer: Trial 1 alone (38 mL) would have made Brand B look worse; the trials varied, so one result could be a fluke.
    Brand B's results ranged from 38 to 51. One trial could have led to the wrong conclusion.
  4. In the bean plant test, which group is the control group?
    Answer: the plants that get plain water and no plant food
    The control group gets no change, so it is the plants without plant food.

Learn 3: Writing a Hypothesis

  1. Complete the hypothesis for the ball experiment.
    Answer: higher / higher
    If I drop it from a higher height, then it will bounce higher.
  2. Write your own hypothesis for the paper towel test in if, then, because form.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Has all three parts: if, then, because; Names the paper type as the change and water absorbed as the result; Gives a reason
    Model answer: If I test the thicker, more expensive brand, then it will absorb more water, because thicker towels have more fibers and spaces to hold water.
  3. Jaylen redoes his test fairly, and the results show his hypothesis was wrong: the store brand absorbed more. Did his experiment fail? Type yes or no.
    Answer: no
    A fair test that disproves a hypothesis still teaches something true. It saved his family money, too.

Learn 4: Fair Tests in the Real World

  1. In a citrus greening treatment test, what is the independent variable?
    Answer: whether the tree gets the treatment
    Scientists change whether the trees get the treatment.
  2. Name a dependent variable scientists could measure in that test.
    Answer: For example: number or size of oranges, or how many trees stay healthy
    They could measure fruit yield, fruit size or tree health.
  3. Why do scientists keep the soil, water and fertilizer the same for both groups of trees?
    Answer: So any difference can only be caused by the treatment
    Controlled variables make sure the treatment is the only difference between the groups.

Think Deeper

  1. Kayla wants to know whether music helps plants grow. She puts one plant by a sunny window with music playing and another plant in a dark closet with no music. After two weeks the music plant is taller, so she says music helps plants grow. Explain what is wrong with her experiment and redesign it as a fair test.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Identifies that two variables changed (music and light); Explains why the conclusion cannot be trusted; Redesign changes only music; Lists controlled variables (light, water, soil, plant type, pot size); Includes a control group and repeated trials or several plants
    Model answer: Kayla changed two things at once: music and light. The window plant got sunlight and the closet plant got none, so the light could explain the growth. A fair test would put several plants of the same type in the same sunny room, with the same soil, pot and water, and play music for only half of them. She should measure height over several weeks and compare the averages.
  2. Go back to the testable question you wrote in Week 1 about your home or neighborhood. Write a full plan: hypothesis, independent variable, dependent variable, at least three controlled variables, control group, and number of trials.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Hypothesis in if, then, because form; Correct independent and dependent variables; Three or more sensible controlled variables; Identifies a control group; Plans repeated trials
    Model answer: (Example built on the question: Does the brand of bird seed change how many birds visit the feeder?) Hypothesis: If I use seed with sunflower hearts, then more birds will visit in one hour, because many birds prefer sunflower seeds. Independent: brand of seed. Dependent: number of birds that land in one hour. Controlled: same feeder, location, time of day, amount of seed. Control group: the seed we usually buy. Trials: five mornings for each seed.

Exit Check

  1. A student tests whether the amount of salt changes how fast ice melts. What is the independent variable?
    Answer: the amount of salt
    The amount of salt is what the student changes.
  2. In that test, what is the dependent variable?
    Answer: how long the ice takes to melt
    Melting time is measured.
  3. Name one controlled variable for the salt and ice test.
    Answer: For example: size of the ice cubes, room temperature or type of salt
    These must stay the same so only the salt amount changes.
  4. Three trials gave melting times of 12, 15 and 18 minutes. What is the average?
    Answer: 15 minutes
    12 + 15 + 18 = 45, and 45 ÷ 3 = 15 minutes.
  5. What is the purpose of a control group?
    Answer: To give a group with no change to compare against
    The control group shows what happens without the change being tested.
  6. What three words begin the three parts of a hypothesis in the form you learned?
    Answer: if, then, because
    If (change), then (result), because (reason).

Answer key: printable page

  1. Marisol tests whether the type of soil changes how tall bean plants grow in 3 weeks. Name the independent variable, the dependent variable and two controlled variables.
    Answer: Independent: type of soil. Dependent: plant height after 3 weeks. Controlled: amount of water, sunlight, type of bean, pot size, temperature.
  2. Write a hypothesis for Marisol's investigation in if, then, because form.
    Answer: For example: If I use potting soil, then the beans will grow taller, because potting soil has more nutrients.
  3. Three trials of a paper airplane flew 4.2 m, 3.9 m and 4.5 m. Find the average distance.
    Answer: (4.2 + 3.9 + 4.5) ÷ 3 = 12.6 ÷ 3 = 4.2 m
  4. Why should Marisol test several bean plants in each soil type instead of just one?
    Answer: One plant could be unusual; several plants make the results reliable.
  5. Malik tests a new sports drink. He gives it to his friends before a race and they run fast. What is missing from his experiment?
    Answer: A control group of runners who drink water (or no sports drink), plus controlled variables and repeated trials.
  6. Explain the difference between a control group and controlled variables.
    Answer: A control group is the group that gets no change, for comparison. Controlled variables are the factors kept the same for every group.
