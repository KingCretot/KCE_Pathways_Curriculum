Grade 6 · Week 2 · Parent Guide

Week 2: Looking Closer

This week every subject asks your child to look more carefully: at decimal places, at who is telling a story, at what makes an experiment fair, and at how maps can mislead.

How scoring works: each lesson scores itself. Your child gets two tries per question: full points on the first try, half points on the second, then the answer and explanation appear. A score of 85 or higher means the lesson is mastered. Written answers go to Jonathan, who scores them with a rubric.

Time this week: About 60 minutes of live lessons per subject, plus 30 to 45 minutes of after-class work per subject.

Gather this week: coins and bills (real or play money) and 5 household items to price; a phone or camera; two brands of paper towel, a paper napkin, a kitchen scale that shows grams, a bowl and a timer; an orange, a permanent marker and a paper plate.

Heads up: The paper towel test takes about 30 minutes and gets a little wet; a tray makes cleanup easy. Permanent marker and orange juice can stain, so cover the table for the orange activity. Personal narrative project: by the end of this week your child should have chosen a true story and built a story mountain (Week 1), and should now be thinking about which person in that story to retell it as.

ELA: Character and Point of View

Your child reads Moving Day, one moment told by two different kids, and learns first person, third person limited and third person omniscient. They study how writers reveal character through speech, thoughts, effect on others, actions and looks, and they rewrite a scene from a new point of view.

Ask at dinner: "If your brother or sister told the story of today, how would it be different from yours?"

Try it together: Help with the photo walk: photograph the same spot from the floor, from eye level and from above, and talk about what each view hides.

Signs it's clicking: They can explain why the reader knows something Priya does not. Signs they need help: They decide point of view from pronouns inside quotation marks. Remind them to look at the narration, not the dialogue.

Math: Adding and Subtracting Decimals

Your child compares and orders decimals, adds and subtracts them by lining up decimal points, checks a real receipt, and learns how cashiers count up to make change.

Ask at dinner: "Which is more, 0.5 or 0.45? How do you know?"

Try it together: Play store with real coins. Let your child be the cashier and count up your change out loud. Next time you shop, have them check the receipt.

Signs it's clicking: They write 8.6 as 8.60 without being told, and they estimate before they calculate. Signs they need help: They think longer decimals are bigger, or they line up the last digits instead of the decimal points. Coins make the idea concrete: 50 cents beats 45 cents.

Life Science: Designing a Fair Test

Your child learns independent, dependent and controlled variables, control groups, repeated trials and averages, and writes hypotheses. They see how Florida scientists test treatments for citrus greening, then run their own fair paper towel test.

Ask at dinner: "What are you changing, what are you measuring, and what are you keeping the same?"

Try it together: Run the paper towel test together. Let your child be in charge of keeping every condition the same.

Signs it's clicking: They spot when two things changed at once and say the test is not fair. Signs they need help: They mix up the control group (the group with no change) and controlled variables (what stays the same).

World and American History: Maps and the True Size of the World

Your child learns latitude and longitude, discovers that every flat map distorts something, compares map projections, and uses real data to show that Africa is about 14 times larger than Greenland, even though many classroom maps make them look similar.

Ask at dinner: "Who made this map, and what did they choose to make big or put in the center?"

Try it together: Do the orange peel activity together, and if you are online, try thetruesize.com to drag countries around and see their true size.

Signs it's clicking: They can explain why the Mercator map is useful for sailing but misleading for comparing sizes. Signs they need help: They mix up latitude and longitude. Latitude lines are flat like the rungs of a ladder.
