Unit 4: The Mediterranean World · Week 17 · World and American History · Teacher Plan

Ancient Greece

Lesson length: 60 minutes live, plus the after-class interactive lesson

Objectives

  • Explain how geography led the Greeks to form independent city-states and to become sailors and traders.
  • Compare the government and the status of citizens in Athens and Sparta.
  • Describe Athenian democracy, its democratic ideas and its limits.
  • Explain the causes and effects of the Persian Wars.
  • Summarize Greek achievements and how Greek and African civilizations, especially Egypt, influenced each other.

Standards

  • SS.6.W.3.2: Explain the causes and effects of the Persian and Peloponnesian Wars.
  • SS.6.W.3.3: Compare life in Athens and Sparta (government and the status of citizens).
  • SS.6.W.3.4: Explain the democratic concepts (polis, civic participation and voting rights, legislative bodies, written constitutions, rule of law) developed in ancient Greece.
  • SS.6.W.3.5: Summarize the important achievements and contributions of ancient Greek civilization.
  • SS.6.G.2.1: Explain how major physical characteristics, natural resources, climate, and absolute and relative locations have influenced settlement, interactions, and the economies of ancient civilizations of the world.
  • SS.6.W.1.3: Interpret primary and secondary sources.

Materials

  • The interactive lesson
  • A bag or bowl, paper slips, pencils
  • Beans or pebbles in two colors, 2 cups, a timer
  • Optional: broken flowerpot pieces (adult handles) or smooth stones and a marker
  • Printable practice page

Vocabulary

  • polis: A Greek city-state: a city and the farmland around it, with its own government.
  • democracy: Government by the people; in Athens, citizens voted directly on laws.
  • citizen: A member of a state with rights, such as voting, and duties, such as serving.
  • oligarchy: Government by a small group of powerful people.
  • assembly: In Athens, the meeting where citizens debated and voted on laws.
  • ostracism: An Athenian vote to send a powerful citizen away from the city for ten years.
  • Hellenistic: Describes the Greek-influenced cultures that spread from Egypt to India after Alexander the Great.

Key questions to ask

  • How did geography shape Greek government?
  • Who counted as a citizen, and who didn't?
  • Is Pericles describing Athens as it was, or as he wanted it seen?
  • How did older civilizations shape Greek ideas?

Common mistakes to watch for

  • Greece was one country with one government.
  • Everyone in Athens could vote.
  • The Greeks invented civilization on their own.
  • The marathon legend is proven history.

Support (if the student is struggling)

  • Use a two-column Athens/Sparta organizer.
  • Read Pericles one sentence at a time and paraphrase.
  • Provide a timeline strip with dates filled in.

Challenge (if the student is ready for more)

  • Read more of Pericles' Funeral Oration (Thucydides 2.35-46) and find what he says about Athenian women.
  • Research the kleroterion, the Athenian lottery machine.

Background for the teacher

Dates: first recorded Olympiad 776 BCE (traditional); Cleisthenes' reforms about 508/507; Ionian Revolt 499-494; Marathon 490; Thermopylae and Salamis 480; Plataea 479; Peloponnesian War 431-404; Alexander enters Egypt 332/331 and founds Alexandria in 331. Herodotus 6.105-106 tells of Pheidippides running to Sparta; the run from Marathon to Athens appears in much later writers (Plutarch, Lucian). Herodotus 2.4 credits the Egyptians with the 365-day year, and 2.50 says the names of nearly all the gods came to Greece from Egypt. Estimates of Athenian citizens (adult men) in the 5th century range from about 30,000 to 60,000, of a total population of perhaps 250,000 to 300,000; estimates of the enslaved share vary widely. The Pericles excerpt follows Crawley's 1874 translation (Project Gutenberg) with British spellings kept and glossed; the omitted words include a phrase the Gutenberg text prints as "if no social standing" (a misprint for "if to social standing"). Standards wording for SS.6.W.3.3-3.5 should be checked against CPALMS.

Sources

  • Thucydides, History of the Peloponnesian War, translated by Richard Crawley (1874), Project Gutenberg.
  • Herodotus, The Histories (Books 2 and 6).
  • Stoa of Attalos and Agora Excavations, American School of Classical Studies at Athens, materials on ostraka and the kleroterion.
  • Pomeroy, Burstein, Donlan and Roberts, Ancient Greece: A Political, Social, and Cultural History.

Answer key: interactive lesson

Reconnect

  1. Which Persian king organized the empire into satrapies and built Persepolis?
    Answer: Darius I
    Ruled 522 to 486 BCE.
  2. Which Greek historian described the Persian messengers?
    Answer: Herodotus
    The Histories, Book 8.
  3. Which city in Asia Minor was at the western end of the Royal Road?
    Answer: Sardis
    It ran about 1,600 miles to Susa.
  4. Last week you learned Herodotus was a Greek writing about Persia's invasion of Greece. How might that affect the way he tells the story of the Persian Wars?
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: He may favor the Greeks, make them look heroic or exaggerate Persian numbers; So historians compare his account with other evidence
    Model answer: He might make the Greeks look heroic and exaggerate the size of the Persian army, so historians check his account against other evidence.

Learn 1: Mountains, Sea and the City-State

  1. Explain how Greece's mountains and its coastline each shaped the way Greeks lived. Give one effect for each.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Mountains: separated communities, leading to independent city-states (or limited farming); Coastline/sea: trade, sailing, fishing or colonies
    Model answer: The mountains cut the land into separate valleys, so each community became its own independent city-state with its own government. The long coastline meant the sea was always close, so Greeks became sailors and traders and started colonies around the Mediterranean.
  2. What is the Greek word for a city-state?
    Answer: polis
    It gives us politics, police and metropolis.
  3. The first recorded Olympic Games were held in 776 BCE. The Battle of Marathon was in 490 BCE. How many years apart were they?
    Answer: 286 years
    For two BCE dates, subtract: 776 − 490 = 286 years.

Learn 2: Athens and Sparta

  1. What were the enslaved farm workers of Sparta called?
    Answer: helots
    They outnumbered Spartan citizens.
  2. Explain the connection between Sparta's helots and its military way of life.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Helots greatly outnumbered citizens, so Spartans feared revolt; They trained every citizen man as a soldier to keep control
    Model answer: Sparta enslaved so many helots that they outnumbered the citizens, and the Spartans were afraid of a revolt. So they turned every citizen man into a full-time soldier to keep the helots under control.
  3. If you were a girl in ancient Greece, would you rather live in Athens or Sparta? If you were an enslaved person, would either be fair? Explain both answers with evidence.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: A choice for a girl with evidence (Spartan women owned land and trained; Athenian women had few rights); Recognizes that enslaved people had no rights in either city
    Model answer: As a girl I would choose Sparta, because Spartan women could own land and trained their bodies, while Athenian women were mostly kept at home. But for an enslaved person, neither was fair: helots in Sparta and enslaved people in Athens had no rights at all.

Learn 3: The First Democracy, and Its Limits

  1. Pericles says Athens' government "favours the many instead of the few." What does he say that is why it is called?
    Answer: a democracy
    demos (people) + kratos (power, rule).
  2. Explain the difference between Athens' direct democracy and the representative democracy of the United States. Why might a direct democracy be hard to run in a country of more than 330 million people?
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Direct: citizens vote on laws themselves; representative: citizens elect lawmakers; Hard with millions: too many people to meet or debate, too many laws to vote on
    Model answer: In Athens citizens voted on every law themselves; in the United States we elect representatives who make laws for us. A direct democracy would be hard with more than 330 million people because they couldn't all meet, debate and vote on every law.
  3. Pericles says Athens gives "equal justice to all" and that poverty does not "bar the way." Using evidence from the lesson, judge how true this was.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Partly true: poor citizen men could vote, serve on juries and be chosen by lottery; Not true for most residents: women, enslaved people and foreign-born residents were excluded; A balanced judgment
    Model answer: It was partly true: a poor Athenian citizen man could vote in the Assembly, sit on a jury and even be chosen by lottery for office. But "all" really meant citizen men. Women, enslaved people and foreign-born residents, most of the people in Athens, had no vote at all, so Pericles' words were only true for a minority.

Learn 4: The Persian Wars and Greek Achievements

  1. Put these events in order.
    Answer: Ionian Revolt → Battle of Marathon → Battle of Thermopylae → Peloponnesian War begins
    499, 490, 480 and 431 BCE.
  2. At which sea battle in 480 BCE did the Athenian navy defeat Xerxes' fleet?
    Answer: Salamis
    Led by Themistocles.
  3. Give one piece of evidence that Greek civilization learned from Egypt, and explain why it matters to tell this part of the story.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Evidence: Herodotus on gods' names or the calendar, Greek thinkers studying in Egypt, Alexandria as a center of learning; Why it matters: civilizations build on each other; Greek achievement rested partly on older African knowledge; a truthful history includes it
    Model answer: Herodotus wrote that the Greeks credited the Egyptians with the 365-day calendar. It matters because it shows Greek ideas didn't come from nowhere; they built on much older African knowledge, and a truthful history gives Egypt credit.

Hands-On

  1. Describe your question, the arguments, and both votes. How did the result or the mood change when some people couldn't vote? What does this show about Athenian democracy?
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Question and main arguments; Results of both votes; Observation of the exclusion's effect; Connects to Athens' limited citizenship
    Model answer: We debated a field trip to the springs or the science museum. In the first vote, springs won 3 to 1. With the "Athenian rules," only my dad and I could vote, so the result was 1 to 1, and my sister was upset that her voice didn't count. It showed that when most people can't vote, the decision doesn't reflect what the whole community wants, which was true in Athens.
  2. Athenians chose most officials by lottery instead of election. What is one advantage and one disadvantage of choosing leaders by lottery?
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Advantage: fair chance for everyone, rich people can't buy power, prevents campaigning; Disadvantage: an unprepared or unskilled person might be chosen
    Model answer: An advantage is that everyone gets an equal chance, so rich or famous people can't take over. A disadvantage is that someone who knows nothing about the job might be chosen.

Think Deeper

  1. Some people call Athens the "birthplace of democracy." Others say a government where most residents could not vote was not really a democracy. Take a position, support it with at least two pieces of evidence, and answer the other side.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: A clear position; At least two pieces of evidence (Assembly, lottery, juries, Pericles' words; or exclusion of women, enslaved people, foreigners); Answers a counterargument; Considers how democracy has grown since
    Model answer: Athens deserves credit as an early democracy, but it was a limited one. Citizen men voted directly on laws in the Assembly and were chosen by lottery for offices and juries, which gave ordinary men real power for the first time. Critics are right that women, enslaved people and foreigners, most of the population, had no voice. Still, Athens planted an idea, rule by the people, that later generations widened. The United States itself took until 1920 and 1965 to protect the votes of women and Black citizens, so democracy is something that grows.
  2. Imagine the Persians had won at Salamis in 480 BCE. Using what you learned about how Persia ruled conquered peoples last week, predict what might have happened to Athens. Would its democracy have survived? Explain your reasoning.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Uses accurate Persian policies (satraps, tribute, tolerance of local customs, revolts punished); A reasoned prediction about Athens' government and culture; Acknowledges uncertainty
    Model answer: Athens would probably have become part of a satrapy and paid tribute. Persia often let peoples keep their religions and customs, so Greek gods, language and festivals might have continued. But Athens had helped the Ionian Revolt and burned Sardis, so Persia might have punished it, and a Persian-appointed ruler would likely have replaced the Assembly. Democracy might not have survived, though we can't know for sure.

Exit Check

  1. Which city-state made every citizen man a soldier and was ruled by two kings?
    Answer: Sparta
    Athens was a democracy.
  2. In which year was the Battle of Marathon? Include BCE.
    Answer: 490 BCE
    Darius's army was defeated.
  3. Which city, founded by Alexander the Great in Egypt, became the center of Hellenistic learning?
    Answer: Alexandria
    Home of the great Library.
  4. Name one group that could not vote in Athenian democracy, and explain why that matters.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: A correct group (women, enslaved people, foreign-born residents); Why it matters (most people had no voice; the democracy was limited)
    Model answer: Women could not vote, so about half the people had no say in laws that affected them.

Answer key: printable page

  1. How did Greece's geography lead to independent city-states?
    Answer: Mountains separated communities into valleys, so each formed its own polis with its own government.
  2. Compare Athens and Sparta in government and in the lives of women.
    Answer: Athens: direct democracy; women had few rights. Sparta: two kings, council and assembly; women could own land and trained for fitness.
  3. Describe two ways Athenian democracy worked and one way it was limited.
    Answer: Citizens voted in the Assembly; officials chosen by lottery; ostracism. Limited: only adult citizen men could vote.
  4. Explain one cause and two effects of the Persian Wars.
    Answer: Cause: Athens helped the Ionian Revolt. Effects: Greek independence, Athens' rise as a sea power, later the Peloponnesian War.
  5. Name three Greek achievements.
    Answer: Philosophy (Socrates, Plato, Aristotle), mathematics (Euclid), medicine (Hippocrates), theater, the Parthenon, democracy.
  6. What did Herodotus say the Greeks learned from Egypt?
    Answer: The 365-day calendar and the names of many gods.
