Week 17: The Coordinate Plane, Evaluating Arguments, Bones and Muscles, and Ancient Greece

Math opens the coordinate plane in all four directions and maps a young baker's delivery route. ELA tests arguments for weak reasoning and studies Frederick Douglass's 1852 Fourth of July speech. Science explores the skeletal and muscular systems with a model arm. Social Studies visits ancient Greece and runs an Athenian assembly at home.

How scoring works: each lesson scores itself. Your child gets two tries per question: full points on the first try, half points on the second, then the answer and explanation appear. A score of 85 or higher means the lesson is mastered. Written answers go to Jonathan, who scores them with a rubric.

Time this week: About 60 minutes of live lessons per subject, plus a hands-on activity, Think Deeper questions and an Exit Check for each.

Gather this week: Painter's tape (indoors) or sidewalk chalk (outdoors), about 33 sticky notes or index cards and a marker, a family member, graph paper or plain paper and a ruler, colored pencils, and optionally a printed or phone map of your neighborhood (Math); 10 index cards and a marker, a family member or two, 3 to 5 real advertisements (a magazine, a cereal box, junk mail, or a video ad watched with a parent), and paper and pencil (ELA); 2 strips of stiff cardboard about 2 inches by 10 inches, a brass paper fastener (brad), 2 long balloons (or 2 pieces of string or yarn, about 14 inches each), tape, a hole punch or a sharp pencil (used by an adult), a marker, paper and pencil, and optionally a cooked, cleaned chicken drumstick bone and a jar of white vinegar (Science); a bag or bowl, slips of paper and a pencil for each person, dried beans or small pebbles in two colors (or white and dark paper squares), 2 cups or bowls for voting, a timer, at least 2 family members, and optionally a clean, broken flowerpot or smooth stones and a marker (Social Studies).

Heads up: Science: if you try the optional vinegar bone, start it on the first day; it needs 3 days. Use a cooked, cleaned bone, wash hands after handling it, and throw it away afterward. An adult punches the cardboard holes. Math: chalk only on your own driveway or sidewalk, away from traffic. Social Studies: if you use broken pottery for ostraka, an adult handles the sharp edges, or use smooth stones. ELA: the Douglass excerpt is about slavery; the lesson leaves out the speech's graphic passages, but you may want to read it together. Video ads should be watched with a parent.

ELA: Evaluating Arguments

Your child learns how arguments persuade with logic, emotion and credibility; spots five fallacies (overgeneralization, bandwagon, attacking the person, either-or and slippery slope); questions misleading statistics; and analyzes how Frederick Douglass used evidence, rhetorical questions and irony in his 1852 speech in Rochester, New York.

Ask at dinner: "Is that a good reason, or is it a fallacy?"

Try it together: Look at an ad together and ask: who measured that number, compared with what, and what was left out?

Signs it's clicking: They name a fallacy in an ad or a family debate and explain why it is weak. Signs they need help: They think every emotional appeal is bad. Ask whether the emotion rests on true facts.

Math: The Four-Quadrant Coordinate Plane

Your child plots ordered pairs with negative numbers, fractions and decimals in all four quadrants, names quadrants from the signs of the coordinates, reflects points across the axes, finds horizontal and vertical distances with absolute value, and plans a delivery route for a home bakery.

Ask at dinner: "Which number tells you left or right, and which tells you up or down?"

Try it together: Walk ordered pairs on the floor grid together and play the reflection game.

Signs it's clicking: They explain that (4, −7) and (−4, −7) are reflections across the y-axis because the x-coordinates are opposites. Signs they need help: They plot (−3, 6) by going up 3 and left 6. Remind them: walk before you climb (x first).

Life Science: The Skeletal and Muscular Systems

Your child learns the five jobs of the skeleton, the parts of a long bone, types of joints, the roles of cartilage, ligaments and tendons, how muscles work in opposing pairs and act on bones as levers, and how exercise, calcium and vitamin D build strong bones. They build a working model arm.

Ask at dinner: "When you straighten your arm, which muscle is pulling?"

Try it together: Feel each other's biceps and triceps while bending and straightening an arm.

Signs it's clicking: They explain that a muscle can only pull, so muscles must work in pairs. Signs they need help: They mix up tendons and ligaments. Tendons link muscle to bone; ligaments link bone to bone.

World and American History: Ancient Greece

Your child connects Greece's mountains and sea to its independent city-states, compares Athens and Sparta, reads Pericles on Athenian democracy and judges its limits, follows the Persian Wars from Marathon to Salamis, and learns how Greek civilization built on knowledge from Egypt and other older civilizations.

Ask at dinner: "Who got to vote in Athens, and who didn't?"

Try it together: Run the Athenian Assembly Day together, including the vote with "Athenian rules."

Signs it's clicking: They say Athens was an important but limited democracy and give evidence for both parts. Signs they need help: They think everyone in Athens could vote. Ask who counted as a citizen.
