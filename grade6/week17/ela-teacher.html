Unit 4: Making the Case (Argument) · Week 17 · ELA · Teacher Plan

Evaluating Arguments and Spotting Weak Reasoning

Lesson length: 60 minutes live, plus the after-class interactive lesson

Objectives

  • Identify how arguments use logic, emotion and credibility to persuade.
  • Recognize five common fallacies (overgeneralization, bandwagon, attacking the person, either-or and slippery slope) and explain why each is weak reasoning.
  • Recognize misleading evidence: cherry-picking, false cause and missing comparisons.
  • Analyze the types of reasoning in Frederick Douglass's 1852 speech, including his use of evidence and irony.
  • Use the roots log, path and fall to unlock word meanings.

Standards

  • ELA.6.R.2.4: Track the development of an argument, identifying the types of reasoning used.
  • ELA.6.R.3.4: Identify rhetorical appeals in a text.
  • ELA.6.V.1.2: Apply knowledge of Greek and Latin roots and affixes to determine meanings of words and phrases in grade-level content.
  • ELA.K12.EE.2.1: Read and comprehend grade-level complex texts proficiently.

Materials

  • The interactive lesson
  • 10 index cards and a marker
  • 3 to 5 real advertisements
  • Paper and pencil
  • Printable practice page

Vocabulary

  • logic (logos): Persuading with facts, evidence and reasoning.
  • emotion (pathos): Persuading by making the audience feel something.
  • credibility (ethos): Persuading by showing the speaker or source can be trusted.
  • fallacy: A mistake in reasoning that makes an argument seem stronger than it is.
  • cherry-picking: Choosing only the evidence that supports your side and leaving out the rest.
  • irony: Pointing out a painful contradiction, or saying the opposite of what you mean, to make a point.
  • abolitionist: A person who worked to end slavery.

Key questions to ask

  • Which appeal is doing the work here?
  • Is this reasoning sound, or is it a fallacy?
  • Who measured this number, compared with what, and what might be left out?
  • Why might a speaker choose emotion over logic?

Common mistakes to watch for

  • Emotional appeals are always bad.
  • A real statistic can never be misleading.
  • A popular opinion is a proven one.
  • Douglass didn't use logic because he said he wouldn't argue.

Support (if the student is struggling)

  • Give a fallacy reference card with one example each.
  • Read the Douglass excerpt aloud twice and paraphrase each paragraph together.
  • Give the three statistic questions on a card.

Challenge (if the student is ready for more)

  • Read more of Douglass's speech, including his closing hope in the Declaration of Independence, and explain how it changes the tone.
  • Find a fallacy in a real newspaper letter to the editor.

Background for the teacher

Douglass spoke at Corinthian Hall, Rochester, on Monday, July 5, 1852, at the invitation of the Rochester Ladies' Anti-Slavery Society; estimates of the audience run from about 500 to 600. The excerpt follows the text printed in the appendix of My Bondage and My Freedom (1855), from Project Gutenberg. The figure of 72 capital crimes for Black men in Virginia versus 2 for white men is Douglass's own statement in the speech. The lesson leaves out the speech's graphic list of slavery's cruelties because of students' age; the challenge points families to the closing passage, where Douglass says he does not despair of the country and draws encouragement from the Declaration of Independence. Aristotle's three modes of persuasion appear in his Rhetoric (4th century BCE).

Answer key: interactive lesson

Reconnect

  1. Type fact, opinion or claim: "Every homeschool co-op should offer a debate class."
    Answer: an arguable claim
    Reasonable people could disagree, and evidence can support it.
  2. A short personal story used as evidence is called an ___.
    Answer: anecdote
    Vivid, but not sufficient alone.
  3. What part of the U.S. Constitution did Susan B. Anthony use as evidence?
    Answer: the preamble ("We, the people")
    Week 16.
  4. Name the three tests for evidence from last week and say which one a website with no author and no sources is most likely to fail.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Relevant, sufficient, credible; Fails credibility
    Model answer: Relevant, sufficient and credible. A website with no author or sources is most likely to fail the credible test.

Learn 1: Logic, Emotion and Credibility

  1. "Our dentist, who has 25 years of experience, recommends brushing twice a day." Which appeal is this mainly: logic, emotion or credibility?
    Answer: credibility (ethos)
    It relies on the dentist's experience.
  2. "Picture a lonely puppy shivering in a cold shelter tonight." Which appeal is this mainly?
    Answer: emotion (pathos)
    Vivid words meant to stir sympathy.
  3. Use the roots.
    Answer: fallible; empathy
    fall (deceive, err); path (feeling).
  4. Write three short sentences arguing that your family should start a vegetable garden: one using logic, one emotion and one credibility. Label each.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: A logic sentence (fact, number or reasoning); An emotion sentence (feelings, vivid image); A credibility sentence (expert or experience); Each labeled correctly
    Model answer: Logic: A packet of tomato seeds costs about $3 and can grow pounds of tomatoes, so a garden saves money. Emotion: Imagine biting into a warm, sweet tomato you grew yourself. Credibility: The county extension office, run by University of Florida experts, says fall is the best time to plant many vegetables here.

Learn 2: Fallacies: Reasoning That Cheats

  1. "Nine million people bought these sneakers. They must be the best." Name the fallacy.
    Answer: bandwagon
    Popularity doesn't prove quality.
  2. "Join the swim team, or you'll never be healthy." Name the fallacy.
    Answer: either-or
    There are many other ways to be healthy.
  3. "Mr. Lee says we should add a crosswalk, but he's not even from around here." Name the fallacy.
    Answer: attacking the person
    Where he's from says nothing about whether the crosswalk is a good idea.
  4. Rewrite this slippery-slope argument so it uses sound reasoning instead: "If we get a dog, next we'll want a cat, then a horse, and the whole house will be a zoo."
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Removes the unsupported chain of events; Gives a real, reasonable concern or reason with evidence
    Model answer: Getting a dog is a big commitment: it needs walks twice a day and costs about $1,000 a year for food and vet visits, so we should be sure we can handle those costs and that time before we decide.

Learn 3: Misleading Evidence: Cherry-Picking and False Cause

  1. "Every time our town opens the pool, ice cream sales go up. The pool must make people buy ice cream." Which weak reasoning is this: cherry-picking or false cause?
    Answer: false cause
    Hot weather causes both; the pool doesn't cause ice cream sales.
  2. In the ice cream example, what third thing probably causes both pool openings and ice cream sales? Explain how that changes the argument.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Hot weather (summer); Both rise because of the weather, so one doesn't cause the other
    Model answer: Hot summer weather. The pool opens in summer and people buy more ice cream when it's hot, so the weather causes both. The pool isn't causing the ice cream sales.
  3. An ad says, "9 out of 10 kids who tried SuperSnack loved it!" Write two questions you would ask before believing this, and explain what each could reveal.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Two good questions (how many kids were asked, who did the survey, were kids paid or chosen, what were they compared with); Explains what each could reveal (a tiny or biased sample, hidden results)
    Model answer: How many kids tried it? If it was only 10, the result means very little. Who ran the survey? If the company paid for it and picked kids who already liked snacks, the results could be cherry-picked.

Learn 4: Douglass's Reasoning: Proof, Then Fire

  1. In paragraph 1, what kind of laws does Douglass use as evidence that slaveholders knew enslaved people were human?
    Answer: laws forbidding teaching enslaved people to read or write
    No one passes such laws about animals.
  2. In paragraph 2 Douglass asks, "Would you have me argue that man is entitled to liberty?" and answers, "You have already declared it." What document is he probably referring to, and why is this an effective argument for a Fourth of July audience?
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: The Declaration of Independence ("all men are created equal ... Liberty"); Effective because he uses the audience's own celebrated words against slavery: they cannot honor the Declaration and defend slavery
    Model answer: He means the Declaration of Independence, which says all men are created equal and have a right to liberty. It is effective because the Fourth of July celebrates that very document. If Americans already declared that everyone has a right to liberty, they can't honestly defend slavery, so Douglass uses their own words as evidence.
  3. In paragraph 3, Douglass says "scorching irony, not convincing argument, is needed." Why might he believe argument was no longer enough in 1852? Do you agree that sometimes strong emotion is the right tool? Explain.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Explains his reasoning: the facts were already known and conceded; people needed to be moved to act, not informed; A thoughtful personal judgment with a reason; Shows awareness that emotion should still rest on truth
    Model answer: Douglass believed the facts were already clear: even slaveholders' own laws admitted that enslaved people were human, and the nation had already declared that all men have a right to liberty, yet slavery continued. People needed their conscience woken up, not more information. I agree that strong emotion can be the right tool when the truth is known but people are ignoring it, as long as the emotion is based on real facts and not on lies.

Hands-On

  1. Describe three ads: what each sells, its main appeal, and any fallacy you found. Which was most persuasive, and which was least fair? Why?
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Three ads with product and main appeal; At least one fallacy correctly identified; A judgment about most persuasive and least fair, with reasons
    Model answer: 1. A cereal box with a cartoon tiger: emotion (fun) and bandwagon ("Kids everywhere love it!"). 2. A toothpaste ad: credibility ("9 out of 10 dentists recommend") and logic (cavity facts). 3. A car flyer: emotion (a happy family on a beach). Most persuasive: the toothpaste ad, because it gave reasons and an expert source. Least fair: the cereal box, because "kids everywhere love it" is bandwagon and tells nothing about nutrition.

Think Deeper

  1. Both Douglass (1852) and Anthony (1873) lived in Rochester and used America's founding documents as evidence. Compare how they used them. Which speaker relied more on logic, and which more on emotion? Use at least one quotation from each.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Accurate quotation from each speech; Compares how each used a founding document (Declaration, Constitution); A supported judgment about logic versus emotion; Recognizes both used more than one appeal
    Model answer: Anthony relied more on logic. She quoted the Constitution's preamble and reasoned that "we, the people" meant "the whole people - women as well as men," so women already had the right to vote. Douglass also used logic, pointing to laws against teaching enslaved people to read as proof that slaveholders knew they were human, and to the Declaration: "You have already declared it." But he chose emotion as his main tool, saying the nation needed "fire" and "thunder" and calling the Fourth of July a day that reveals "gross injustice and cruelty." Anthony wanted to prove a legal point before a trial; Douglass wanted to shock a nation into acting.
  2. A neighbor posts this online: "The new park is a terrible idea. Everyone I know is against it, and the people who proposed it are just trying to look important. If we build a park, next they'll raise our taxes every year." Identify three fallacies, then write a respectful reply that uses sound reasoning and asks for real evidence.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Three fallacies correctly named and explained (bandwagon, attacking the person, slippery slope); A respectful reply with a clear claim and reasoning; Asks for or offers real evidence (cost, use, data); Avoids fallacies itself
    Model answer: Fallacies: "Everyone I know is against it" is bandwagon; "just trying to look important" attacks the people instead of the idea; "next they'll raise our taxes every year" is a slippery slope with no evidence. Reply: I think the park deserves a fair look. A park gives kids a safe place to play and adds shade trees to our neighborhood. Instead of guessing about taxes, let's ask the town for the actual cost and how it would be paid for, and look at how much families in nearby towns use their parks, before deciding.

Exit Check

  1. Which appeal uses facts and reasoning: logic, emotion or credibility?
    Answer: logic (logos)
    From the root log: word, reason.
  2. "If you skip one practice, you'll quit the team, then quit everything." Name the fallacy.
    Answer: slippery slope
    An unsupported chain of disasters.
  3. In what year did Frederick Douglass deliver "What to the Slave Is the Fourth of July?"
    Answer: 1852
    July 5, 1852, in Rochester, New York.
  4. "I ate carrots and then got an A on my test, so carrots make you smarter." Explain why this reasoning is weak.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: False cause: one thing happening before another doesn't prove it caused it; Other causes (studying) are likely
    Model answer: It is false cause. Eating carrots before the test doesn't prove the carrots caused the A; studying probably did.

Answer key: printable page

  1. Name the three appeals and give one example of each.
    Answer: Logic (facts), emotion (vivid story), credibility (expert or experience).
  2. Name and explain three fallacies, each with an example.
    Answer: Any three of overgeneralization, bandwagon, attacking the person, either-or, slippery slope, with examples.
  3. Explain cherry-picking and false cause, with an example of each.
    Answer: Cherry-picking: using only helpful evidence and hiding the rest (example). False cause: assuming one event caused another because it came first (example).
  4. What evidence did Douglass use to show slaveholders knew enslaved people were human?
    Answer: Laws forbidding teaching enslaved people to read or write (and Virginia's 72 capital crimes for Black men vs. 2 for white men).
  5. What is a rhetorical question? Give one from Douglass's speech.
    Answer: A question asked for effect, not to get an answer; "Would you have me argue that man is entitled to liberty?"
  6. Use the roots: what do logic, empathy and fallacy have to do with their roots?
    Answer: log (reason), path (feeling), fall (deceive).
