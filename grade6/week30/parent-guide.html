Week 30: End-of-Year Review and Unit 6 Checkpoints

Congratulations: this is the last week of Grade 6. Each subject reviews Unit 6, looks back over the whole year and ends with the Unit 6 checkpoint. Math plans a summer business using skills from all six units. ELA delivers the research presentation. Science launches a capstone investigation. Social Studies traces the beginning of the Atlantic world through the founding of St. Augustine in 1565 and builds a year-long timeline museum. The end-of-year comprehensive mastery test follows this week.

How scoring works: each lesson scores itself. Your child gets two tries per question: full points on the first try, half points on the second, then the answer and explanation appear. A score of 85 or higher means the lesson is mastered. Written answers go to Jonathan, who scores them with a rubric.

Checkpoint scoring: checkpoint questions give one try and do not show answers, so the checkpoint fairly measures what your child can do alone. Jonathan adds the points for written answers and shares the checkpoint score. Mastery is 85; below 85 means a review lesson, then a retake. Projects below 85 are returned with feedback and revised once.

Time this week: About 60 minutes of live lessons per subject, plus a hands-on activity, Think Deeper questions and a checkpoint for each. Each checkpoint takes about 30 to 45 minutes and is done independently after class. The science capstone usually runs one to two weeks for plant investigations and may be finished after the checkpoint.

Gather this week: Paper and pencil, graph paper, a ruler, a measuring tape, grocery flyers or store websites, an optional calculator, a family member, and at least 10 family members or friends to survey, with your approval (Math); your child's slides or poster and note cards, a computer or tablet to show slides, a timer, an optional phone or tablet to record, an audience of at least two people (family, co-op friends or grandparents on a video call), and the Unit 6 project rubric (ELA); a science notebook, paper and pencil, graph paper, a ruler, a metric ruler or measuring tape, a timer or clock, materials for the chosen capstone (such as bean seeds, cups, potting soil and water), and a family member (Science); about 25 index cards, markers or colored pencils, a long piece of string or painter's tape (at least 3 meters, about 10 feet), clothespins or tape, a meterstick or measuring tape, a world map or atlas, your child's notes from the year, and a family member (Social Studies).

Heads up: Checkpoints are done independently, one try per item, with no answers shown until you review them together; mastery is 85, and a score below 85 means a review lesson and a retake. Approve your child's capstone question and materials before they begin, and supervise any real summer business. If the presentation is on a video call, set up the call yourself and have your child share only their first name. Social Studies discusses the Atlantic slave trade and the destruction of Indigenous nations as part of the year's honest review. Wash hands after handling soil, seeds or food, peel up painter's tape gently, and keep string off walkways so no one trips.

ELA: End-of-Year Review and Research Presentation

Your child delivers the 4- to 6-minute research presentation with visuals, spoken citations and a Q&A, reviews research skills (questions, judging sources, synthesis and citation), and reflects on growth across six units. The Unit 6 checkpoint asks them to evaluate an invented practice source, use research skills and write a synthesized paragraph.

Ask at dinner: "Who made this source, and why should I trust it?"

Try it together: Be in the audience, ask at least one hard question, and give one specific strength and one specific suggestion.

Signs it's clicking: They compare this presentation with their Week 20 speech and name exactly how they improved. Signs they need help: They treat personal experience as evidence. Ask, "What data or expert source backs that up?"

Math: End-of-Year Review and Unit 6 Checkpoint

Your child reviews area, surface area and volume, statistical questions, data displays and measures of center, reviews the year's money skills (unit price, discounts and tax, integers, interest and budgets), and plans a summer business that uses a skill from every unit. The Unit 6 checkpoint covers Weeks 26 to 29.

Ask at dinner: "Is this an area, surface area or volume problem, and how do you know?"

Try it together: Help your child price real supplies for their summer business plan and check their profit math.

Signs it's clicking: They explain why the median describes a typical day better than the mean when there's an outlier. Signs they need help: They mix up square and cubic units. Ask, "Are we covering or filling?"

Life Science: End-of-Year Review and Capstone Investigation

Your child reviews traits, alleles and Punnett squares, adaptations and natural selection, connects ideas across the year from cells to populations, and designs and carries out a fair-test capstone investigation with a hypothesis, variables, repeated trials, a graph and a conclusion. The Unit 6 checkpoint covers Weeks 26 to 29.

Ask at dinner: "What's the one thing you're changing, and what are you measuring?"

Try it together: Help set up the capstone, and check the data table with your child each day.

Signs it's clicking: They explain why a litter with no white pups doesn't prove a Punnett square wrong. Signs they need help: They want to change two things at once. Ask, "If both change, how will you know which one caused the result?"

World and American History: End-of-Year Review: The Atlantic World Begins

Your child reviews the Olmec, Maya, Mexica, Inca, Cahokia, Indigenous Florida and the Columbian Exchange, learns how St. Augustine was founded in 1565 on Timucua land with Africans present from the start, traces big themes such as trade and writing across the whole year, and builds a timeline museum. The Unit 6 checkpoint covers Weeks 26 to 29 and includes a Columbus primary source.

Ask at dinner: "Whose story is missing if we tell it only from one point of view?"

Try it together: Take your child's timeline museum tour and ask which theme connects three of the cards.

Signs it's clicking: They explain why St. Augustine's story includes the Timucua and Africans as well as the Spanish. Signs they need help: They think U.S. history starts in 1607 or 1776. Point to St. Augustine (1565) and the 14,000-year Indigenous story.
