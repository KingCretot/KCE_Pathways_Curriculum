Unit 6: Research and Presentation · Week 30 · ELA · Teacher Plan

End-of-Year Review and Research Presentation

Lesson length: 60 minutes live, plus the after-class interactive lesson

Objectives

  • Deliver a 4- to 6-minute research presentation with visuals, oral citations and a Q&A.
  • Evaluate a source for reliability using SIFT and the source questions from Week 27.
  • Synthesize two sources in a paragraph with in-text citations.
  • Connect the skills from all six units of the year.
  • Demonstrate mastery of Unit 6 on the checkpoint (85 or higher).

Standards

  • ELA.6.C.2.1: Present information orally, in a logical sequence, using nonverbal cues, appropriate volume, clear pronunciation, and appropriate pacing.
  • ELA.6.C.4.1: Conduct research to answer a question, drawing on multiple reliable and valid sources, and refocusing the inquiry when appropriate.
  • ELA.6.C.5.1: Integrate diverse digital media to enhance audience engagement in oral or written tasks.
  • ELA.6.C.1.4: Write expository texts to explain and/or analyze information from multiple sources, using a logical organizational structure, relevant elaboration, and varied transitions.
  • ELA.K12.EE.1.1: Cite evidence to explain and justify reasoning.

Materials

  • The interactive lesson
  • The student's slides or poster and note cards, a device to show slides, a timer, optional recording device, an audience
  • The Unit 6 project rubric
  • Printable practice page

Vocabulary

  • research question: An open, focused question that can be answered with evidence from several sources.
  • reliable source: A source with expert authors, clear evidence and an honest purpose.
  • synthesis: Combining information from several sources into one new understanding.
  • in-text citation: A short credit in your writing, such as (Florida Department of Health), that points to your works-cited list.
  • oral citation: Naming a source aloud while you speak.
  • reflection: Thinking back on your work to see how you grew and what you'll do next.

Key questions to ask

  • Who made this source, and why?
  • Where does each borrowed fact come from?
  • How did a skill from an earlier unit help you here?
  • What will you do differently next year?

Common mistakes to watch for

  • A personal experience is the same as data.
  • Paraphrasing means changing a few words.
  • A source with a professional-looking design is reliable.
  • A presentation is the report read aloud.

Support (if the student is struggling)

  • Allow the presentation to be delivered to a small audience first, then repeated.
  • Provide a paragraph frame for Part C.
  • Review the SIFT bookmark before the checkpoint.

Challenge (if the student is ready for more)

  • Present to an audience outside the family, such as a library group or co-op class.
  • Turn the research into a letter to a local official or newspaper.

How the lesson runs

During the live lesson, the student shares their screen and answers the Reconnect questions and the Learn checks live while you teach. The Hands-On activity, Think Deeper and Exit Check are completed after class.

Scoring

The interactive lesson scores itself: full points on the first try, half points on the second try, then the answer and explanation appear. Mastery is 85. Written responses arrive in your results email with the score; score them with the rubric below before the student moves on.

Checkpoint scoring

Checkpoint questions are scored separately from the lesson. The student gets one try per question and the answers are not shown, so the checkpoint measures independent mastery and a retake stays fair. Your results email shows the auto-scored checkpoint points, a list of each item marked correct or MISSED, and the points available for the written items. Score the written items with their rubrics, then: checkpoint score = (auto points + rubric points) / total possible × 100. Mastery is 85. Below 85: teach a targeted review lesson on the parts missed (A to D), then the student retakes the checkpoint. Record both attempts in the Weekly Score Log.

Learn 1: Review: Research Questions and Sources

  1. Rank these three sources from most to least reliable for a report on Florida springs, and explain your top and bottom choices: a tube-rental company's blog, a U.S. Geological Survey report, a classmate's social media post.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: A sensible ranking; Explains the most reliable choice; Explains the least reliable choice
    Model answer: 1. The USGS report, because government scientists collect data and show their methods. 2. The tube-rental blog, which may have some local facts but wants to sell rentals. 3. The classmate's post, because there's no evidence or expert author.

Learn 2: Review: Synthesizing and Citing

  1. Join these into one sentence with a transition and two citations: Source A (Florida Fish and Wildlife Conservation Commission) says manatees need water warmer than about 68°F in winter. Source B (Save the Manatee Club) says many manatees gather at power plant outflows.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: One sentence combining both sources; A transition; Credits both sources
    Model answer: Manatees need water warmer than about 68°F in winter (Florida Fish and Wildlife Conservation Commission), so many of them gather at the warm outflows of power plants (Save the Manatee Club).

Learn 3: The Year in Review: Skills That Connect

  1. Choose two earlier units and explain exactly how a skill from each one appears in your research presentation.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Two earlier units named; A specific skill from each; Shows where it appears in the presentation
    Model answer: From Unit 1, I open with a short scene of my family seeing green water at the beach, using sensory details. From Unit 4, I answer the counterclaim that blooms are natural by showing the data on fertilizer runoff.

Hands-On

  1. How long did you present? What questions were you asked, and what feedback did you receive?
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Time reported; Questions listed; Specific feedback listed
    Model answer: I presented for 5 minutes 20 seconds. Grandma asked whether the blooms hurt fish, and my uncle asked who pays for the reservoir; I said I'd need to research the second one. Feedback: my map was very clear and I spoke slowly, but I looked at the screen too much during the second finding.
  2. Compare this presentation with your Week 20 argument speech. Name one way you improved and one goal for next year.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: A specific improvement with evidence; A specific goal
    Model answer: In Week 20 I rushed and said "um" a lot; this time I paused after key facts and used note cards with key words, so I hardly said "um." Next year I want to look at my audience more instead of the screen.

Think Deeper

  1. Choose the piece of writing you're proudest of this year (narrative, article, poetry analysis, argument, literary analysis or research). Explain why, using specific details, and describe how one skill you learned in it showed up again later in the year.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Names a piece and explains why with specific details; Traces one skill into a later unit; Reflects honestly on growth
    Model answer: I'm proudest of my argument essay about adding more water fountains to parks, because I answered the counterclaim about cost with real numbers from our city budget. That same skill, using evidence and answering the other side, showed up in my research report when I explained why some people say algae blooms are natural and showed the data that runoff makes them worse. I used to only list facts, but now I explain how the evidence proves my point.

Checkpoint: Part A: Evaluate a source

  1. Practice source (invented for this checkpoint): "SPRINGS ARE FINE, SAYS LOCAL EXPERT" , posted on FloridaFunTubing.biz , no date. "Our springs are as clear as ever! Some so-called scientists claim flow is down, but I've been tubing here for 20 years and I haven't noticed a thing. Book your tube rental today and see for yourself! Use code CLEAR for 10% off." Author: "Big Jim," owner. Evaluate this source's reliability for a research report on spring flow. Use at least three specific clues from the source.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric (6 points toward the checkpoint).
    Rubric: A clear judgment of reliability; Three specific clues from the source; Explains how each clue affects reliability
    Model answer: It's not reliable. The author is "Big Jim," the owner of a tube-rental business, so he wants people to keep buying rentals. His only evidence is that he hasn't noticed a change, which is personal opinion, not data. There's no date, and the post ends with a discount code, which shows its purpose is to sell.
  2. Name the SIFT move you would use next with this source, and describe exactly what you would do.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric (4 points toward the checkpoint).
    Rubric: Names a SIFT move; Describes a specific action that fits the move
    Model answer: Find better coverage: I'd search for spring-flow data from the St. Johns River Water Management District or the U.S. Geological Survey to see what measurements show.
  3. The source says "so-called scientists claim flow is down." Explain how a reader can check whether that claim is true.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric (4 points toward the checkpoint).
    Rubric: Describes tracing the claim to original data or experts; Names a type of reliable source
    Model answer: I'd trace the claim to the original studies by looking for reports from the water management district or university scientists, then read their actual flow measurements instead of trusting the tube company's summary.

Checkpoint: Part B: Research skills

  1. "Florida" is a topic, not a research question. Write a strong research question about Florida and two sub-questions that would help answer it.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric (4 points toward the checkpoint).
    Rubric: An open, focused research question; Two sub-questions that each help answer it
    Model answer: How have invasive Burmese pythons changed the Everglades? Sub-questions: How did pythons get into the Everglades? Which native animals have declined since pythons spread?
  2. What do we call using someone else's words or ideas without giving credit, even by accident?
    Answer: plagiarism
    Cite every borrowed idea.
  3. Paraphrase this practice statement and add an in-text citation: "Burmese pythons have spread across thousands of square miles of South Florida, where they eat native mammals and birds" (U.S. Geological Survey).
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric (4 points toward the checkpoint).
    Rubric: Own words and sentence structure; Keeps the meaning; Includes an in-text citation
    Model answer: Across a huge area of South Florida, invasive pythons are eating native birds and mammals (U.S. Geological Survey).
  4. Name two things a presenter can do to help an audience understand and trust numbers from research.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric (4 points toward the checkpoint).
    Rubric: Two specific, valid techniques
    Model answer: Round and compare the number, like "about one-third," and name the source aloud, like "according to the U.S. Geological Survey."

Checkpoint: Part C: Write a synthesized paragraph

  1. Write one paragraph (5 to 7 sentences) that answers this question: Why are Florida's springs worth protecting? Use both practice sources below, connect them with transitions, cite each one, and add your own commentary. Source 1 (practice, adapted from Florida State Parks): Florida's springs stay about 68 to 72°F all year, giving manatees warm water in winter. Source 2 (practice, adapted from the Florida Geological Survey): Springs are windows into the Floridan aquifer, which supplies most of the state's drinking water.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric (8 points toward the checkpoint).
    Rubric: A topic sentence that answers the question; Accurate evidence from both sources with citations; Transitions that connect the sources; Commentary that explains why the evidence matters; Correct conventions
    Model answer: Florida's springs are worth protecting because they support both wildlife and people. Springs stay about 68 to 72°F all year, so they give manatees a warm place to survive cold winters (Florida State Parks). In addition, springs are windows into the Floridan aquifer, the source of most of the state's drinking water (Florida Geological Survey). This means that when a spring is polluted or loses flow, it's a warning sign about the water families drink every day. Protecting springs protects manatees, the aquifer and Floridians themselves.

Answer key: printable page

  1. List the four SIFT moves.
    Answer: Stop; investigate the source; find better coverage; trace claims to the original.
  2. Name three clues that a source may not be reliable.
    Answer: No author or expert; selling something; no date; no evidence; opinion presented as fact.
  3. Paraphrase with a citation: "Gopher tortoise burrows shelter more than 350 other species" (practice statement, adapted from FWC).
    Answer: Model: Hundreds of other kinds of animals use gopher tortoise burrows for shelter (FWC).
  4. What is the difference between a topic and a research question? Give an example.
    Answer: A topic is a subject; a research question is an open, focused question, such as How do springs help manatees survive winter?
  5. Write one specific strength and one specific suggestion for a presentation you watched this week.
    Answer: Model: Your map made the spread easy to see; next time, face the audience instead of the screen.
