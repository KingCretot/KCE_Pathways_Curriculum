Unit 6: Geometry and Data · Week 30 · Math · Teacher Plan

End-of-Year Review

Lesson length: 60 minutes live, plus the after-class interactive lesson

Objectives

  • Choose the right geometry tool: area, surface area or volume.
  • Choose and interpret the right data display and measure of center or spread.
  • Apply the year's money skills: unit prices, percents, integers, interest and budgets.
  • Plan a real summer business using math from all six units.
  • Demonstrate mastery of Unit 6 on the checkpoint (85 or higher).

Standards

  • MA.6.GR.2.1: Derive a formula for the area of a right triangle using a rectangle. Apply a formula to find the area of a triangle.
  • MA.6.GR.2.2: Solve mathematical and real-world problems involving the area of quadrilaterals and composite figures by decomposing them into triangles or rectangles.
  • MA.6.GR.2.3: Solve mathematical and real-world problems involving the volume of right rectangular prisms with positive rational number edge lengths using a visual model and a formula.
  • MA.6.GR.2.4: Given a mathematical or real-world context, find the surface area of right rectangular prisms and right rectangular pyramids using the figure's net.
  • MA.6.DP.1.1: Recognize and formulate a statistical question that would generate numerical data.
  • MA.6.DP.1.2: Given a numerical data set within a real-world context, find and interpret mean, median, mode and range.
  • MA.6.DP.1.3: Given a box plot within a real-world context, determine the minimum, the lower quartile, the median, the upper quartile and maximum. Use this summary of the data to describe the spread and distribution of the data.
  • MA.6.DP.1.5: Create a histogram or line plot to represent numerical data.
  • MA.6.DP.1.6: Given a real-world scenario, determine and describe how changes in data values impact measures of center and variation.
  • Financial literacy: spending, saving, budgeting and earning income (Jump$tart National Standards).

Materials

  • The interactive lesson
  • Paper and pencil, graph paper and a ruler, a measuring tape, grocery flyers or store websites, optional calculator
  • Printable practice page

Vocabulary

  • area: The number of square units that cover a flat figure.
  • surface area: The total area of all the faces of a solid.
  • volume: The number of cubic units that fill a solid.
  • statistical question: A question that expects answers that vary.
  • measure of center: One number that describes a typical value: the mean, median or mode.
  • interquartile range (IQR): Q3 minus Q1: the spread of the middle half of the data.

Key questions to ask

  • Am I covering, wrapping or filling?
  • Is the height perpendicular to the base?
  • Is there an outlier, and which measure handles it?
  • Which unit of the year does this money problem use?

Common mistakes to watch for

  • Surface area and volume use the same units.
  • The slanted side of a triangle is its height.
  • The mean is always the best measure of center.
  • A percent off and then tax on can be found by subtracting the percents.

Support (if the student is struggling)

  • Provide the toolkit chart as a reference card.
  • Use centimeter cubes or sugar cubes to rebuild volume.
  • Give a template for the business plan with one box per unit.

Challenge (if the student is ready for more)

  • Compare the surface areas of two boxes with the same volume and decide which uses less cardboard.
  • Find the mean absolute deviation for the lemonade data.

How the lesson runs

During the live lesson, the student shares their screen and answers the Reconnect questions and the Learn checks live while you teach. The Hands-On activity, Think Deeper and Exit Check are completed after class.

Scoring

The interactive lesson scores itself: full points on the first try, half points on the second try, then the answer and explanation appear. Mastery is 85. Written responses arrive in your results email with the score; score them with the rubric below before the student moves on.

Checkpoint scoring

Checkpoint questions are scored separately from the lesson. The student gets one try per question and the answers are not shown, so the checkpoint measures independent mastery and a retake stays fair. Your results email shows the auto-scored checkpoint points, a list of each item marked correct or MISSED, and the points available for the written items. Score the written items with their rubrics, then: checkpoint score = (auto points + rubric points) / total possible × 100. Mastery is 85. Below 85: teach a targeted review lesson on the parts missed (A to D), then the student retakes the checkpoint. Record both attempts in the Weekly Score Log.

Answer key: interactive lesson

Reconnect

  1. Week 26: A triangle has a base of 10 inches and a height of 7 inches. What is its area?
    Answer: 35 square inches
    ½ × 10 × 7 = 35.
  2. Week 27: What is the volume of a box 4 cm long, 3 cm wide and 5 cm tall?
    Answer: 60 cubic centimeters
    4 × 3 × 5 = 60.
  3. Week 28: You asked 30 people their ages and want to group the ages into intervals of 10 years. What display should you use?
    Answer: a histogram
    Histograms group data into equal intervals.
  4. Week 29: Find the median of 4, 9, 2, 7, 5.
    Answer: 5
    In order: 2, 4, 5, 7, 9.

Learn 1: Review: Area

  1. A parallelogram-shaped garden bed has a base of 12 feet and a height of 5 feet. What is its area?
    Answer: 60 square feet
    12 × 5 = 60.
  2. A stand's sign is a 10-inch by 6-inch rectangle with a triangle on top that has a 6-inch base and a 4-inch height. What is the sign's total area?
    Answer: 72 square inches
    60 + ½ × 6 × 4 = 60 + 12 = 72.

Learn 2: Review: Surface Area and Volume

  1. A gift box is 6 inches long, 4 inches wide and 2 inches tall. How much wrapping paper covers it exactly (its surface area)?
    Answer: 88 square inches
    2(24 + 12 + 8) = 88.
  2. A cooler is 2.5 feet long, 2 feet wide and 1.5 feet tall. What is its volume?
    Answer: 7.5 cubic feet
    2.5 × 2 × 1.5 = 7.5.
  3. For each job, tell whether you need area, surface area or volume, and why: (a) painting a wooden toy chest, (b) filling a sandbox, (c) buying carpet for a bedroom.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: A correct choice for each job; A reason for each choice
    Model answer: (a) Surface area, because paint covers every face of the chest. (b) Volume, because sand fills the space inside. (c) Area, because carpet covers one flat floor.

Learn 3: Review: Data

  1. Lemonade cups sold each day: 3, 5, 5, 6, 8, 9, 20. Find the mean.
    Answer: 8 cups
    56 ÷ 7 = 8.
  2. Find the median of the same data.
    Answer: 6 cups
    The 4th of 7 values.
  3. Which number better describes a typical day at the stand, the mean or the median? Explain.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Chooses a measure; Justifies it using the outlier
    Model answer: The median, 6 cups, because the 20-cup day (maybe a holiday) is an outlier that pulls the mean up to 8.

Learn 4: Money: End-of-Year Money Review

  1. A 12-ounce bag of lemons costs $3.60, and an 18-ounce bag costs $4.86. What is the unit price of the better buy, in dollars per ounce?
    Answer: 0.27 dollars per ounce
    3.60 ÷ 12 = 0.30; 4.86 ÷ 18 = 0.27; the 18-ounce bag is cheaper per ounce.
  2. A $24 shirt is 25% off, and then 6% sales tax is added. What is the final price?
    Answer: 19.08 dollars
    24 × 0.75 = 18; 18 × 1.06 = 19.08.
  3. Your business owes your parents $15 for supplies (−15). Then you earn $40. What is your balance now?
    Answer: 25 dollars
    −15 + 40 = 25.
  4. Your lemonade recipe makes 8 cups for $2.40 in supplies. You plan to sell each cup for $0.75. Find your cost per cup, your profit per cup and how many cups you must sell to earn $27 in profit. Show your work.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Correct cost per cup; Correct profit per cup; Correct number of cups with an equation or explanation
    Model answer: Cost per cup: 2.40 ÷ 8 = $0.30. Profit per cup: 0.75 − 0.30 = $0.45. Cups needed: 0.45c = 27, so c = 27 ÷ 0.45 = 60 cups.

Hands-On

  1. Share your business plan: supplies with unit prices, your price and profit per item, your sales-goal equation, one area or volume measurement and your survey results.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Supplies with correct unit prices; A price, profit per item and solved goal equation; An area or volume measurement with units; Survey results with a median and range
    Model answer: Lemonade stand. Supplies: lemons $0.27 per ounce, sugar $0.05 per ounce, cups $0.06 each; one batch makes 8 cups for $2.40. Price $0.75; profit $0.45 per cup. Goal $45: 0.45c = 45, so c = 100 cups. Sign: 24 in by 18 in = 432 square inches. Cooler: 2.5 × 2 × 1.5 = 7.5 cubic feet. Survey of 12 people: median price $0.75, range $0.75.
  2. Which unit's math was most useful for your plan, and what would you change after seeing your survey results?
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Names a unit with a reason; A change based on the survey data
    Model answer: Unit 3's percents and unit prices were most useful, because they decided whether I'd make a profit. My survey's median was $0.75, but three people would pay $1, so I might sell a large cup for $1.

Think Deeper

  1. Look back over the whole year: decimals, fractions and ratios, rates and percents, integers, expressions and equations, geometry and data. Which idea will you use most as an adult, and which was hardest for you? Give a real example for each and explain how you worked through the hard one.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Names an idea likely to be used as an adult, with a real example; Names the hardest idea, with an example; Explains a strategy that helped
    Model answer: I'll use percents most, because every store sale, tip and loan uses them; at a restaurant I can find a 20% tip by taking 10% and doubling it. The hardest was dividing fractions. A $12 roll of ribbon cut into 3/4-yard pieces confused me until I drew the yards and counted the pieces, which showed me why dividing by 3/4 gives more pieces than the number of yards.

Checkpoint: Part A: Area

  1. A triangle has a base of 14 cm and a height of 9 cm. What is its area?
    Answer: 63 square centimeters
    ½ × 14 × 9 = 63.
  2. A parallelogram has a base of 8.5 m and a height of 6 m. What is its area?
    Answer: 51 square meters
    8.5 × 6 = 51.
  3. A shed wall is a 12-foot by 8-foot rectangle with a triangle on top that has a 12-foot base and a 5-foot height. What is the wall's total area?
    Answer: 126 square feet
    96 + 30 = 126.
  4. Explain why the area of a triangle is ½ × base × height. Use a sketch described in words or a rectangle or parallelogram in your explanation.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric (4 points toward the checkpoint).
    Rubric: Relates the triangle to a rectangle or parallelogram with the same base and height; Explains why the triangle is half of it
    Model answer: Two copies of the same triangle fit together to make a parallelogram with the same base and height. The parallelogram's area is base × height, so one triangle is half of that: ½ × base × height.

Checkpoint: Part B: Surface area and volume

  1. Find the surface area of a rectangular prism 5 in long, 4 in wide and 3 in tall.
    Answer: 94 square inches
    2(20 + 15 + 12) = 94.
  2. Find the volume of a box 2.5 ft long, 4 ft wide and 6 ft tall.
    Answer: 60 cubic feet
    2.5 × 4 × 6 = 60.
  3. A cube has edges ½ foot long. What is its volume? Type a decimal.
    Answer: 0.125 cubic feet
    ½ × ½ × ½ = ⅛ = 0.125.
  4. A net of a square pyramid has how many squares and how many triangles?
    Answer: 1; 4
    One square base and four triangular faces.
  5. A shipping box is 12 inches long, 10 inches wide and 8 inches tall. You want to cover it completely in decorative paper. Tell whether you need surface area or volume, explain why, and find the amount.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric (4 points toward the checkpoint).
    Rubric: Chooses the correct measurement with a reason; Correct calculation with square units
    Model answer: Surface area, because the paper covers the outside faces. SA = 2(12 × 10 + 12 × 8 + 10 × 8) = 2(120 + 96 + 80) = 2(296) = 592 square inches.

Checkpoint: Part C: Statistical questions and displays

  1. "How tall am I?" is not a statistical question. Rewrite it so it is one, and explain what makes your new question statistical.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric (3 points toward the checkpoint).
    Rubric: A rewritten question that expects varied answers; Explains variability
    Model answer: "How tall are the students in my co-op?" It's statistical because the students have different heights, so the answers vary.
  2. A histogram shows books read this year by 20 students: 0 to 4 books: 3 students; 5 to 9: 7; 10 to 14: 6; 15 to 19: 4. How many students read 10 or more books?
    Answer: 10 students
    6 + 4 = 10.
  3. Using the same histogram, what percent of the students read fewer than 5 books?
    Answer: 15 percent
    3 ÷ 20 = 0.15 = 15%.
  4. To find the town's favorite sport, a student surveys only players at a soccer camp. Explain why the results may be biased and describe a fairer sample.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric (4 points toward the checkpoint).
    Rubric: Explains the bias; Describes a fairer sample
    Model answer: Soccer campers probably like soccer more than most people, so the results would favor soccer. A fairer sample would be people chosen at random from all over town, like every fifth shopper at the library and the grocery store.

Checkpoint: Part D: Measures of center and spread

  1. Find the mean of 12, 15, 9, 18, 16.
    Answer: 14
    70 ÷ 5 = 14.
  2. Find the median of 7, 3, 10, 4, 8, 12.
    Answer: 7.5
    In order: 3, 4, 7, 8, 10, 12; (7 + 8) ÷ 2 = 7.5.
  3. Find the interquartile range of 2, 5, 6, 8, 10, 13, 15.
    Answer: 8
    Median 8; Q1 = 5; Q3 = 13; 13 − 5 = 8.
  4. Five houses on a street sold for $250,000, $260,000, $270,000, $280,000 and $1,400,000. Find the mean and median, and explain which better describes a typical house on the street.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric (4 points toward the checkpoint).
    Rubric: Correct mean; Correct median; Chooses a measure and justifies it with the outlier
    Model answer: Mean: 2,460,000 ÷ 5 = $492,000. Median: $270,000. The median is better, because the $1,400,000 house is an outlier that pulls the mean above what four of the five houses sold for.

Answer key: printable page

  1. Find the area of a triangle with base 18 m and height 7 m.
    Answer: 63 square meters.
  2. Find the surface area and volume of a box 8 cm by 5 cm by 3 cm.
    Answer: SA = 2(40 + 24 + 15) = 158 square cm; V = 120 cubic cm.
  3. Data: 4, 6, 6, 7, 9, 10, 35. Find the mean, median and mode. Which measure best describes the data?
    Answer: Mean 11; median 7; mode 6; median, because 35 is an outlier.
  4. Find the five-number summary of 1, 3, 4, 6, 7, 9, 12.
    Answer: 1, 3, 6, 9, 12.
  5. A $40 jacket is 30% off, with 7% sales tax added. Find the final price.
    Answer: 40 × 0.70 = 28; 28 × 1.07 = $29.96.
