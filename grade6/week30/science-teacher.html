Unit 6: Heredity and Adaptation · Week 30 · Life Science · Teacher Plan

End-of-Year Review and Capstone Investigation

Lesson length: 60 minutes live, plus the after-class interactive lesson

Objectives

  • Explain how traits pass from parents to offspring and predict outcomes with Punnett squares.
  • Explain how adaptations arise through natural selection over generations.
  • Connect ideas across the year: cells, classification, body systems, ecosystems and heredity.
  • Design, carry out and report a fair-test capstone investigation.
  • Demonstrate mastery of Unit 6 on the checkpoint (85 or higher).

Standards

  • SC.7.L.16.1: Understand and explain that every organism requires a set of instructions that specifies its traits, that this hereditary information (DNA) contains genes located in the chromosomes of each cell, and that heredity is the passage of these instructions from one generation to another.
  • SC.7.L.16.2: Determine the probabilities for genotype and phenotype combinations using Punnett Squares and pedigrees.
  • SC.7.L.15.2: Explore the scientific theory of evolution by recognizing and explaining ways in which genetic variation and environmental factors contribute to evolution by natural selection and diversity of organisms.
  • SC.7.L.15.3: Explore the scientific theory of evolution by relating how the inability of a species to adapt within a changing environment may contribute to the extinction of that species.
  • SC.6.N.1.1: Define a problem from the sixth grade curriculum, use appropriate reference materials to support scientific understanding, plan and carry out scientific investigation of various types, such as systematic observations or experiments, identify variables, collect and organize data, interpret data in charts, tables, and graphics, analyze information, make predictions, and defend conclusions.

Materials

  • The interactive lesson
  • Science notebook, graph paper, a ruler, a metric ruler or measuring tape, a timer, and materials for the chosen capstone (such as bean seeds, cups, potting soil and water)
  • The Unit 6 project rubric
  • Printable practice page

Vocabulary

  • allele: One version of a gene, such as T for tall or t for short.
  • dominant: An allele whose trait shows whenever it is present.
  • recessive: An allele whose trait shows only when two copies are present.
  • genotype: The pair of alleles an organism has.
  • phenotype: The trait that actually shows.
  • natural selection: Individuals with helpful inherited traits survive and reproduce more, so those traits become more common over generations.
  • independent variable: The one factor a scientist changes on purpose in a fair test.

Key questions to ask

  • Which allele came from each parent?
  • Is each box a promise or a probability?
  • Was this trait inherited or developed during one lifetime?
  • What is the one thing you changed, and what did you measure?

Common mistakes to watch for

  • A Punnett square predicts exact numbers.
  • Dominant means more common.
  • Individuals evolve.
  • A single trial is enough to draw a conclusion.

Support (if the student is struggling)

  • Provide a blank Punnett square template.
  • Offer three pre-approved capstone questions to choose from.
  • Provide a data-table template with columns for each trial.

Challenge (if the student is ready for more)

  • Design a capstone with two dependent variables (for example, sprouting time and height).
  • Research a real Florida natural selection study and present its data.

Answer key: interactive lesson

Reconnect

  1. Week 26: Thread-like structures in the nucleus that carry genes are called ___.
    Answer: chromosomes
    Humans have 23 pairs.
  2. Week 27: In a Tt × tt cross, what percent of offspring are expected to be short (recessive)?
    Answer: 50 percent
    2 of 4 boxes are tt.
  3. Week 28: Classify each adaptation as structural, behavioral or physiological.
    Answer: structural; behavioral
    A body part; an inherited action.
  4. Week 29: Use V-I-S-T in one or two sentences to explain why Florida's Gulf Coast beach mice are pale.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Uses all four steps correctly
    Model answer: Mice varied in coat color, color is inherited, owls caught more dark mice on white sand, and over many generations most beach mice became pale.

Learn 1: Review: Traits, Genes and Punnett Squares

  1. Two pea plants that both carry one tall and one short allele (Tt × Tt) are crossed. Out of 100 offspring, about how many would you expect to be short?
    Answer: 25 plants
    1 of 4 boxes is tt; 25% of 100.
  2. Two brown-eyed parents have a blue-eyed child. Using the words dominant, recessive and allele, explain how this is possible. (Use the simple one-gene model from Week 27.)
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Explains that each parent carries a hidden recessive allele; Explains that the child received one from each parent
    Model answer: Each parent has one dominant brown allele and one hidden recessive blue allele. The child inherited the recessive blue allele from both parents, so with two copies, the blue trait shows. (Real eye color involves several genes, but this model shows how a recessive trait can skip a generation.)

Learn 2: Review: Adaptations and Natural Selection

  1. Burmese pythons now live in the Everglades. Predict how natural selection might change a native prey population, such as marsh rabbits, over many generations, if some rabbits are better at detecting or escaping pythons.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Applies variation and inheritance; Explains differential survival; Describes change over generations, not in individuals
    Model answer: Rabbits vary in how alert they are, and that alertness can be inherited. Rabbits that notice pythons sooner survive and have more babies. Over many generations, more of the population would be very alert. But if pythons eat rabbits faster than they can reproduce, the population could disappear first.

Learn 3: The Year in Review: The Science Practices

  1. A student tests whether fertilizer amount affects bean plant height. Name the independent variable, the dependent variable and two controlled variables.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Correct independent variable; Correct dependent variable; Two reasonable controlled variables
    Model answer: Independent: the amount of fertilizer. Dependent: plant height. Controlled: the amount of water and the amount of sunlight (also the type of bean and pot size).
  2. Choose two units from this year and explain one connection between them.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Two units named; A specific, accurate connection
    Model answer: Unit 2 and Unit 6: genes are stored on chromosomes inside the nucleus of every cell, so the cell parts I studied in Unit 2 hold the instructions for traits in Unit 6.

Hands-On

  1. Share your question, hypothesis, variables and data table (or a summary of it).
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: A testable question and hypothesis; Independent, dependent and controlled variables; Data from at least three trials per condition
    Model answer: Question: Does light affect how fast bean seeds sprout? Hypothesis: Seeds in the dark will sprout at about the same speed, because seeds use stored food. Independent: light or dark. Dependent: days to sprout. Controlled: same bean type, soil, water and temperature. Data: light cups sprouted in 4, 5 and 4 days (mean 4.3); dark cups in 4, 4 and 5 days (mean 4.3).
  2. What is your conclusion? Support it with your data, and name one source of error and one new question.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: A conclusion supported by data; A specific source of error; A new testable question
    Model answer: Light didn't affect sprouting speed: both groups averaged 4.3 days, which supports my hypothesis. One source of error was that the dark closet might have been cooler than the windowsill. Next I'd test whether light affects how tall seedlings grow after sprouting.

Think Deeper

  1. Which science idea from this year changed how you see the living world around you? Describe something you now notice or understand in your own backyard, a park or your own body that you didn't before, and connect it to at least two units.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Names a specific idea and an example from the student's world; Connects to at least two units accurately; Reflects on how the student's thinking changed
    Model answer: Learning about adaptations changed how I see our backyard. Now when I see an anole change from green to brown, I know it's a physiological adaptation for camouflage (Unit 6), and I know it's a consumer eating insects in our yard's food web (Unit 5). I also notice that the anoles on our fence look different from the ones in our trees, and I wonder whether natural selection is at work, like the green anoles in Mosquito Lagoon.

Checkpoint: Part A: Traits and inheritance

  1. Each body cell in a human has how many chromosomes?
    Answer: 46 (23 pairs)
    One of each pair comes from each parent.
  2. Explain why a child resembles both parents but is not identical to either one.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric (3 points toward the checkpoint).
    Rubric: Explains that half the alleles come from each parent; Explains that the combination is new
    Model answer: A child gets half of its chromosomes and alleles from each parent, so it shares traits with both, but the mix of alleles is a new combination that neither parent has.
  3. Identical twins share the same genes. One grows up playing outdoor sports and the other mostly indoors. Name one trait that could differ between them and explain why.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric (4 points toward the checkpoint).
    Rubric: Names a trait that the environment can affect; Explains the role of the environment
    Model answer: Their muscle strength or skin tone could differ, because exercise and sunlight affect the body even when the genes are the same.

Checkpoint: Part B: Punnett squares

  1. In guinea pigs, black fur (B) is dominant over white fur (b). A Bb guinea pig is crossed with a bb guinea pig. What percent of offspring are expected to have white fur?
    Answer: 50 percent
    Two of four boxes are bb.
  2. In the same species, two Bb guinea pigs are crossed. How many of the four Punnett-square boxes would show black fur?
    Answer: 3 boxes
    BB, Bb and Bb are all black.
  3. A Bb × Bb cross predicts 25% white offspring, but a litter of 4 pups has no white pups. Does this prove the Punnett square is wrong? Explain.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric (4 points toward the checkpoint).
    Rubric: Clear decision; Explains that each box is a probability for each offspring; Explains that small samples can differ from predictions
    Model answer: No. A Punnett square shows the chance for each pup, not a promise. With only 4 pups, it's like flipping a coin 4 times and not getting exactly 2 heads. With many more pups, about 25% would be white.

Checkpoint: Part C: Adaptations

  1. Classify each adaptation.
    Answer: structural; behavioral; physiological
    A body part; an inherited action; a body process.
  2. Explain how camouflage helps both a predator and its prey. Give one Florida example.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric (4 points toward the checkpoint).
    Rubric: Explains the benefit to prey; Explains the benefit to predators; A valid Florida example
    Model answer: Camouflage helps prey hide from predators and helps predators sneak up on prey. A green anole blends into leaves to hide from birds, and an alligator lying still in dark water looks like a log until prey comes close.
  3. A dog learns to open a gate latch. Explain why this is not an adaptation in the scientific sense.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric (3 points toward the checkpoint).
    Rubric: Explains that it is learned during one lifetime; Explains that it is not inherited
    Model answer: The dog learned it during its own life, and the skill isn't carried in its genes, so its puppies won't inherit it.

Checkpoint: Part D: Natural selection and extinction

  1. Bacteria in an infection vary in how well they resist an antibiotic. Use all four steps of natural selection to explain how a population of resistant bacteria can develop.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric (5 points toward the checkpoint).
    Rubric: Variation; Inheritance; Selection; Time (change over generations)
    Model answer: The bacteria vary: a few can resist the antibiotic. Resistance is passed on when bacteria divide. The antibiotic kills the others, so the resistant ones survive and multiply. Over many generations, most of the population becomes resistant.
  2. What kind of preserved evidence shows that organisms like mammoths and giant ground sloths once lived in Florida?
    Answer: fossils
    Remains or traces preserved in rock or sediment.
  3. Use the Florida panther to explain why a population with very little variation is at greater risk of extinction, and what scientists did about it.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric (4 points toward the checkpoint).
    Rubric: Explains the risk of low variation; Describes the 1995 action; Explains why it helped
    Model answer: With only 20 to 30 panthers left, there was little variation, so harmful traits like heart defects were common and the population couldn't adapt to new problems. In 1995 scientists released eight Texas pumas, adding new variation, and the population grew healthier and larger.

Answer key: printable page

  1. Define dominant and recessive alleles.
    Answer: Dominant shows whenever present; recessive shows only with two copies.
  2. Draw a Punnett square for Rr × rr. What percent of offspring show the recessive trait?
    Answer: Rr, Rr, rr, rr; 50%.
  3. Explain the difference between an inherited adaptation and a learned behavior, with an example of each.
    Answer: Inherited: passed in genes, like a spoonbill's bill; learned: in one lifetime, like a dog opening a latch.
  4. Explain natural selection with V-I-S-T.
    Answer: Variation; inheritance; selection (helpful traits survive and reproduce more); time (traits become common over generations).
  5. Name the independent, dependent and controlled variables in your capstone.
    Answer: Answers vary; one changed factor, one measured result, factors kept the same.
