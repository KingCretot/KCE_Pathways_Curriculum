Unit 6: The Americas and the Meeting of Worlds · Week 30 · World and American History · Teacher Plan

End-of-Year Review: The Atlantic World Begins

Lesson length: 60 minutes live, plus the after-class interactive lesson

Objectives

  • Compare the civilizations of the Americas studied in Unit 6.
  • Explain how 1492 and the Columbian Exchange connected Europe, Africa and the Americas.
  • Describe the founding of St. Augustine and the early Atlantic world, including the presence of Africans and the Timucua.
  • Trace big themes (geography, trade, writing, belief, power) across the year.
  • Demonstrate mastery of Unit 6 on the checkpoint (85 or higher).

Standards

  • SS.6.W.1.3: Interpret primary and secondary sources.
  • SS.6.W.1.4: Describe the methods of historical inquiry and how history relates to the other social sciences.
  • SS.6.W.1.6: Describe how history transmits culture and heritage and provides models of human character.
  • SS.6.G.2.1: Explain how major physical characteristics, natural resources, climate, and absolute and relative locations have influenced settlement, interactions, and the economies of ancient civilizations of the world.
  • SS.6.G.2.6: Explain the concept of cultural diffusion, and identify the influences of different ancient cultures on one another.
  • SS.6.E.3.4: Describe the relationship among civilizations that engage in trade, including the benefits and drawbacks of voluntary trade.

Materials

  • The interactive lesson
  • About 25 index cards, markers or colored pencils, string or painter's tape, clothespins or tape, a meterstick or measuring tape, a world map or atlas, the student's notes
  • Printable practice page

Vocabulary

  • Atlantic world: The connected lands and peoples around the Atlantic Ocean (Europe, Africa and the Americas) after 1492.
  • colony: A settlement or territory ruled by a distant country.
  • presidio: A Spanish fort and military settlement.
  • Columbian Exchange: The transfer of plants, animals, people and diseases between the hemispheres after 1492.
  • Atlantic slave trade: The forced transport of millions of Africans to the Americas, where they were enslaved.
  • theme: A big idea that appears again and again across history.

Key questions to ask

  • Whose land was this, and whose voices are in the sources?
  • How did geography shape this civilization?
  • What crossed the Atlantic, in which direction, and was it voluntary?
  • Which theme connects these civilizations?

Common mistakes to watch for

  • American history begins with Columbus or the English colonies.
  • Indigenous peoples of Florida and the Caribbean disappeared completely.
  • Africans arrived in Florida only as enslaved people under the English.
  • The Columbian Exchange was a fair trade between equals.

Support (if the student is struggling)

  • Provide a timeline template with the year's units pre-marked.
  • Offer a word bank for the checkpoint's short answers.
  • Review the Columbus passage aloud together before the checkpoint.

Challenge (if the student is ready for more)

  • Research Fort Mose and write a museum label for it.
  • Compare St. Augustine (1565) with Jamestown (1607) and Santa Fe (about 1610).

Background for the teacher

St. Augustine was founded September 8, 1565, by Pedro Menéndez de Avilés near the Timucua town of Seloy; the National Park Service describes it as the oldest continuously occupied European-established settlement in the continental United States. Juan Garrido, a free African conquistador, accompanied Juan Ponce de León. Castillo de San Marcos construction began in 1672. Fort Mose (Gracia Real de Santa Teresa de Mose) was established in 1738. The Columbus passage repeats the verified Markham (1893) translation used in Week 29. Unit 6's pre-1492 Americas content extends beyond Florida's Grade 6 NGSSS (ancient world focus); KCE teaches it here per the Grade 6 scope. Verify standard wording against CPALMS before release.

How the lesson runs

During the live lesson, the student shares their screen and answers the Reconnect questions and the Learn checks live while you teach. The Hands-On activity, Think Deeper and Exit Check are completed after class.

Scoring

The interactive lesson scores itself: full points on the first try, half points on the second try, then the answer and explanation appear. Mastery is 85. Written responses arrive in your results email with the score; score them with the rubric below before the student moves on.

Checkpoint scoring

Checkpoint questions are scored separately from the lesson. The student gets one try per question and the answers are not shown, so the checkpoint measures independent mastery and a retake stays fair. Your results email shows the auto-scored checkpoint points, a list of each item marked correct or MISSED, and the points available for the written items. Score the written items with their rubrics, then: checkpoint score = (auto points + rubric points) / total possible × 100. Mastery is 85. Below 85: teach a targeted review lesson on the parts missed (A to D), then the student retakes the checkpoint. Record both attempts in the Weekly Score Log.

Answer key: interactive lesson

Reconnect

  1. Week 26: The Maya were among the first people in the world to use a symbol for what number?
    Answer: zero
    The Maya used a shell-shaped glyph for zero.
  2. Week 27: What was the capital city of the Mexica, built on an island in a lake?
    Answer: Tenochtitlan
    Today's Mexico City stands on its site.
  3. Week 28: How many Seminole Wars did the United States fight?
    Answer: 3 wars
    1817 to 1818, 1835 to 1842 and 1855 to 1858.
  4. Week 29: Which historian gave the Columbian Exchange its name?
    Answer: Alfred Crosby
    In his 1972 book.

Learn 1: Review: Civilizations of the Americas

  1. Choose two of these civilizations and explain how each one adapted to its environment.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Two civilizations named; An accurate environmental adaptation for each
    Model answer: The Mexica built chinampas, floating garden beds, to farm the shallow lake around Tenochtitlan. The Inca carved terraces into steep mountainsides and built roads and rope bridges to connect their empire across the Andes.

Learn 2: Review: Florida and 1492

  1. Give one positive and one devastating effect of the Columbian Exchange, and explain why historians say it changed the whole world.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: A positive effect; A devastating effect; Explains the global reach
    Model answer: Potatoes and maize fed growing populations in Europe, Africa and Asia. But smallpox and other diseases killed millions of Indigenous Americans, and the demand for sugar workers drove the enslavement of millions of Africans. It changed the whole world because foods, animals and people moved to every continent.

Learn 3: The Atlantic World Begins: St. Augustine

  1. Why is it inaccurate to tell the story of St. Augustine as only a Spanish story? Use at least two groups from the lesson.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Names at least two groups besides the Spanish; Explains each group's role accurately
    Model answer: The town was built next to the Timucua town of Seloy, on Timucua land, and the Timucua traded with and resisted the Spanish. Africans, both free and enslaved, helped build and defend it from the beginning, and Fort Mose later became a free Black town nearby.
  2. How many years after Columbus's 1492 voyage was St. Augustine founded?
    Answer: 73 years
    1565 − 1492 = 73.

Learn 4: The Year in Review: Big Themes

  1. Choose one big theme and trace it through three civilizations from different units this year.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Names a theme; Three civilizations from different units; Accurate example for each
    Model answer: Trade: the Silk Road carried Chinese silk west (Unit 3); Mali grew rich from gold and salt crossing the Sahara (Unit 5); and the Columbian Exchange moved crops and animals across the Atlantic (Unit 6). Each time, trade brought wealth and new ideas, but also disease or conflict.

Hands-On

  1. List at least eight cards on your timeline in order, with dates. Which part of the timeline was most crowded, and why?
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: At least eight events in correct order with dates; Explains the crowded section
    Model answer: Early humans in Africa (about 300,000 years ago); Mesopotamian writing (about 3200 BCE); Egypt's pyramids (about 2500 BCE); Kush rules Egypt (about 750 BCE); Rome becomes an empire (27 BCE); Mansa Musa's pilgrimage (1324); Tenochtitlan founded (1325); St. Augustine (1565). The last 5,000 years were crowded, because writing began then, so we have many more records.
  2. What big theme did you highlight on your tour, and which three cards did you connect?
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: A theme; Three accurately connected cards
    Model answer: Writing and knowledge: Mesopotamian cuneiform, Timbuktu's manuscripts and Maya glyphs all show people recording laws, science and history so later generations could learn from them.

Think Deeper

  1. This year you studied history from many points of view: African, Asian, European and Indigenous American. Explain why it matters to learn history from many perspectives, using at least two specific examples from the year where one perspective alone would have given an incomplete or misleading picture.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Explains why multiple perspectives matter; Two specific, accurate examples from the year; Explains what a single perspective would miss
    Model answer: Learning from many perspectives shows what really happened instead of only what the most powerful people wrote down. If we read only Columbus's journal, we'd think the Taíno had "no religion," but evidence of their zemí shows he was wrong. If we learned only Roman sources about Carthage, we'd see Carthage mainly as Rome's enemy, not as a great trading city in North Africa. Hearing many voices helps me judge sources and understand people as they saw themselves.

Checkpoint: Part A: The Olmec and the Maya

  1. Name one Olmec center where colossal stone heads have been found.
    Answer: San Lorenzo, La Venta or Tres Zapotes
    All along Mexico's Gulf Coast.
  2. What is the Maya number with one dot in the twenties place and one bar in the ones place?
    Answer: 25
    1 × 20 + 5 = 25.
  3. Explain how we know the Maya were not one empire, and why that matters for understanding their "decline."
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric (4 points toward the checkpoint).
    Rubric: Explains independent, competing city-states; Explains that some cities declined while Maya people and culture continued
    Model answer: The Maya lived in many independent city-states, like Tikal and Calakmul, with their own kings who often fought each other. So the "decline" meant some southern cities were abandoned, not that the Maya disappeared; millions of Maya people still live in Mexico and Central America today.

Checkpoint: Part B: The Mexica, the Inca and Cahokia

  1. What was the Inca road system called?
    Answer: the Qhapaq Ñan
    It stretched thousands of miles through the Andes.
  2. What is Cahokia's largest earthen mound called?
    Answer: Monks Mound
    The largest earthen mound in the Americas north of Mexico.
  3. A few hundred Spanish soldiers led by Hernán Cortés defeated the powerful Mexica Empire. Explain at least two reasons, using evidence from Week 27.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric (5 points toward the checkpoint).
    Rubric: Two accurate reasons; Evidence from the lesson for each
    Model answer: Thousands of Indigenous allies, especially the Tlaxcalans, who had never been conquered by the Mexica and resented their power, fought alongside the Spanish. Smallpox also swept through Tenochtitlan in 1520, before the final siege of 1521, killing many people, including the ruler Cuitláhuac.

Checkpoint: Part C: Indigenous Peoples of Florida

  1. Name Florida's two federally recognized tribes today.
    Answer: the Seminole Tribe of Florida and the Miccosukee Tribe of Indians of Florida
    Recognized in 1957 and 1962.
  2. Which southwest Florida nation built a powerful chiefdom from fishing and gathering, without farming?
    Answer: the Calusa
    Estuaries provided rich food.
  3. Explain two causes of the destruction of Florida's original nations after European arrival.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric (4 points toward the checkpoint).
    Rubric: Two accurate causes; An explanation of each
    Model answer: European diseases like smallpox killed huge numbers of people who had no prior exposure, often before they even met Europeans. Then in the early 1700s, raids by English Carolina colonists and their allies destroyed missions and carried thousands of Apalachee and Timucua people away into slavery.

Checkpoint: Part D: 1492 and the Columbian Exchange

  1. Primary source: Christopher Columbus, journal entry headed October 11, 1492, describing the landing on October 12, as abstracted by Bartolomé de las Casas and translated by Clements R. Markham (1893): "They should be good servants and intelligent, for I observed that they quickly took in what was said to them, and I believe that they would easily be made Christians, as it appeared to me that they had no religion." What does this passage reveal about Columbus's goals and assumptions? Identify one goal and one assumption, and explain how knowing his audience helps a historian read it.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric (5 points toward the checkpoint).
    Rubric: Identifies a goal shown in the passage; Identifies an assumption shown in the passage; Explains the role of his audience
    Model answer: His goal was to use the Taíno as servants and convert them, which would bring Spain labor and converts. He assumed they had no religion just because he didn't see one he recognized, which was wrong. He was writing to the Spanish monarchs who paid for his voyage, so he wanted to make the islands sound valuable.
  2. Name one living thing or good that crossed from the Americas to Afro-Eurasia, and one that crossed from Afro-Eurasia to the Americas.
    Answer: maize, potatoes, tomatoes, cacao (from); wheat, sugarcane, horses, cattle, smallpox (to)
    The exchange went both ways.
  3. Explain how the collapse of the Taíno population and the growth of sugar plantations led to the Atlantic slave trade.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric (4 points toward the checkpoint).
    Rubric: Connects Taíno deaths to a labor shortage; Connects sugar plantations to demand for labor; Explains the forced transport of Africans
    Model answer: Forced labor and disease killed most of the Taíno, so Spanish colonists lost the workers they had been forcing to labor. As sugar plantations grew, colonists brought enslaved Africans across the Atlantic by force to do the work, and the trade grew to about 12.5 million people.

Answer key: printable page

  1. Compare the Maya and the Inca: name one achievement of each.
    Answer: Maya: writing, base-20 numbers with zero, calendars; Inca: roads, terraces, quipus.
  2. Why did Spain found St. Augustine in 1565?
    Answer: To protect its treasure fleets and drive out the French at Fort Caroline.
  3. Name three groups whose stories are part of early St. Augustine.
    Answer: The Timucua, Africans (free and enslaved) and the Spanish.
  4. Give one example of the Columbian Exchange in each direction.
    Answer: Potatoes to Europe; horses to the Americas (other answers possible).
  5. Choose one big theme from the year and give an example from two different units.
    Answer: Trade: the Silk Road carried silk west from China (Unit 3), and Mali grew rich from trans-Saharan gold and salt (Unit 5).
