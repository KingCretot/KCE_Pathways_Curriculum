Unit 6: Research and Presentation · Week 29 · ELA · Teacher Plan

Speaking and Presenting

Lesson length: 60 minutes live, plus the after-class interactive lesson

Objectives

  • Adapt a written report into a 4- to 6-minute presentation for a specific audience.
  • Design slides or posters that follow principles of clear visual design.
  • Explain data and visuals aloud and cite sources while speaking.
  • Use delivery skills (eye contact, volume, pace, gestures) and respond to audience questions.
  • Give and use specific feedback to improve a presentation.

Standards

  • ELA.6.C.2.1: Present information orally, in a logical sequence, emphasizing key points that support the central idea.
  • ELA.6.C.5.1: Integrate diverse digital media to build cohesion in a presentation.
  • ELA.6.C.4.1: Conduct research to answer a question, drawing on multiple reliable and valid sources, and refocusing the inquiry when appropriate.
  • ELA.K12.EE.1.1: Cite evidence to explain and justify reasoning.

Materials

  • The interactive lesson
  • The student's report draft
  • Slide software or poster board and markers
  • 5 to 8 index cards, a timer, optional recording device
  • Printable practice page

Vocabulary

  • audience: The people listening to or reading your work.
  • visual aid: A slide, poster, chart, map or object that helps an audience understand your message.
  • oral citation: Naming a source aloud, such as "According to the U.S. Geological Survey..."
  • pace: How fast or slow you speak.
  • signpost: A phrase that tells the audience where you are in a talk, such as "My second finding is..."
  • Q&A: The question-and-answer time after a presentation.

Lesson flow

Key questions to ask

  • Who is your audience, and what should they remember?
  • Does each slide have one idea and a source?
  • How will you make this number understandable?
  • What will you say if you don't know an answer?

Common mistakes to watch for

  • A presentation is the report read aloud.
  • More text on slides is more helpful.
  • Admitting you don't know looks bad.
  • Any image online is free to use.

Support (if the student is struggling)

  • Provide a slide template with headline, visual and source boxes.
  • Let the student rehearse first to a pet or stuffed animal, then a person.
  • Provide note-card frames for each section.

Challenge (if the student is ready for more)

  • Add a short demonstration or object to the presentation.
  • Present to an audience outside the family, such as a co-op class or library group.

Background for the teacher

The final research presentation is delivered in Week 30 and scored with the Unit 6 project rubric. Week 20 introduced speech delivery (eye contact, volume, pauses, note cards); this week builds research-presentation skills: audience analysis, visual design, speaking with data, oral citations and Q&A. Verify ELA.6.C.2.1 and C.5.1 wording against CPALMS before release.

How the lesson runs

During the live lesson, the student shares their screen and answers the Reconnect questions and the Learn checks live while you teach. The Hands-On activity, Think Deeper and Exit Check are completed after class.

Scoring

The interactive lesson scores itself: full points on the first try, half points on the second try, then the answer and explanation appear. Mastery is 85. Written responses arrive in your results email with the score; score them with the rubric below before the student moves on.

Answer key: interactive lesson

Reconnect

  1. What do we call combining information from several sources into one new understanding?
    Answer: synthesizing
    Not just listing sources.
  2. Write the three main points (section topic sentences) of your research report.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Three main points that answer the research question
    Model answer: 1. Fertilizer runoff and warm water feed the algae. 2. Water releases carry the algae to both coasts, harming people and pets. 3. A new reservoir and Everglades restoration could help stop the blooms.
  3. In Week 20 you delivered an argument speech. Name one delivery skill you did well and one you want to improve this time.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: One strength; One goal
    Model answer: I made good eye contact in Week 20, but I talked too fast when I got nervous, so this time I want to slow down and pause after key facts.

Learn 1: From Report to Talk: Know Your Audience

  1. Explain two changes you would make to your presentation if your audience were 8-year-olds instead of adults.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Two specific changes; Each tied to what young listeners need
    Model answer: I would replace words like phosphorus with "fertilizer that feeds algae" and use a picture of green water instead of a data chart, because younger kids need simpler words and pictures they can understand quickly.
  2. Write the three signpost sentences you'll use to introduce your three findings.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Three signposts that name each finding
    Model answer: My first finding is what feeds the algae. Next, I'll show how the algae reach our beaches. Finally, I'll share what scientists are doing to stop it.

Learn 2: Designing Clear Visuals

  1. A classmate's slide has the title "Manatees," three paragraphs of small text and a photo copied from a news website with no credit. Give three specific fixes.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Three specific, correct fixes
    Model answer: Change the title to a headline that states the idea, like "Manatees need warm water to survive winter." Cut the paragraphs to a few key words and say the rest aloud. Replace the photo with one that's free to use, or credit it, and add a source line.
  2. Which kind of visual (photo, map, bar graph, line graph, dot plot or diagram) would best show each: (a) how Silver Springs' flow changed from 1940 to 2020, (b) where algae blooms spread, (c) how a spring is connected to the aquifer?
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: A sensible visual for each with a reason
    Model answer: (a) A line graph, because it shows change over time. (b) A map, because it shows places. (c) A diagram, because it shows how parts connect underground.

Learn 3: Speaking with Data and Sources

  1. Rewrite this sentence so it works better when spoken aloud, with an oral citation: "Flow declined 32.4% from the 1933-2010 mean."
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Rounds or simplifies the number; Adds an oral citation; Easy to understand when heard
    Model answer: According to the U.S. Geological Survey, Silver Springs now flows about one-third less than it used to.

Learn 4: Delivery and Questions

  1. During your Q&A, someone asks a question you can't answer. Write exactly what you would say.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Honest about not knowing; Suggests where the answer might be found
    Model answer: That's a great question. I didn't find that in my research, but I'd check the South Florida Water Management District's reports, and I can look it up after the presentation.
  2. Turn this vague feedback into specific feedback: "It was good but kind of boring."
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Names something specific that worked; Names a specific, actionable change
    Model answer: Your map of where the algae spread was really clear. To make it more engaging, you could open with the story of the beach that closed instead of starting with numbers.

Hands-On

  1. How long was your first rehearsal and your last? List the feedback you received and the changes you made.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Both times reported; Specific feedback listed; Specific changes described
    Model answer: My first rehearsal was 7 minutes 40 seconds and my last was 5 minutes 15 seconds. My mom said my map was clear but I read the slides word for word and said "um" a lot. I cut the slide text to five words each, practiced with note cards, and paused instead of saying "um."
  2. What two questions did your audience ask, and how did you answer them? Was there one you couldn't answer, and what did you say?
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Two questions with answers; Honest handling of a question that couldn't be answered, or a note that all were answered
    Model answer: My dad asked whether the algae can hurt dogs; I said yes, and that the Department of Health warns pet owners. My sister asked whether it happens every year; I wasn't sure, so I said blooms happen most summers but I'd need to check the exact years.

Think Deeper

  1. What makes an audience trust a speaker who is presenting research? Give at least three things, and explain which matters most.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Three things that build trust; Explains which matters most and why
    Model answer: Citing reliable sources aloud, showing clear data with sources, and admitting what you don't know all build trust. I think citing sources matters most, because like we learned in Week 27, people should be able to check where information came from instead of just believing the speaker.
  2. Think of a speaker you've heard (a pastor, a teacher, a museum guide, a documentary narrator, a family member). What did they do that kept you listening, and how will you use one of those techniques?
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Names a speaker and specific techniques; Explains how the student will use one
    Model answer: Our museum guide at the Florida Museum told a story about one fossil before giving facts, and she asked us questions. I'll open my presentation with the story of the beach closing and ask my audience whether they've ever seen green water.

Exit Check

  1. Write one signpost phrase you could use in a presentation.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: A phrase that tells listeners where they are in the talk
    Model answer: My second finding is...
  2. Name two rules for a clear slide.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Two valid rules from the lesson
    Model answer: One idea per slide, and big readable text with a source line.
  3. Write an oral citation for a fact from your research.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Names the source aloud with the fact
    Model answer: According to the Florida Department of Health, algae blooms can make pets sick.

Answer key: printable page

  1. Write your hook and research question as you'll say them.
    Answer: Model: In 2018, families couldn't swim at their beaches because of thick green algae. Why do these blooms keep spreading, and what could stop them?
  2. List your three findings and the visual you'll use for each.
    Answer: Model: causes (diagram of runoff), spread (map of releases), solutions (photo of the reservoir site).
  3. Write one sentence that explains a number from your research in a way listeners will remember.
    Answer: Model: Silver Springs now flows about one-third less than it did in the 1930s, according to the U.S. Geological Survey.
  4. List three rules for clear slides.
    Answer: One idea per slide; big readable text; a source line on every slide with outside data or images.
  5. Write what you'll say if someone asks a question you can't answer.
    Answer: Model: I didn't find that in my research, but I'd look in the water district's reports.
