Unit 6: The Americas and the Meeting of Worlds · Week 29 · World and American History · Teacher Plan

1492, the Taíno and the Columbian Exchange

Lesson length: 60 minutes live, plus the after-class interactive lesson

Objectives

  • Describe the Taíno and their Caribbean societies before 1492.
  • Analyze Columbus's journal as a primary source, identifying his goals and assumptions.
  • Explain the causes and catastrophic effects of disease, forced labor and conquest on the Taíno.
  • Trace the plants, animals, diseases and people that crossed the Atlantic in the Columbian Exchange.
  • Evaluate the long-term effects of 1492, including the Atlantic slave trade, and recognize Taíno descendants today.

Standards

  • SS.6.W.1.3: Interpret primary and secondary sources.
  • SS.6.W.1.5: Describe the roles of historians and recognize varying historical interpretations (historiography).
  • SS.6.G.2.6: Explain the concept of cultural diffusion, and identify the influences of different ancient cultures on one another.
  • SS.6.E.3.4: Describe the relationship among civilizations that engage in trade, including the benefits and drawbacks of voluntary trade.

Materials

  • The interactive lesson
  • Kitchen access (with permission), paper and pencil, a world map or atlas, colored pencils or sticky dots
  • Printable practice page

Vocabulary

  • Taíno: Indigenous Arawakan-speaking peoples of the Greater Antilles and the Bahamas (Lucayans) when Columbus arrived.
  • cacique: A Taíno chief.
  • Columbian Exchange: The transfer of plants, animals, diseases, people and ideas between the Eastern and Western Hemispheres after 1492.
  • encomienda: A Spanish system that granted colonists the labor of Indigenous people, which in practice was a form of forced labor.
  • epidemic: A disease that spreads quickly and affects many people in an area.
  • Atlantic slave trade: The forced transport of millions of Africans across the Atlantic to be enslaved in the Americas.

Key questions to ask

  • What did Columbus notice, assume and want?
  • Who benefited and who suffered from the exchange?
  • How did disease and forced labor work together?
  • How do Taíno people and cultures continue today?

Common mistakes to watch for

  • Columbus discovered an empty land.
  • The Taíno had no religion or government.
  • The Taíno simply vanished.
  • The Columbian Exchange was an equal, voluntary trade.

Support (if the student is struggling)

  • Read the journal excerpts aloud together and underline assumptions.
  • Provide a two-column exchange chart.
  • Pre-teach encomienda and epidemic.

Challenge (if the student is ready for more)

  • Read a short excerpt from Bartolomé de las Casas and compare his view with Columbus's.
  • Research how horses transformed life for the Comanche or Lakota.

Background for the teacher

Columbus's journal survives only in Las Casas's abstract; the excerpts are from Clements R. Markham's 1893 Hakluyt Society translation (entry for October 11 to 12), checked against the scanned text. Guanahani's location in the Bahamas is debated (San Salvador is the most common identification). Estimates of Hispaniola's 1492 population range from about 100,000 to several million. Smallpox reached Hispaniola by about 1518 to 1519. Enriquillo's rebellion lasted from about 1519 to 1533. The figure of about 12.5 million Africans embarked comes from the Trans-Atlantic Slave Trade Database (SlaveVoyages). Genetic studies (for example, Schroeder and others, 2018, PNAS) find Indigenous Caribbean ancestry in many Puerto Ricans and other Caribbean people today. Florida's Grade 6 NGSSS world history range ends before 1492; the standards above cover the skills taught.

Sources

  • Columbus, Christopher, The Journal of Christopher Columbus, trans. Clements R. Markham (Hakluyt Society, 1893).
  • Crosby, Alfred W., The Columbian Exchange (1972).
  • Smithsonian National Museum of the American Indian, Taíno: Native Heritage and Identity in the Caribbean (2018 exhibition).
  • SlaveVoyages, Trans-Atlantic Slave Trade Database.

Answer key: interactive lesson

Reconnect

  1. Which Seminole leader was captured under a flag of truce in 1837?
    Answer: Osceola
    During the Second Seminole War.
  2. Which Florida nation built a powerful chiefdom without farming?
    Answer: the Calusa
    Estuaries fed them.
  3. Juan Ponce de León reached Florida in 1513. How many years after 1492 was that?
    Answer: 21 years
    1513 − 1492 = 21.
  4. Last week you learned that European diseases devastated Florida's nations, often before people even met Europeans. How could a disease travel ahead of the people who brought it?
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Explains spread through trade and contact between Native communities
    Model answer: Native traders and travelers who met Europeans could carry the disease home and pass it along trade routes to towns that had never seen a European.

Learn 1: The Taíno World

  1. What was a Taíno chief called?
    Answer: a cacique
    Caciques could be men or women.
  2. Hurricane, hammock and canoe come from Taíno words. What does this tell historians about how Europeans encountered the Caribbean?
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Europeans learned about new things from the Taíno; Borrowing words shows Taíno knowledge and cultural influence
    Model answer: Europeans had no words for hurricanes, hammocks or canoes, so they learned them from the Taíno along with the things themselves. It shows that Europeans depended on Taíno knowledge of the Caribbean.

Learn 2: Reading Columbus Critically

  1. Columbus wrote that the Taíno "should be good servants." What does this reveal about his goals, and how does knowing his audience (the Spanish monarchs) help explain it?
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Explains the goal revealed (using Native people for labor, profit, conversion); Connects to the audience who funded the voyage
    Model answer: It shows that from the first day Columbus thought of the Taíno as people to use for labor, not as equals. He was writing to the Spanish monarchs who paid for his voyage, so he wanted to show that the islands could bring them workers, wealth and converts.
  2. Columbus said the Taíno "had no religion." Use evidence from the lesson to explain why he was wrong, and why he might have thought so.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Evidence of Taíno religion (zemí, ceremonies); Explains his assumption (no churches or Christian practices he recognized; he had just arrived)
    Model answer: The Taíno honored spirits called zemí and held ceremonies, so they did have a religion. Columbus had only been there one day and didn't see churches or anything he recognized from Christianity, so he assumed they had none.

Learn 3: Catastrophe for the Taíno

  1. Explain how forced labor and disease worked together to cause the collapse of the Taíno population.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Describes forced labor's effects; Describes disease's effects; Explains how they made each other worse
    Model answer: Forced labor in mines and fields left the Taíno exhausted and hungry, and families couldn't farm their own food. When smallpox and measles arrived, weakened people had no immunity and many died. With so many dead, survivors were forced to work even harder, and more died.
  2. Many textbooks describe the Taíno only as victims. Why is it important to also learn about resisters like Hatuey and Enriquillo?
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Explains that the Taíno acted and fought back; Explains why a complete, truthful history matters
    Model answer: Hatuey and Enriquillo show that the Taíno fought for their freedom, and Enriquillo even won a free town. Telling only the story of victims makes them seem helpless, which isn't true, and a complete history respects their courage.

Learn 4: The Columbian Exchange and Its Legacy

  1. Give one example of a positive effect and one example of a devastating effect of the Columbian Exchange, and explain who benefited or suffered in each.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: One positive effect with who benefited; One devastating effect with who suffered
    Model answer: Potatoes from the Andes fed millions of people in Europe, helping its population grow. But smallpox brought by Europeans killed huge numbers of Indigenous people in the Americas, who had no immunity.
  2. Explain the connection between sugarcane, the collapse of Indigenous populations and the Atlantic slave trade.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Sugarcane plantations needed huge labor; As Indigenous people died, colonists forced enslaved Africans to work
    Model answer: Europeans planted sugarcane in the Caribbean, which required brutal, nonstop labor. As forced labor and disease killed most of the Indigenous workers, colonists turned to kidnapping and enslaving millions of Africans, beginning the Atlantic slave trade.

Hands-On

  1. List your foods in the two columns. Which column was longer, and which food surprised you most?
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: At least 15 foods sorted correctly; Compares the columns; Names a surprising food with a reason
    Model answer: From the Americas: corn flakes, potatoes, tomato sauce, peanut butter, chocolate chips, salsa, vanilla, pumpkin seeds. To the Americas: rice, wheat bread, sugar, coffee, oranges, bananas, milk, eggs, cinnamon. The "to the Americas" column was longer, but I was surprised that chocolate and vanilla both came from the Americas.
  2. Describe your before-1492-impossible meal and explain which ingredients came from each hemisphere.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: A meal combining both hemispheres; Correctly identifies each ingredient's origin
    Model answer: Chicken tacos with salsa and rice: corn tortillas, tomatoes, peppers and avocados came from the Americas, while chicken, rice, onions and lime came from the Eastern Hemisphere. Nobody anywhere could have made this meal before 1492.

Think Deeper

  1. In Week 1 you questioned the word "discovered." Some people now say Columbus "encountered" or "invaded" the Americas instead of "discovered" them. Which word do you think is most accurate? Support your choice with evidence from this week.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Chooses a word and makes a clear claim; At least two pieces of evidence from the lesson; Addresses why the other words are less accurate
    Model answer: Encountered is most accurate for October 1492 because millions of people already lived in the Americas, so nothing was discovered; two peoples met. But what followed was an invasion: Columbus came back with 1,200 men, forced the Taíno into labor and took their land. I'd say Columbus encountered the Americas in 1492, and Spain invaded in the years after.
  2. Choose one food from the Columbian Exchange and trace how it changed life in at least two parts of the world. Then explain whether the Columbian Exchange should be remembered mostly for its benefits or its harms.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Traces one food's effects in two regions; A reasoned judgment about benefits and harms
    Model answer: The potato, first grown in the Andes, became a staple in Ireland and across Europe, feeding growing populations, and later spread to China. But I think the Columbian Exchange should be remembered mostly for its harms, because the same contact that spread potatoes also spread smallpox that killed millions and launched the Atlantic slave trade. The benefits came at a terrible human cost.

Exit Check

  1. What were Taíno chiefs called?
    Answer: caciques
    Including women such as Anacaona.
  2. Name one food that traveled from the Americas to the rest of the world.
    Answer: potatoes (also maize, tomatoes, cacao, peanuts, chili peppers)
    They transformed diets worldwide.
  3. Name one thing a historian should keep in mind when reading Columbus's journal.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: A valid consideration about author, audience, assumptions or the copied text
    Model answer: He was writing for the Spanish monarchs who paid for his voyage, so he described the islands in ways that would please them.

Answer key: printable page

  1. Describe three features of Taíno life before 1492.
    Answer: Caciques led villages; farming cassava in conucos; ball courts; zemí spirits; dugout canoes.
  2. What do Columbus's journal entries reveal about his goals and assumptions?
    Answer: He wanted servants, converts and gold; he assumed the Taíno had no weapons or religion.
  3. Name two causes of the Taíno population collapse.
    Answer: Forced labor under the encomienda, European diseases, violence.
  4. Give two items that traveled each way in the Columbian Exchange.
    Answer: To the Eastern Hemisphere: potatoes, maize; to the Americas: horses, wheat (and diseases).
  5. How did the Columbian Exchange lead to the Atlantic slave trade?
    Answer: Sugar plantations needed labor; as Indigenous workers died, colonists enslaved millions of Africans.
