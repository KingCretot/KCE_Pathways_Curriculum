Unit 6: Heredity and Change · Week 29 · Life Science · Teacher Plan

Natural Selection Over Time

Lesson length: 60 minutes live, plus the after-class interactive lesson

Objectives

  • Explain natural selection using variation, inheritance, selection and time.
  • Distinguish changes in a population over generations from changes in one individual's life.
  • Describe evidence for natural selection, including Florida beach mice, anoles and antibiotic resistance.
  • Explain how environmental change can lead to extinction when a species cannot adapt.
  • Model natural selection, collect data across generations and interpret the results.

Standards

  • SC.7.L.15.2: Explore the scientific theory of evolution by recognizing and explaining ways in which genetic variation and environmental factors contribute to evolution by natural selection and diversity of organisms.
  • SC.7.L.15.3: Explore the scientific theory of evolution by relating how the inability of a species to adapt within a changing environment may contribute to the extinction of that species.
  • SC.7.L.15.1: Recognize that fossil evidence is consistent with the scientific theory of evolution that living things evolved from earlier species.
  • SC.6.N.1.1: Define a problem from the sixth grade curriculum, use appropriate reference materials to support scientific understanding, plan and carry out scientific investigation of various types, such as systematic observations or experiments, identify variables, collect and organize data, interpret data in charts, tables, and graphics, analyze information, make predictions, and defend conclusions.

Materials

  • The interactive lesson
  • Newspaper, white paper, scissors or a hole punch, tweezers or a clothespin, a timer, graph paper, a ruler, paper and pencil
  • Printable practice page

Vocabulary

  • natural selection: The process in which individuals with inherited traits that help them survive and reproduce leave more offspring, so those traits become more common over generations.
  • variation: Differences in traits among individuals of the same species.
  • population: All the members of one species living in an area.
  • generation: One step in a family line: parents, then offspring, then their offspring.
  • evolution: Change in the inherited traits of a population over many generations.
  • fossil: Preserved remains or traces of an organism that lived long ago.
  • extinction: The permanent loss of every member of a species.

Key questions to ask

  • What varied, and was it inherited?
  • Which individuals survived and reproduced more, and why?
  • Did an individual change, or did the population?
  • Why can't some species adapt in time?

Common mistakes to watch for

  • Organisms change because they need or want to.
  • Individuals evolve during their lifetimes.
  • Natural selection always produces perfect organisms.
  • Evolution means humans came from today's monkeys (we share common ancestors).

Support (if the student is struggling)

  • Use the beach mouse diagram as a model for every example.

Challenge (if the student is ready for more)

  • Research the Grants' finch studies and graph the beak-depth change.
  • Design a version of the dot simulation with three colors and predict the outcome.

Background for the teacher

Beach mice (Peromyscus polionotus subspecies) on Gulf Coast barrier islands; Hoekstra et al., Science (2006), linked a Mc1r mutation to light coat color. Grant and Grant documented a mean beak-depth increase of about 0.5 mm after the 1977 Daphne Major drought. Stuart et al., Science (2014), measured green anole toe-pad change on Mosquito Lagoon spoil islands. Dusky seaside sparrow declared extinct 1990 after the last bird died in 1987. Florida panther: eight Texas females released 1995; FWC estimates 120 to 230 adults. Florida places evolution benchmarks in Grade 7 (SC.7.L.15.x); KCE teaches them here per the Grade 6 scope. Answer key: interactive lesson

Reconnect

  1. A gopher tortoise digging a burrow is what kind of adaptation?
    Answer: behavioral
    It's an action.
  2. Why can't a tan or a learned skill be passed to offspring?
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Explains that it is not in the genes (not inherited)
    Model answer: A tan or a learned skill develops during one life and isn't carried in the genes, so offspring don't inherit it.
  3. Spoonbills have spoon-shaped bills. Do you think an ancestor spoonbill grew a spoon bill because it needed one? Explain your thinking.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: States a prediction with a reason (scored for reasoning, revisited later in the lesson)
    Model answer: No. I think birds with slightly wider bills caught more food and had more chicks, so over many generations the bills got wider. One bird can't change its bill by needing it.

Learn 1: Darwin, Wallace and the Four Steps

  1. Match each statement to its step: variation, inheritance, selection or time.
    Answer: inheritance; selection; variation; time
    V-I-S-T.
  2. A friend says, "Giraffes got long necks because they stretched to reach leaves, and their babies were born with longer necks." Use natural selection to correct this.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Explains that stretching during a lifetime isn't inherited; Explains variation and differential survival; Explains change over generations
    Model answer: Stretching your neck doesn't change your genes, so babies wouldn't inherit it. Instead, giraffes varied in neck length, and those with longer necks reached more food during droughts, survived and had more babies. Over many generations, long necks became more common.

Learn 2: Natural Selection Happening Now

  1. Explain the green anoles' larger toe pads using V-I-S-T.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Applies each of the four steps to anoles
    Model answer: Green anoles varied in toe-pad size. Toe-pad size is inherited. After brown anoles pushed them higher, anoles with bigger pads gripped thin branches better, survived and laid more eggs. Over about 20 generations, big toe pads became common.
  2. Why does using antibiotics when they aren't needed make resistant bacteria more common?
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Explains that antibiotics kill susceptible bacteria; Explains that resistant survivors multiply
    Model answer: Each use kills the bacteria that can't resist the drug, but any resistant ones survive and multiply with less competition. The more often antibiotics are used, the more often resistant bacteria get that advantage.

Learn 3: Evidence from Fossils and Living Things

  1. A bat wing and a human arm have the same bones arranged differently. What does this suggest, and why are they shaped differently?
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Suggests a shared ancestor; Explains different shapes as fits to different ways of life
    Model answer: It suggests bats and humans share an ancient ancestor with those bones. Over millions of years natural selection shaped them differently because bats fly and humans grab and carry.

Learn 4: When a Species Can't Keep Up: Extinction

  1. Why did adding Texas pumas help Florida panthers survive? Use the word variation.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Uses variation correctly; Connects variation to survival or adaptation
    Model answer: The tiny panther population had very little variation, so harmful traits like heart defects were common. The Texas pumas added new variation, so more kittens were healthy and the population could adapt.
  2. Why couldn't the dusky seaside sparrow adapt in time to survive the loss of its marsh?
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Explains the change was too fast or there was no helpful variation
    Model answer: The marsh was flooded and drained in just a few decades, much faster than natural selection could work, and the sparrows had nowhere else to live.

Hands-On

  1. Record the number of white and newspaper dots at the start of each generation, and describe the trend in your graph.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Data for 4 generations; A graph described; Describes the trend correctly
    Model answer: Start: 20 white, 20 newspaper. Gen 2: 12 white, 28 newspaper. Gen 3: 6 white, 34 newspaper. Gen 4: 2 white, 38 newspaper. My line graph shows white dots dropping and newspaper dots rising every generation.
  2. Explain your results using V-I-S-T, and predict what would happen on a plain white habitat.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Applies all four steps; A logical prediction for the white habitat
    Model answer: The dots varied in color. Survivors made copies of themselves, like inheritance. I spotted and picked up white dots more easily, which is selection. Over four generations, camouflaged newspaper dots took over. On white paper the opposite would happen: white dots would survive and newspaper dots would disappear.

Think Deeper

  1. Return to your Reconnect answer about spoonbill bills. Rewrite your explanation using everything you learned, including V-I-S-T and the difference between individuals and populations.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Applies all four steps to spoonbills; States that populations, not individuals, change; Revises or confirms the earlier thinking
    Model answer: Ancestral spoonbills varied in bill shape, and bill shape is inherited. Birds whose bills were a bit wider at the tip swept up more small fish and shrimp, survived and raised more chicks. Over many generations, the population came to have spoon-shaped bills. No single bird changed its bill; the population changed. My first answer was right, and now I can explain each step.
  2. Florida's climate is warming and sea level is rising. Choose one Florida species and predict whether it might adapt, move or decline. Use variation, generation time and the speed of change in your reasoning.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Names a Florida species; Uses variation, generation time and speed of change; A reasoned prediction
    Model answer: Key deer live only in the low Florida Keys. They have few individuals and produce one fawn a year, so natural selection would be slow, and rising seas are flooding their freshwater and habitat quickly. They can't easily move off the islands. I predict they'll decline unless people protect fresh water and habitat for them.

Exit Check

  1. Name the four parts of natural selection.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Variation, inheritance, selection, time
    Model answer: Variation, inheritance, selection and time.
  2. Do individuals or populations evolve?
    Answer: populations
    Individuals can't change their genes.
  3. Give one reason a species might go extinct.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: A valid reason tied to environmental change or lack of variation
    Model answer: Its habitat changed faster than natural selection could help it adapt.

Answer key: printable page

  1. Explain natural selection using V-I-S-T and one example.
    Answer: Variation in traits, inherited, individuals with helpful traits survive and reproduce more, over time traits become common; beach mice.
  2. Why don't individuals evolve?
    Answer: An individual can't change its inherited genes; populations change as survivors pass on traits.
  3. How do antibiotic-resistant bacteria become more common?
    Answer: Antibiotics kill susceptible bacteria; resistant survivors multiply.
  4. Give two kinds of evidence that species change over time.
    Answer: Fossils; similar bone structures; DNA.
  5. Why are species with little variation at greater risk of extinction?
    Answer: If the environment changes, there may be no individuals with helpful traits to survive, as with Florida panthers.
