Week 28: Data Displays, Drafting a Report, Adaptations and Indigenous Peoples of Florida

Math asks statistical questions, runs a fair customer survey and displays the results in dot plots and histograms. ELA turns research notes into an outline and a first draft of the research report. Science explores structural, behavioral and physiological adaptations of Florida plants and animals. Social Studies traces more than 14,000 years of Indigenous Florida, from Ice Age hunters to today's Seminole and Miccosukee nations.

How scoring works: each lesson scores itself. Your child gets two tries per question: full points on the first try, half points on the second, then the answer and explanation appear. A score of 85 or higher means the lesson is mastered. Written answers go to Jonathan, who scores them with a rubric.

Time this week: About 60 minutes of live lessons per subject, plus a hands-on activity, Think Deeper questions and an Exit Check for each. The ELA draft takes about 1 to 2 extra hours.

Gather this week: Paper and pencil (or a phone or tablet to record answers), graph paper, colored pencils, a ruler, and at least 15 people to survey with your permission (Math); your child's research note cards or notes, about 10 extra index cards and a marker, painter's tape or string, paper and pencil or a computer, and a family member (ELA); a spoon, tweezers, a clothespin and chopsticks (or two pencils), dry rice, small marbles or dried beans, cut-up drinking straws, a shallow bowl of water with a few floating cereal pieces, four paper plates and a cup, a timer, a family member, and paper and pencil (Science); a clear plastic container or shoebox lined with a plastic bag, sand, soil and gravel (or flour, cornmeal and oats) in three colors or textures, 10 to 15 small household artifacts (buttons, a shell, a bead, a coin, a bottle cap, a small toy), a spoon, a paintbrush and a toothpick, paper, pencil and a ruler, and a family member to build the site (Social Studies).

Heads up: Math's survey should include only people you know or approve, with no personal information collected. Science's lesson includes snake safety: never handle a snake in the wild, and don't rely on color rhymes to identify snakes. In Social Studies, your child learns that digging at real archaeological sites or collecting artifacts from public land is harmful and illegal; the dig is a home model only. Don't eat the foods used in the beak test. Keep marbles, beans, buttons and other small items away from young children (choking hazard) and pets, peel up tape, and wipe up spilled water so no one slips.

ELA: Organizing and Drafting a Report

Your child sorts research notes into sections built from their sub-questions, synthesizes sources that agree and disagree, paraphrases and quotes with in-text citations and a works-cited list, writes a hook and conclusion, and drafts the research report.

Ask at dinner: "Which sub-question does this note answer, and where did you find it?"

Try it together: Listen as your child walks the floor outline and explains the report section by section.

Signs it's clicking: They connect two sources in one sentence instead of listing them. Signs they need help: They copy sentences from a source. Ask them to close the source and explain the idea in their own words.

Math: Statistical Questions and Data Displays

Your child learns what makes a question statistical, designs a fair survey and spots biased samples and leading questions, organizes data in frequency tables, and draws dot plots and histograms, describing each display's center, spread and shape. They survey real people to make a decision for a small business.

Ask at dinner: "Would everyone give the same answer to that question, or would answers vary?"

Try it together: Be one of your child's survey respondents and ask how they chose who to survey.

Signs it's clicking: They rewrite a leading question so it's fair. Signs they need help: They write a question with one answer. Ask, "Who else could you ask, and would their answers differ?"

Life Science: Adaptations

Your child classifies structural, behavioral and physiological adaptations using Florida examples such as spoonbills, mangroves, gopher tortoises and fire-adapted longleaf pines, learns about camouflage and mimicry, distinguishes inherited adaptations from changes in one lifetime, and tests how beak shapes fit foods.

Ask at dinner: "Is that adaptation a body part, an action or something happening inside the body?"

Try it together: Race your child in the beak buffet using different tools.

Signs it's clicking: They explain why a learned skill or a tan isn't an inherited adaptation. Signs they need help: They say animals adapt because they want to. Remind them adaptations are inherited traits that helped ancestors survive.

World and American History: Indigenous Peoples of Florida

Building on Week 1, your child learns what archaeology reveals about Florida's earliest peoples, compares the Calusa, Timucua and Apalachee, explains how disease, missions and slave raids devastated the original nations, learns how the Seminole and Miccosukee formed and resisted removal in three wars, and recognizes both as sovereign nations in Florida today.

Ask at dinner: "Which Native nations lived where we live, and which nations are our neighbors today?"

Try it together: Build the layered dig site for your child and see whether they can order the layers.

Signs it's clicking: They explain why the Seminole are called "unconquered." Signs they need help: They think Florida's history began in 1513. Point to the 14,500-year-old Page-Ladson site.
