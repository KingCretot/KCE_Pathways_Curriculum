Unit 6: Research and Presentation · Week 28 · ELA · Teacher Plan

Organizing and Drafting a Report

Lesson length: 60 minutes live, plus the after-class interactive lesson

Objectives

  • Organize research notes into an outline built from sub-questions.
  • Synthesize information from several sources, including sources that disagree.
  • Paraphrase and quote correctly with in-text citations and a works-cited list.
  • Write an introduction that hooks readers and states the research question, and a conclusion that answers it.
  • Draft a research report using varied transitions and precise vocabulary.

Standards

  • ELA.6.C.1.4: Write expository texts to explain and/or analyze information from multiple sources, using a logical organizational structure, relevant elaboration, and varied transitions.
  • ELA.6.C.4.1: Conduct research to answer a question, drawing on multiple reliable and valid sources, and refocusing the inquiry when appropriate.
  • ELA.K12.EE.1.1: Cite evidence to explain and justify reasoning.

Materials

  • The interactive lesson
  • The student's research notes, about 10 extra index cards, a marker, painter's tape or string
  • Paper and pencil or a computer
  • Printable practice page

Vocabulary

  • outline: A plan that organizes the main ideas and supporting details of a piece of writing.
  • synthesize: To combine information from several sources into a new understanding.
  • in-text citation: A short note in the text, such as the author or organization, showing where information came from.
  • works cited: A list at the end of a report of every source used.
  • transition: A word or phrase that connects ideas, such as however, as a result or in contrast.
  • draft: An early version of writing that will be revised.

Key questions to ask

  • Which sub-question does this note answer?
  • How do these sources connect?
  • Is this in your own words, and is it cited?
  • Does your conclusion answer the question?

Common mistakes to watch for

  • A report is a list of facts.
  • Changing a few words makes a paraphrase.
  • Only quotations need citations.
  • The conclusion should repeat the introduction.

Support (if the student is struggling)

  • Provide an outline template with three sections.
  • Offer transition word banks for agreeing and disagreeing sources.
  • Let the student dictate the first draft.

Challenge (if the student is ready for more)

  • Add a section comparing two possible solutions with evidence for each.
  • Add a chart or map with a caption and citation.

Background for the teacher

The research report drafted this week becomes the script for Week 29's presentation and the final research presentation in Week 30 (see the Unit 6 projects page). Lake Okeechobee and Silver Springs examples use practice figures drawn from public sources and should be checked by students in their own research.

How the lesson runs

During the live lesson, the student shares their screen and answers the Reconnect questions and the Learn checks live while you teach. The Hands-On activity, Think Deeper and Exit Check are completed after class.

Scoring

The interactive lesson scores itself: full points on the first try, half points on the second try, then the answer and explanation appear. Mastery is 85. Written responses arrive in your results email with the score; score them with the rubric below before the student moves on.

Answer key: interactive lesson

Reconnect

  1. What do the letters in SIFT stand for? Type all four words.
    Answer: Stop, Investigate, Find, Trace
    The fact-checker's four moves.
  2. What is false information spread on purpose to deceive called?
    Answer: disinformation
    Misinformation is shared by mistake.
  3. Name your strongest research source and its scorecard total from last week. Why is it your strongest?
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Names a source and score; Explains why it is strongest
    Model answer: The South Florida Water Management District site scored 14 of 15. It's strongest because the agency collects the lake data itself and updates it often.

Learn 1: Sorting Notes into an Outline

  1. A student researching "Why do algae blooms spread from Lake Okeechobee, and what could stop them?" has these notes. Sort them into three sections and name each: (a) farm fertilizer runoff adds phosphorus; (b) water released east and west carries algae to the coasts; (c) blooms can make people and pets sick; (d) a reservoir south of the lake will clean water; (e) warm summer water helps algae grow; (f) Everglades restoration sends water south instead.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Three sensible sections with names; Each note placed logically
    Model answer: Causes: (a) and (e). Spread and effects: (b) and (c). Solutions: (d) and (f).
  2. Write a topic sentence for the "Solutions" section of that report.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: A sentence that states the section's main idea and answers its sub-question
    Model answer: Scientists and engineers are working on two big solutions: cleaning the water before it leaves the lake and sending more of it south to the Everglades.

Learn 2: Synthesizing Sources

  1. Combine these two notes into one synthesized sentence with a transition: Source A (St. Johns River Water Management District): Silver Springs' flow has dropped about 32 percent since the 1930s. Source B (St. Johns River Water Management District): less rainfall, more water plants in the Silver River and groundwater pumping all lowered the flow.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: One or two sentences combining both sources; A transition showing their relationship; Credits both sources
    Model answer: According to the St. Johns River Water Management District, Silver Springs' flow has dropped about 32 percent since the 1930s, because of less rainfall, more water plants in the Silver River and groundwater pumping.
  2. Two reliable sources disagree about how much farms cause Lake Okeechobee's pollution compared with cities and septic tanks. How should a report handle this?
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Presents both views fairly with citations; Explains what evidence supports each or what is still unknown
    Model answer: The report should explain both views and name the sources, then describe what the evidence shows, such as measurements of where phosphorus enters the lake, and say clearly if scientists are still studying it, instead of picking one side and hiding the other.

Learn 3: Paraphrasing, Quoting and Citing

  1. Paraphrase this statement (adapted from the Florida Department of Health) and add an in-text citation: "Blue-green algae blooms can produce toxins that harm people, pets and wildlife" (Florida Department of Health).
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Own words and structure; Keeps the meaning; Includes an in-text citation
    Model answer: The Florida Department of Health warns that some algae blooms release poisons dangerous to humans, animals and pets.
  2. Do you need a citation for each of these? Explain briefly: (a) Florida is a state in the southeastern United States. (b) Lake Okeechobee covers about 730 square miles. (c) Your own opinion that the state should act faster.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: A correct decision for each statement; A reason for each decision
    Model answer: (a) No, that's common knowledge. (b) Yes, it's a specific figure someone measured, so cite where you found it. (c) No citation, because it's my opinion, but I should make clear it's my view.

Learn 4: Introductions and Conclusions

  1. Write a hook for a report on your own research question. Label which kind of hook it is (surprising fact, short scene or question).
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: A hook connected to the student's question; Labeled correctly
    Model answer: Short scene: The water in Silver Springs used to be so clear that glass-bottom boat riders could see fish 30 feet down; today the water is noticeably less clear than it once was.

Hands-On

  1. Write your outline: your research question, each section's topic sentence, and which sources support each section. What went into the parking lot, and why?
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Research question and topic sentences; Sources named for each section; Explains the parking lot choices
    Model answer: Question: Why do algae blooms spread from Lake Okeechobee, and what could stop them? Section 1: Fertilizer runoff and warm water feed the blooms (SFWMD, UF/IFAS). Section 2: Water releases carry algae to both coasts, harming people and pets (Florida Department of Health, news). Section 3: A new reservoir and Everglades restoration could help (Army Corps of Engineers, SFWMD). Parking lot: a fact about the lake's fish, which is interesting but doesn't answer my question.
  2. Paste or copy your introduction and one body paragraph from your draft, with in-text citations.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: An introduction with a hook, background, question and preview; A body paragraph with a topic sentence, synthesized evidence and citations; Paraphrased in the student's own words
    Model answer: In the summer of 2018, families on Florida's coasts couldn't swim at their beaches: thick green algae covered the water. The algae came from Lake Okeechobee, Florida's largest lake. Why do these blooms keep spreading, and what could stop them? Fertilizer, warm water and water releases are to blame, but new projects offer hope. Fertilizer from farms and lawns washes into the lake, adding phosphorus that feeds algae (South Florida Water Management District). University of Florida scientists add that summer heat speeds the algae's growth, so blooms explode in July and August (UF/IFAS). Together, extra nutrients and heat turn the lake into an algae factory.

Think Deeper

  1. Explain the difference between a report that lists sources ("Source A says... Source B says...") and one that synthesizes them. Why do readers prefer synthesis? Use an example from your research.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Explains listing versus synthesizing; Explains why synthesis helps readers; An example from the student's research
    Model answer: A list report makes readers do the thinking, jumping from source to source. A synthesized report connects them into one explanation, showing where they agree and how they add to each other. For example, instead of listing what the water district and UF said separately, I combined them to show that nutrients and heat together cause the blooms.
  2. Why is citing sources important beyond avoiding trouble? Give at least two reasons, connecting to what you learned in Week 27.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: At least two reasons; A connection to judging sources or trust
    Model answer: Citations let readers check your facts, just as we checked sources with SIFT in Week 27, so they make your report trustworthy. They also give credit to the scientists and writers who did the work, like the credit Rosalind Franklin deserved for her DNA photograph.

Exit Check

  1. What do we call combining information from several sources into one new understanding?
    Answer: synthesizing
    Not just listing sources.
  2. Name two jobs of a report's introduction.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Two correct jobs
    Model answer: Hook the reader and state the research question.
  3. When should you quote a source instead of paraphrasing?
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: A reason from the lesson for keeping exact words
    Model answer: When the exact words matter, such as a powerful statement or an expert's opinion.

Answer key: printable page

  1. How do sub-questions help you organize a report?
    Answer: Each sub-question becomes a body section.
  2. Combine into one sentence with a transition: Source A says manatees gather at warm springs in winter. Source B says power plant outflows also draw manatees.
    Answer: Model: Manatees gather at warm springs in winter (Source A), and power plant warm-water outflows also attract them (Source B).
  3. Paraphrase with a citation (statement adapted from FWC): "Sea turtles nest on Florida beaches from March through October" (FWC).
    Answer: Model: From spring through mid-fall, sea turtles lay eggs on Florida's beaches (FWC).
  4. List the four jobs of an introduction.
    Answer: Hook, background, research question, preview of the answer.
  5. What should a conclusion do?
    Answer: Answer the question, explain why it matters, note what's unknown or what readers can do.
