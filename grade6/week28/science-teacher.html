Unit 6: Heredity and Adaptation · Week 28 · Life Science · Teacher Plan

Adaptations

Lesson length: 60 minutes live, plus the after-class interactive lesson

Objectives

  • Define an adaptation and classify adaptations as structural, behavioral or physiological.
  • Explain how specific adaptations help Florida organisms survive and reproduce in their environments.
  • Distinguish inherited adaptations from changes an individual makes during its life (acclimatization and learning).
  • Explain camouflage and mimicry as adaptations.
  • Connect adaptations to inherited variation in a population.

Standards

  • SC.7.L.15.2: Explore the scientific theory of evolution by recognizing and explaining ways in which genetic variation and environmental factors contribute to evolution by natural selection and diversity of organisms.
  • SC.7.L.15.3: Explore the scientific theory of evolution by relating how the inability of a species to adapt within a changing environment may contribute to the extinction of that species.
  • SC.6.N.1.1: Define a problem from the sixth grade curriculum, use appropriate reference materials to support scientific understanding, plan and carry out scientific investigation of various types, such as systematic observations or experiments, identify variables, collect and organize data, interpret data in charts, tables, and graphics, analyze information, make predictions, and defend conclusions.

Materials

  • The interactive lesson
  • A spoon, tweezers, a clothespin, chopsticks; rice, marbles or beans, cut straws, a shallow bowl of water with cereal; paper plates and cups; a timer
  • Printable practice page

Vocabulary

  • adaptation: An inherited trait that helps an organism survive and reproduce in its environment.
  • structural adaptation: A body part or shape that helps an organism survive, such as a beak or a thick coat.
  • behavioral adaptation: An inherited action or pattern of behavior that helps an organism survive, such as migration.
  • physiological adaptation: A body process or chemistry that helps an organism survive, such as making venom.
  • camouflage: Coloring or shape that helps an organism blend into its surroundings.
  • mimicry: When an organism looks or acts like another species, which helps it survive.
  • variation: Differences in traits among individuals of the same species.

Key questions to ask

  • Is this a body part, an action or a body process?
  • How does this trait help it survive or reproduce here?
  • Would this trait help in a different environment?
  • Was this trait inherited or developed during one life?

Common mistakes to watch for

  • Organisms adapt on purpose because they need to.
  • Changes during one lifetime are passed on.
  • An adaptation is helpful in every environment.
  • Rhymes reliably identify venomous snakes.

Support (if the student is struggling)

  • Provide a three-column sorting chart.
  • Use pictures of Florida animals for classifying.
  • Demonstrate one beak test first.

Challenge (if the student is ready for more)

  • Research why the Florida scrub-jay lives only in Florida scrub and is adapted to fire.
  • Compare Darwin's finch beaks with the beak buffet results.

Background for the teacher

Gopher tortoise burrows average about 15 feet long and can exceed 40 feet; more than 350 commensal species are documented (FWC). Viceroy and monarch mimicry is now understood as mutual (Müllerian) mimicry, since both are distasteful. Scarlet kingsnakes resemble eastern coral snakes; KCE avoids teaching color rhymes as a safety rule because they vary by region and pattern. Florida places these benchmarks in Grade 7 (SC.7.L.15.x); KCE teaches them here per the Grade 6 scope.

Answer key: interactive lesson

Reconnect

  1. In a Bb × bb cross, what percent of offspring are expected to show the recessive trait?
    Answer: 50 percent
    2 of 4 boxes are bb.
  2. The pair of allele letters an organism has, such as Bb, is its ___.
    Answer: genotype
    The trait that shows is the phenotype.
  3. Whose pea plant experiments discovered dominant and recessive traits?
    Answer: Gregor Mendel
    In the 1850s and 1860s.
  4. Last week's Punnett squares showed that offspring of the same parents can have different traits. Why might that variety matter if the environment changes?
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Explains that some individuals may have traits that help in the new conditions
    Model answer: If the environment changes, some offspring might have a trait that helps them survive the new conditions, so the population is more likely to survive.

Learn 1: Three Kinds of Adaptations

  1. Classify each adaptation as structural, behavioral or physiological.
    Answer: structural; behavioral; physiological
    A body part; an action; a body process.
  2. Gopher tortoise burrows shelter more than 350 other species, such as indigo snakes and burrowing owls, during fires and cold. Explain why scientists call the gopher tortoise a keystone species, using an idea from Unit 5.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Explains that many species depend on the burrows; Connects to food webs, interactions or commensalism from Unit 5
    Model answer: So many other animals depend on gopher tortoise burrows that losing the tortoise would affect a huge part of the community, like removing a keystone collapses an arch. Animals sheltering in the burrows is commensalism from Week 24: they benefit, and the tortoise isn't harmed.

Learn 2: Adaptations Fit Environments

  1. Predict what would happen to a population of roseate spoonbills moved to a dry pine forest with no shallow water. Explain using the idea of adaptations fitting environments.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: A prediction about the population; Explains using how the bill fits the environment
    Model answer: They would struggle to survive because their spoon-shaped bills are adapted to sweeping shallow water for shrimp and small fish. In a dry forest there's no food they can catch that way, so most would starve or leave.
  2. In Week 21 you saw fire as an abiotic factor in longleaf forests. Now explain how longleaf pines are adapted to fire, and why firefighters and land managers sometimes set controlled burns in Florida forests.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Describes a fire adaptation of longleaf pine; Explains why controlled burns help the ecosystem
    Model answer: Young longleaf pines protect their growing bud inside a thick tuft of needles, and older trees have thick bark, so low fires don't kill them. Land managers set controlled burns because fire clears out competing shrubs and keeps the pine forest healthy for the many species adapted to it, like gopher tortoises.

Learn 3: Camouflage and Mimicry

  1. A harmless scarlet kingsnake looks like a venomous coral snake. What is this adaptation called?
    Answer: mimicry
    Predators avoid both.
  2. Explain how looking like a coral snake helps a scarlet kingsnake survive, even though it has no venom.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Explains how predators respond to the kingsnake's appearance; Connects that response to the kingsnake's survival
    Model answer: Predators that have learned to avoid coral snakes stay away from anything with similar bands, so the kingsnake gets left alone even though it can't hurt them.

Learn 4: Inherited Adaptations Versus Changes in a Lifetime

  1. A dog grows a thicker coat when its family moves from Miami to Minnesota. Is the thicker coat an adaptation in the scientific sense? Explain.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: A clear decision about whether it is an adaptation; Explains the difference between an inherited ability and a change during one lifetime
    Model answer: Not exactly. The dog's ability to grow a thicker coat in cold weather is an inherited adaptation, but the thicker coat itself is a change in one dog's lifetime, called acclimatization. Its puppies born in Miami wouldn't be born with thicker coats.

Hands-On

  1. Share your data table. Which beak was best for each food, and which real Florida bird does each winning beak resemble?
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: A data table for all beaks and foods; Best beak named for each food; A real bird connection for each
    Model answer: Rice: tweezers won (42 grains), like a warbler picking insects. Marbles: clothespin won (9), like a cardinal's strong seed-cracking beak. Straw pieces: chopsticks won (14), like a heron grabbing fish. Floating cereal: spoon won (11), like a spoonbill sweeping water.
  2. Describe the organism you designed, its habitat and its three adaptations, and explain how each helps it survive or reproduce there.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: A habitat and organism; One structural, one behavioral and one physiological adaptation; Explains how each helps
    Model answer: My "spring sprinter" is a small fish for Florida's springs. Structural: a flat, silver body to blend with the sandy bottom. Behavioral: it schools with others near eelgrass so predators can't single one out. Physiological: it can handle the spring's constant 72°F water and low oxygen near the vents. Each helps it avoid predators and live where few other fish can.

Think Deeper

  1. Burmese pythons have spread through the Everglades (Week 22). Name two adaptations that help them survive there, and explain why native animals had no defenses against them.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Two plausible python adaptations; Explains the mismatch with native prey's adaptations
    Model answer: Pythons are strong swimmers and can go months between meals, both adaptations for swampy habitats with patchy food. Native mammals like raccoons and marsh rabbits never evolved alongside giant constrictors, so they don't recognize pythons as a danger or have behaviors to avoid them.
  2. As Florida's climate warms and seas rise, which kinds of organisms might do better and which might struggle? Use the idea of adaptations fitting environments and give one example of each. What could happen to a species that cannot adapt?
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: One organism likely to do better, with a reason; One organism likely to struggle, with a reason; Uses the idea of adaptations fitting environments; Explains a possible outcome for a species that cannot adapt
    Model answer: Mangroves, adapted to salty water, are already spreading north as winters get warmer, so they may do better. Freshwater plants near the coast, like some sawgrass marshes, may struggle as salt water moves inland, because they aren't adapted to handle salt. A species that can't adapt or move could decline or even go extinct.

Exit Check

  1. Manatees moving to warm springs in winter is what kind of adaptation?
    Answer: behavioral
    It's an action.
  2. Coloring that helps an organism blend into its surroundings is called ___.
    Answer: camouflage
    Like a green anole on a leaf.
  3. Why isn't a suntan an adaptation, even though it protects the skin?
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Explains that it develops during one lifetime and is not inherited
    Model answer: A tan develops during one person's life and isn't passed on to their children, so it isn't an inherited adaptation.

Answer key: printable page

  1. Define adaptation and give one structural, one behavioral and one physiological example.
    Answer: An inherited trait that helps survival and reproduction; spoonbill bill; manatees moving to springs; black mangroves pushing out salt through their leaves.
  2. How are red mangroves adapted to salty coastal water?
    Answer: Prop roots hold them up in water and bring in air; their roots filter out most of the salt.
  3. Explain mimicry with an example.
    Answer: Looking like another species; a scarlet kingsnake resembles a venomous coral snake, so predators avoid it.
  4. Why is a raccoon's learned trash-can skill not an adaptation?
    Answer: It's learned in one lifetime, not inherited.
  5. Why do Florida land managers use controlled burns?
    Answer: Many Florida plants and animals are adapted to fire, which clears competitors and keeps the ecosystem healthy.
