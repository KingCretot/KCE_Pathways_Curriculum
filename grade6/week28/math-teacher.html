Unit 6: Geometry and Data · Week 28 · Math · Teacher Plan

Statistical Questions and Data Displays

Lesson length: 60 minutes live, plus the after-class interactive lesson

Objectives

  • Recognize and write statistical questions that anticipate variability.
  • Design a fair survey and identify sources of bias in sampling and wording.
  • Organize data in frequency tables, dot plots and histograms.
  • Describe a data set's center, spread and overall shape from a display.
  • Use survey data to make a business decision.

Standards

  • MA.6.DP.1.1: Recognize and formulate a statistical question that would generate numerical data.
  • MA.6.DP.1.4: Given a histogram or line plot within a real-world context, qualitatively describe and interpret the spread and distribution of the data, including any symmetry, skewness, gaps, clusters, outliers and the range.
  • MA.6.DP.1.5: Create box plots and histograms to represent sets of numerical data within real-world contexts.
  • Financial literacy: using data to make business decisions (Jump$tart Spending and Saving).

Materials

  • The interactive lesson
  • Paper, graph paper, colored pencils, a ruler
  • At least 15 survey respondents (with parent permission)
  • Printable practice page

Vocabulary

  • statistical question: A question that expects many different answers (variability) and is answered by collecting data.
  • variability: How much data values differ from one another.
  • frequency: How many times a value or interval appears in a data set.
  • dot plot: A display that shows each data value as a dot above a number line.
  • histogram: A bar graph of data grouped into equal intervals, with no gaps between bars.
  • sample: The part of a group that is surveyed to learn about the whole group.
  • bias: Anything in a survey that pushes results away from the truth.

Key questions to ask

  • Does this question expect many different answers?
  • Who is left out of this sample?
  • Is this question pushing people toward an answer?
  • Where is the center, and how spread out are the data?

Common mistakes to watch for

  • Any question is a statistical question.
  • A bigger sample always fixes bias.
  • Histogram bars should have gaps like bar graphs.
  • A histogram shows each individual value.

Support (if the student is struggling)

  • Provide a frequency table template.
  • Build a dot plot with sticky notes first.
  • Offer sentence frames for center, spread and shape.

Challenge (if the student is ready for more)

  • Compare two dot plots, such as loaves bought by subscribers and non-subscribers.
  • Find a real survey in the news and evaluate its sample and question wording.

Background for the teacher

Florida's Grade 6 data benchmarks are MA.6.DP.1.1 to 1.6; Florida's benchmarks call dot plots "line plots." Box plots are introduced in Week 29 with the median. Maya's data are practice figures; the hands-on uses real survey data.

Sources

  • Florida Department of Education, B.E.S.T. Standards for Mathematics (2020).
  • American Statistical Association, GAISE II: Pre-K–12 Guidelines for Assessment and Instruction in Statistics Education (2020).

How the lesson runs

During the live lesson, the student shares their screen and answers the Reconnect questions and the Learn checks live while you teach. The Hands-On activity, Think Deeper and Exit Check are completed after class.

Scoring

The interactive lesson scores itself: full points on the first try, half points on the second try, then the answer and explanation appear. Mastery is 85. Written responses arrive in your results email with the score; score them with the rubric below before the student moves on.

Answer key: interactive lesson

Reconnect

  1. Find the volume of a box 8 in by 5 in by 4 in.
    Answer: 160 cubic inches
    8 × 5 × 4 = 160.
  2. Find the surface area of the same box.
    Answer: 184 square inches
    2(40) + 2(32) + 2(20) = 80 + 64 + 40 = 184.
  3. Maya saves $4.20 per order with a smaller box. How much does she save on 40 orders?
    Answer: 168 dollars
    40 × 4.20 = 168.
  4. Maya wondered whether her customers would pay more for a bigger box with a gift card. How could she find out what her customers want instead of guessing?
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: A practical way to get information from customers
    Model answer: She could survey her customers, asking a sample of them whether they'd pay extra for a gift box, and use their answers to decide.

Learn 1: Statistical Questions

  1. Change this into a statistical question: "How many books did I read this summer?"
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: A question expecting many different answers from a group
    Model answer: How many books did the students in my homeschool co-op read this summer?
  2. Explain why "What is the temperature in Arcadia right now?" is not a statistical question but "How did the daily high temperature in Arcadia vary in July?" is.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Explains one answer versus many expected answers
    Model answer: The first has one answer at one moment. The second expects many different values, 31 daily highs that vary, so it needs data to answer.

Learn 2: Fair Surveys and Bias

  1. Rewrite this leading question so it is fair: "Wouldn't a monthly bread subscription be a great deal?"
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: A neutral question with no loaded words; Still asks about the subscription
    Model answer: Would you be interested in a monthly bread subscription? Yes, no or not sure.
  2. Maya surveyed only customers who follow her on Instagram. Explain why her results might be biased and suggest a better sample.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Explains who is left out and why that matters; Suggests a more representative sample
    Model answer: Instagram followers might be younger and more excited about her bakery than other customers, so the results might overstate interest. A better sample would randomly choose 25 customers from her full order list, including people who buy at the market.

Learn 3: Frequency Tables and Dot Plots

  1. From the dot plot, how many customers bought 2 loaves?
    Answer: 6
    Count the dots above 2.
  2. How many customers bought 4 or more loaves?
    Answer: 5
    3 + 1 + 1 = 5.
  3. Describe the dot plot's center, spread and shape in two or three sentences.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Describes the center; Describes the spread; Describes the shape
    Model answer: Most customers bought 2 or 3 loaves, so the center is around 2 or 3. The data spread from 0 to 6. The shape has a peak at 2 and trails off to the right, with just a couple of customers buying 5 or 6.

Learn 4: Histograms

  1. From the histogram, how many customers are 40 or older?
    Answer: 10
    6 + 3 + 1 = 10.
  2. What percent of the 25 customers are in their 30s?
    Answer: 32 percent
    8 ÷ 25 = 0.32.
  3. When is a histogram better than a dot plot? Give an example of data that fit each display best.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Explains histograms for many different values grouped into intervals; An example for each display
    Model answer: A histogram is better when there are many different values, like customers' ages from 12 to 68, so grouping them into intervals shows the shape. A dot plot works better for a few whole-number values, like loaves bought per month from 0 to 6.

Hands-On

  1. Write your two survey questions, who you surveyed and your frequency tables. What display did you choose for the number question, and why?
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Two statistical, unbiased questions; Describes the sample; Frequency tables; Justifies the display choice
    Model answer: Questions: "How much would you pay for a dozen cookies?" and "Which flavor would you buy most: chocolate chip, oatmeal or sugar?" I surveyed 18 people: family, co-op friends and neighbors from age 8 to 70. Prices: $3 (2), $4 (3), $5 (7), $6 (4), $8 (2). Flavors: chocolate chip 10, oatmeal 5, sugar 3. I used a dot plot for price because there were only five different values.
  2. Describe your number data's center, spread and shape, and explain the business decision you would make based on your survey.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Describes center, spread and shape; A decision clearly based on the data
    Model answer: Most people would pay $5, with prices spread from $3 to $8 and a peak at $5. I'd sell a dozen chocolate chip cookies for $5, since that's the most common price and chocolate chip was the favorite flavor by far.

Think Deeper

  1. A cereal company's website reports that "9 out of 10 kids prefer our cereal!" based on an online poll on its own website. Explain at least two reasons to be skeptical, connecting to Week 27 ELA's SIFT moves.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: At least two sources of bias in the poll; Connects to judging sources
    Model answer: First, the company made the poll and wants to sell cereal, so it has a reason to report good results. Second, people visiting the company's own site probably already like the cereal, which is sampling bias, and anyone can answer, so it's a voluntary response. Like we learned with SIFT in Week 27 ELA, I'd look for an independent survey before trusting the claim.
  2. Design a statistical question you could investigate about your homeschool or community, explain how you'd choose a fair sample, and which display you'd use and why.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: A real statistical question; A fair sampling plan; A justified display choice
    Model answer: Question: How many minutes do families at our co-op spend reading together each week? I'd randomly pick 20 families from the co-op directory by drawing names, so I don't only ask my friends. I'd use a histogram, because the minutes would vary widely, from 0 to 400, and grouping by 60 minutes would show the shape.

Exit Check

  1. Write one statistical question about pets.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: A question that expects many different answers from a group
    Model answer: How many pets do the families in our neighborhood have?
  2. What display groups data into equal intervals, with bars that touch?
    Answer: a histogram
    For many different values.
  3. Give one way a survey can become biased.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: A valid source of bias
    Model answer: Asking a leading question, like "Don't you love our new bread?"

Answer key: printable page

  1. Label each as statistical or not: (a) How old is my dog? (b) How old are the dogs at the shelter? (c) How many hours do teens in Florida sleep on school nights?
    Answer: (a) not (b) statistical (c) statistical
  2. Rewrite fairly: "Don't you agree our park needs a new playground?"
    Answer: Model: Should our park get a new playground? Yes, no or not sure.
  3. Make a frequency table and dot plot for these quiz scores: 7, 8, 8, 9, 10, 8, 7, 9, 8, 10, 6, 8.
    Answer: 6: 1, 7: 2, 8: 5, 9: 2, 10: 2; dot plot from 6 to 10 with a peak at 8.
  4. Group these ages into intervals of 10 and make a histogram: 12, 15, 22, 27, 28, 31, 33, 35, 38, 41, 44, 52.
    Answer: 10-19: 2, 20-29: 3, 30-39: 4, 40-49: 2, 50-59: 1.
  5. Describe a fair way to survey customers about a new product.
    Answer: Randomly choose customers from the whole list and ask a neutral question.
