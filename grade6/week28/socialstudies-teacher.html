Unit 6: The Americas and the Meeting of Worlds · Week 28 · World and American History · Teacher Plan

Indigenous Peoples of Florida

Lesson length: 60 minutes live, plus the after-class interactive lesson

Objectives

  • Describe what archaeology reveals about Florida's earliest peoples.
  • Compare the ways of life of the Calusa, Timucua and Apalachee.
  • Explain how disease, missions, slave raids and war devastated Florida's original nations.
  • Explain how the Seminole and Miccosukee formed and resisted removal.
  • Recognize Florida's Indigenous nations as living peoples with sovereign governments today.

Standards

  • SS.6.W.1.4: Describe the methods of historical inquiry and how history relates to the other social sciences.
  • SS.6.W.1.6: Describe how history transmits culture and heritage and provides models of human character.
  • SS.6.G.2.1: Explain how major physical characteristics, natural resources, climate, and absolute and relative locations have influenced settlement, interactions, and the economies of ancient civilizations of the world.

Materials

  • The interactive lesson
  • A clear container or shoebox with a plastic bag, sand, soil and gravel (or flour, cornmeal and oats), 10 to 15 small household artifacts
  • A spoon, paintbrush, toothpick, ruler, paper and pencil
  • Printable practice page

Vocabulary

  • Paleo-Indian: The earliest peoples of the Americas, who lived during and just after the last Ice Age.
  • shell mound: A large mound built from shells and other materials, used for homes, temples or burials.
  • chiefdom: A society led by a chief who rules several towns, often with ranks of nobles and commoners.
  • mission: A settlement where Spanish priests worked to convert Native people to Christianity.
  • removal: The U.S. government policy of forcing Native nations off their lands to territory west of the Mississippi.
  • sovereignty: A nation's right to govern itself.

Key questions to ask

  • How do archaeologists know what happened before writing?
  • How did each nation adapt to its environment?
  • Why did the original nations decline so quickly?
  • Who are Florida's Indigenous nations today?

Common mistakes to watch for

  • Florida's history began in 1513.
  • The Seminole are Florida's original people (they formed in the 1700s).
  • Native peoples belong only to the past.
  • Spanish missions were peaceful places for Native people.

Support (if the student is struggling)

  • Use a three-column comparison chart.
  • Read the Seminole section aloud together.

Challenge (if the student is ready for more)

  • Visit (in person or online) the Ah-Tah-Thi-Ki Museum of the Seminole Tribe of Florida and report on one exhibit.
  • Research Black Seminoles and the Battle of Lake Okeechobee (1837).

Background for the teacher

Page-Ladson (Aucilla River): stone tools and a butchered mastodon tusk dated about 14,550 years ago (Halligan and others, 2016, Science Advances). Windover: burials about 7,000 to 8,000 years old (dates vary by method); textiles and preserved brain matter. Turtle Mound is about 35 to 50 feet tall depending on source. Population estimates for Florida in 1500 vary widely (about 100,000 to over 350,000). Fray Francisco Pareja's Timucua catechisms were printed in 1612 to 1627. Moore's raids on Apalachee were in 1704. The Seminole Tribe of Florida was federally recognized in 1957 and the Miccosukee Tribe in 1962; the Seminole Nation of Oklahoma descends from those removed. Brighton Reservation is in Glades County. Tribal names and spellings follow the tribes' own usage. The pre-1500 Americas are outside Florida's Grade 6 NGSSS world history range; the standards above cover the skills taught, and this content supports Florida's Grade 4 and 8 history strands.

Answer key: interactive lesson

Reconnect

  1. What is the largest earthen mound in the Americas, at Cahokia?
    Answer: Monks Mound
    Built basket by basket.
  2. What raised farm fields did the Mexica build in Lake Texcoco?
    Answer: chinampas
    Several harvests a year.
  3. A quipu cord has 2 knots in the hundreds place, 0 in the tens and 6 in the ones. What number is it?
    Answer: 206
    200 + 0 + 6.
  4. In Week 1 you learned that a Spanish account said Florida was "discovered." Why was that word misleading?
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Explains that Indigenous nations already lived in Florida
    Model answer: The land was already home to the Calusa, Timucua, Tequesta and many other nations, so the Spanish didn't discover it; they arrived in someone else's homeland.

Learn 1: Florida's Earliest Peoples

  1. Why do archaeologists often find Florida's oldest sites in springs, sinkholes and river bottoms? Connect your answer to Week 23 science (springs and the Floridan aquifer) and to how Florida's climate has changed.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Explains why these places mattered to people in Ice Age Florida; Explains how the sites were later preserved
    Model answer: During the Ice Age, Florida was drier and fresh water was scarce, so people and animals gathered at springs and sinkholes fed by the Floridan aquifer. When the climate warmed, water levels rose and covered these sites, and the water preserved tools and bones.
  2. The peat at Windover Pond preserved fabric and even brain tissue. What kinds of questions could scientists answer from these remains that stone tools alone couldn't answer?
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: At least two questions that stone tools alone could not answer
    Model answer: Scientists could learn how people made clothing and bags, what they ate, what illnesses they had and even how people were related through DNA. Stone tools only show how they hunted and worked.

Learn 2: Calusa, Timucua and Apalachee

  1. Which nation built a powerful chiefdom on Florida's southwest coast without farming?
    Answer: the Calusa
    Estuaries provided plenty of fish and shellfish.
  2. Most powerful chiefdoms in the Americas depended on farming. Explain how the Calusa could build one without it, using what you learned about Florida's estuaries.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Explains why estuaries gave a reliable food supply; Connects a reliable food surplus to supporting chiefs, nobles and labor
    Model answer: The estuaries around Charlotte Harbor and Estero Bay were full of fish, oysters and clams all year, so the Calusa had a large, reliable food supply without farming. That surplus fed nobles, warriors and the workers who built shell mounds and canals, just as corn did for farming peoples.
  3. Compare the Timucua and the Apalachee with the Calusa. Give one similarity and one difference.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: One accurate similarity; One accurate difference
    Model answer: All three were organized under chiefs and built towns. But the Timucua and Apalachee were farmers who grew maize, beans and squash, while the Calusa lived from fishing and gathering in coastal estuaries.

Learn 3: Catastrophe: Disease, Missions and Raids

  1. Explain how two of these causes, disease, missions and slave raids, worked together to devastate Florida's nations.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Names two causes; Explains how they made each other worse
    Model answer: Epidemics had already killed many people and weakened towns, so the missions' survivors were fewer and less able to defend themselves. When English and Creek raiders attacked between 1702 and 1706, the weakened mission towns couldn't resist, and thousands were killed or enslaved.

Learn 4: The Seminole and Miccosukee

  1. Which Seminole leader was captured under a flag of truce in 1837?
    Answer: Osceola
    His capture outraged many Americans.
  2. Many people say the Seminole are "unconquered." Explain what that means, using evidence from the lesson.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Explains that some Seminole were never defeated or removed; Uses specific evidence from the lesson
    Model answer: Despite three wars, the United States never forced all the Seminole out of Florida. A few hundred retreated into the Everglades and kept resisting, and their descendants, the Seminole Tribe of Florida and the Miccosukee Tribe, still live in Florida today as self-governing nations.
  3. What does it mean that the Seminole Tribe of Florida and the Miccosukee Tribe are sovereign nations? Give one example of how sovereignty works today.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Defines sovereignty as self-government; A concrete example of self-government today
    Model answer: Sovereignty means the tribes govern themselves. For example, the Seminole Tribe has its own elected council, police force and schools on its reservations.

Hands-On

  1. Share your artifact chart (layer, depth, artifact). What story does your site tell, and which artifact was most important for dating a layer?
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: A complete chart with layers and depths; A story that follows superposition; Identifies a key dating artifact with a reason
    Model answer: Bottom layer, 9 cm: three shells and a bead. Middle, 5 cm: a metal button and a coin. Top, 2 cm: a bottle cap and a plastic toy. My story: people first lived here collecting shellfish; later, people with European metal goods came; finally, modern people left trash. The coin was most important, because its date showed the middle layer came after contact.
  2. Why is it illegal and harmful to dig up artifacts from real sites, even just one arrowhead? Connect to what archaeologists learned at Windover or Page-Ladson.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Explains why an artifact's original context matters; Connects to respect for Indigenous ancestors and laws protecting sites
    Model answer: An artifact's meaning comes from exactly where it was found. At Page-Ladson, the tools mattered because they were found in the same layer as the mastodon tusk, which could be dated. Taking one arrowhead destroys that evidence forever, and many sites are graves and sacred places of Indigenous ancestors, which laws protect.

Think Deeper

  1. Most textbooks once began Florida's history in 1513. Write a paragraph explaining why starting in 1513 gives a false picture, using at least three pieces of evidence from this lesson.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: A clear claim; At least three accurate pieces of evidence; Explains what is lost by starting in 1513
    Model answer: Starting Florida's history in 1513 leaves out more than 14,000 years. People hunted mastodons at Page-Ladson about 14,500 years ago, buried their dead at Windover Pond 7,000 years ago, and built shell mounds and chiefdoms like the Calusa's long before Ponce de León arrived. Starting in 1513 makes Europeans seem like the first actors, when they arrived in a homeland that already had a long, rich history.
  2. Find out which Indigenous nations lived in or used the area where you live, and whether any Seminole or Miccosukee land is nearby. Explain how knowing this changes how you see your community.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Names a nation connected to the local area; Mentions nearby tribal land or a related place; A thoughtful reflection
    Model answer: In DeSoto County, the Calusa influenced the Peace River area and Seminole people later lived along it; today the Seminole Tribe's Brighton Reservation is in nearby Glades County. Knowing this reminds me that the rivers and towns I know have Native histories and that Native nations are still my neighbors.

Exit Check

  1. Name one of Florida's original nations living there in the 1500s.
    Answer: Calusa (also Timucua, Apalachee, Tequesta, Tocobaga, Ais)
    The Seminole formed later, in the 1700s.
  2. Name one federally recognized tribe in Florida today.
    Answer: the Seminole Tribe of Florida or the Miccosukee Tribe of Indians of Florida
    Both are sovereign nations.
  3. Give two reasons Florida's original nations were nearly destroyed after 1513.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Two valid causes
    Model answer: European diseases like smallpox, and English and Creek slave raids on the mission towns.

Answer key: printable page

  1. What did archaeologists find at Page-Ladson and Windover Pond?
    Answer: Page-Ladson: tools and a butchered mastodon tusk about 14,500 years old; Windover: 7,000-year-old burials with fabric and brain tissue.
  2. Compare how the Calusa and the Apalachee got their food.
    Answer: Calusa: fishing and gathering in estuaries; Apalachee: farming maize, beans and squash.
  3. Name three causes of the destruction of Florida's original nations.
    Answer: Disease, mission labor, slave raids, war.
  4. How did the Seminole form, and what happened in the Seminole Wars?
    Answer: From Creek and other peoples in the 1700s, with Black Seminoles; three wars against U.S. removal; some were forced west, but some were never removed.
  5. Name Florida's two federally recognized tribes today.
    Answer: The Seminole Tribe of Florida and the Miccosukee Tribe of Indians of Florida.
