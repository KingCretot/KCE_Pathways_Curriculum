Unit 1: Thinking Like a Scientist · Week 5 · Life Science · Teacher Plan

Unit 1 Checkpoint and Investigation Project

Lesson length: 60 minutes live, plus the after-class interactive lesson

Objectives

  • Plan and carry out your own fair-test investigation with three trials.
  • Record data in a table with units and display it in the correct type of graph.
  • Write a conclusion using Claim, Evidence and Reasoning (CER).
  • Explain the limits of an investigation and why scientists repeat each other's work.
  • Show mastery of Unit 1 on the checkpoint (85 or higher).

Standards

  • SC.6.N.1.1: Define a problem from the sixth grade curriculum, use appropriate reference materials to support scientific understanding, plan and carry out scientific investigation of various types, such as systematic observations or experiments, identify variables, collect and organize data, interpret data in charts, tables, and graphics, analyze information, make predictions, and defend conclusions.
  • SC.6.N.1.2: Explain why scientific investigations should be replicable.
  • SC.6.N.1.4: Discuss, compare, and negotiate methods used, results obtained, and explanations among groups of students conducting the same investigation (for a homeschool student: compare with a parent or a KCE classmate).
  • SC.6.N.2.2: Explain that scientific knowledge is durable because it is open to change as new evidence or interpretations are encountered.
  • NGSS Science and Engineering Practices 3, 4 and 7: planning investigations; analyzing data; engaging in argument from evidence.

Materials

  • The interactive lesson
  • The student's two testable questions from Week 4
  • Graph paper and colored pencils
  • The Unit 1 Projects page with the investigation rubric
  • Printable practice page

Vocabulary

  • claim: A statement that answers the investigation question.
  • evidence: Data that supports a claim.
  • reasoning: The explanation of why the evidence supports the claim, using science ideas.
  • conclusion: The final section of an investigation that states and explains what was found.
  • replicate: To repeat another scientist's investigation to see if you get the same results.
  • peer review: When other scientists check a study before it is published.
  • procedure: The step-by-step plan for an investigation, written so someone else could repeat it.

Key questions to ask

  • Can this question be answered by measuring something?
  • What is the one thing you will change?
  • What does your evidence actually show?
  • What could have affected your results?

Common mistakes to watch for

  • Thinking an experiment proves a hypothesis right or wrong forever.
  • Writing a claim with no numbers.
  • Changing more than one variable at a time.
  • Believing a surprising result is a mistake and leaving it out.

Support (if the student is struggling)

  • Choose one of the four strong project questions.
  • Provide a data table template.
  • Give sentence starters for CER: I claim that... My data shows... This happens because...

Challenge (if the student is ready for more)

  • Test four or more levels of the independent variable.
  • Compare your results with a classmate's or online data and explain any differences.

Background for the teacher

How the lesson runs

During the live lesson, the student shares their screen and answers the Reconnect questions and the Learn checks live while you teach. The Hands-On activity, Think Deeper and Exit Check are completed after class.

Scoring

The interactive lesson scores itself: full points on the first try, half points on the second try, then the answer and explanation appear. Mastery is 85. Written responses arrive in your results email with the score; score them with the rubric below before the student moves on.

Checkpoint scoring

Checkpoint questions are scored separately from the lesson. The student gets one try per question and the answers are not shown, so the checkpoint measures independent mastery and a retake stays fair. Your results email shows the auto-scored checkpoint points, a list of each item marked correct or MISSED, and the points available for the written items. Score the written items with their rubrics, then: checkpoint score = (auto points + rubric points) / total possible × 100. Mastery is 85. Below 85: teach a targeted review lesson on the parts missed (A to D), then the student retakes the checkpoint. Record both attempts in the Weekly Score Log.

Answer key: interactive lesson

Reconnect

  1. "The paper towel feels wet" is an observation. Is "It must have rained last night" an observation or an inference? Explain how you know.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Inference; It explains or guesses a cause instead of using only the senses
    Model answer: It is an inference, because it is a possible explanation for the wet towel, not something I observed with my senses.
  2. In a test of whether paper color changes temperature in the sun, name each variable.
    Answer: paper color; temperature
    You change the color (independent) and measure the temperature (dependent).
  3. Convert 350 milliliters to liters.
    Answer: 0.35 L
    350 ÷ 1,000 = 0.35 L.
  4. Three trials measured 18 °C, 21 °C and 21 °C. What is the average?
    Answer: 20 °C
    (18 + 21 + 21) ÷ 3 = 60 ÷ 3 = 20 °C.
  5. You compare the temperature under four different colors of paper. Which kind of graph fits best?
    Answer: a bar graph
    Separate categories (colors) fit a bar graph.
  6. Name one model you studied in Week 4, one thing it shows well and one limitation.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Names a model (forecast cone, globe, map); A real strength; A real limitation
    Model answer: The hurricane forecast cone shows where the storm's center will probably go, but it does not show how big the storm is or where the rain will fall.

Learn 1: Writing a Conclusion With CER

  1. Using Jaylen's averages, how many degrees warmer was the black paper than the red paper?
    Answer: 5.1 °C
    41.2 − 36.1 = 5.1 °C.
  2. Label the claim, evidence and reasoning in this paragraph: Ice melts faster in a black cup. In my test it melted in 18 minutes in the black cup and 27 minutes in the white cup. Dark colors absorb more of the sun's energy, which warms the ice.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Claim: first sentence; Evidence: the times with units; Reasoning: dark colors absorb more energy
    Model answer: Claim: Ice melts faster in a black cup. Evidence: 18 minutes in the black cup versus 27 minutes in the white cup. Reasoning: dark colors absorb more of the sun's energy, which warms the ice.
  3. A student writes: "My hypothesis was right. Black was hotter." Explain two things missing from this conclusion, then rewrite it as a stronger claim with evidence.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Missing specific data with units; Missing reasoning (why); Rewrite includes numbers and units
    Model answer: It has no data and no reasoning. A stronger version: Black paper got hotter than white paper. After 30 minutes, the space under black paper averaged 41.2 °C and under white paper 31.4 °C, almost 10 degrees warmer, because dark colors absorb more of the sun's energy.

Learn 2: Limits, Errors and Replication

  1. Name two limits of Jaylen's investigation and explain how each could change his results.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Two real limits (few trials, one location, cloud cover, thermometer differences, paper material); Explains how each could affect results
    Model answer: He only tested three days, so one cloudy afternoon could change an average a lot. He also used one kind of paper, so a thicker paper might trap heat differently.
  2. What is the word for repeating another scientist's investigation to see if you get the same results?
    Answer: replicate
    Replication builds trust in results.
  3. Why did John Glenn want Katherine Johnson to check the computer's calculations by hand? What does this tell you about how science works?
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Computers were new and could be wrong; a human expert confirmed the numbers; Science depends on checking and repeating work
    Model answer: Electronic computers were new and he did not fully trust them, so he wanted an expert he trusted to confirm the numbers. It shows that science depends on checking work more than once before trusting it, especially when lives are at stake.

Learn 3: Planning Your Investigation Project

  1. Explain why "Which color is the prettiest?" is NOT a testable science question, then rewrite it so it is testable.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Pretty is an opinion, cannot be measured; Rewrite has a measurable dependent variable
    Model answer: Prettiest is an opinion, so there is nothing to measure. A testable version: Does the color of a cup change how fast an ice cube melts inside it?
  2. Write your project question, hypothesis (If... then... because...), independent variable, dependent variable and three controlled variables.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Testable question; Hypothesis in If-then-because form; Correct independent and dependent variables; Three relevant controlled variables
    Model answer: Question: Does the height of a ramp change how far a toy car rolls? Hypothesis: If I raise the ramp, then the car will roll farther, because it gains more speed on the way down. IV: ramp height (cm). DV: distance rolled (cm). Controlled: the same car, the same floor, releasing without pushing.

Hands-On

  1. Type your numbered procedure and your data table, including all three trials and the averages.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Numbered, repeatable procedure; Table with units in headings; Three trials for each condition; Correct averages
    Model answer: 1. Lean a board on books 10 cm high. 2. Release the toy car from the top without pushing. 3. Measure from the bottom of the ramp to the front of the car. 4. Repeat 3 times. 5. Repeat at 20 cm and 30 cm. Ramp height (cm) | Trial 1 (cm) | Trial 2 (cm) | Trial 3 (cm) | Average (cm) 10 | 92 | 88 | 95 | 91.7 20 | 151 | 147 | 156 | 151.3 30 | 203 | 210 | 199 | 204
  2. Write your conclusion using CER. Then name two limits of your investigation and one question you would test next.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Clear claim that answers the question; Evidence with specific averages and units; Reasoning with a science idea; Two limits; A next question
    Model answer: Claim: Raising the ramp made the toy car roll farther. Evidence: At 10 cm the car rolled an average of 91.7 cm, at 20 cm 151.3 cm, and at 30 cm 204 cm. Reasoning: A higher ramp gives the car more height to fall, so it reaches the bottom moving faster and keeps going longer before friction stops it. Limits: I used only one car, and my measuring tape may have slipped on the carpet. Next, I would test whether the floor surface changes the distance.

Think Deeper

  1. A website claims, "Our study proves plants grow faster with music." The study used one plant with music and one without, for three days. Use what you learned this unit to explain three problems with this study and how to fix each one.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Sample too small: one plant each; Too short or only one trial: needs repeats; Other variables not controlled or not stated (light, water, pot); A fix for each problem
    Model answer: First, one plant in each group is far too few, because one plant might just be healthier; use at least ten in each group. Second, three days is too short to see real growth; run it for several weeks and repeat the whole test. Third, they do not say whether light, water, soil and pot size were kept the same; control every variable except the music. With these fixes, other scientists could replicate it.

Checkpoint: Part A: Observations, questions and fair tests

  1. You find a puddle under the kitchen sink. Write one observation and one inference about it.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric (4 points toward the checkpoint).
    Rubric: Observation uses the senses only; Inference is a possible explanation
    Model answer: Observation: there is a puddle of clear water about 20 cm wide under the sink. Inference: the pipe under the sink might be leaking.
  2. A student tests whether the amount of fertilizer changes how tall bean plants grow. Name each variable.
    Answer: amount of fertilizer; plant height
    You change the fertilizer and measure the height.
  3. In that test, what do we call the group of plants that gets no fertilizer?
    Answer: the control group
    The control group gets no change and is used for comparison.

Checkpoint: Part B: Measurement

  1. Convert 4.2 kilograms to grams.
    Answer: 4200 g
    4.2 × 1,000 = 4,200 g.
  2. Convert 85 millimeters to centimeters.
    Answer: 8.5 cm
    85 ÷ 10 = 8.5 cm.
  3. An oven thermometer reads 190 °C, 191 °C and 190 °C, but the oven is really at 175 °C. Is it accurate, precise, both or neither? Explain.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric (4 points toward the checkpoint).
    Rubric: Precise but not accurate; Readings close together, but far from the true value
    Model answer: It is precise but not accurate, because the readings are almost the same each time but all about 15 degrees too high.

Checkpoint: Part C: Graphs and models

  1. Which kind of graph best shows how a puppy's mass changed each week for 10 weeks?
    Answer: a line graph
    Change over time fits a line graph.
  2. In Jaylen's graph, how many degrees warmer was the blue paper than the white paper?
    Answer: 6.2 °C
    37.6 − 31.4 = 6.2 °C.
  3. A bar graph of the same data starts its y-axis at 30 °C. Explain how this would mislead a reader.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric (4 points toward the checkpoint).
    Rubric: Bars would look many times different; Because the axis does not start at 0, bar length no longer matches the amount
    Model answer: The white bar would look tiny and the black bar huge, about eight times taller, even though 41.2 is only about 1.3 times 31.4. A bar graph should start at 0 because the bar's length shows the amount.
  4. Your cousin lives just outside the hurricane forecast cone and says, "We're safe, we're not in the cone." What do you tell her, and why?
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric (4 points toward the checkpoint).
    Rubric: Not necessarily safe; The cone shows only the probable path of the center, not the storm's size
    Model answer: She is not necessarily safe. The cone shows only where the center will probably go, not how big the storm is, so wind, rain and flooding can reach places outside the cone.

Checkpoint: Part D: Conclusions

  1. A student's conclusion says, "My plant grew best in sunlight because plants need light." What part of CER is missing, and what should the student add?
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric (3 points toward the checkpoint).
    Rubric: Evidence is missing; Should add measured data with units (heights in cm for each group)
    Model answer: The evidence is missing. The student should add the measured heights, for example: in sunlight the plants averaged 14 cm, in shade 6 cm.
  2. Data: sugar dissolved in 98 seconds in 10 °C water, 60 seconds in 25 °C water and 31 seconds in 50 °C water. Write a claim and evidence for the question "Does water temperature change how fast sugar dissolves?"
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric (4 points toward the checkpoint).
    Rubric: Claim: warmer water dissolves sugar faster; Evidence uses at least two data points with units
    Model answer: Claim: Sugar dissolves faster in warmer water. Evidence: In 10 °C water it took 98 seconds, but in 50 °C water it took only 31 seconds, about three times faster.

Answer key: printable page

  1. What do the letters C, E and R stand for in a conclusion? Describe each in a few words.
    Answer: Claim (answers the question), Evidence (data with units), Reasoning (science explanation).
  2. Write a testable question about something in your kitchen. Name the independent and dependent variables.
    Answer: For example: Does water temperature change how fast salt dissolves? IV: water temperature; DV: time to dissolve (s).
  3. Why should a scientist run three trials instead of one?
    Answer: One trial might be off because of an error or chance; averaging three gives more reliable results.
  4. Explain what replication is and why it matters.
    Answer: Repeating another scientist's investigation; if others get the same results, the finding is more trustworthy.
  5. Draw a data table for your own investigation with units in every heading and room for three trials and an average.
    Answer: For example: Water temperature (°C) | Trial 1 (s) | Trial 2 (s) | Trial 3 (s) | Average (s).
  6. Name one limit of an investigation you have done this unit.
    Answer: For example: few trials, only one location, a thermometer that may read differently.
