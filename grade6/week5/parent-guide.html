Grade 6 · Week 5 · Parent Guide

Week 5: Projects and the Unit 1 Checkpoint

This is the week everything comes together. Your child finishes three projects (a personal narrative, a science investigation and a museum exhibit), learns GCF, LCM and savings goals in math, and takes a checkpoint in every subject.

How scoring works: each lesson scores itself. Your child gets two tries per question: full points on the first try, half points on the second, then the answer and explanation appear. A score of 85 or higher means the lesson is mastered. Written answers go to Jonathan, who scores them with a rubric.

Time this week: About 60 minutes of live lessons per subject. Plan extra time after class this week: about 2 hours for the science investigation (spread over several days), 2 hours for the museum exhibit, 1 hour to finish the narrative, and 30 to 40 minutes for each checkpoint.

Gather this week: 36 and 48 small counters (beans or coins); the price of something your child wants to save for; your child's Week 4 story draft and a family audience; materials for the science project your child chooses (see their plan) plus a thermometer, ruler, timer or measuring cup; a table or trifold board, index cards and the Unit 1 artifacts (Week 4 flour, Week 3 dig box objects, Week 1 interview).

Heads up: Checkpoints are done independently after class, without a calculator in math. Mastery is 85. Below 85 is not failure: your child gets a review lesson on what was missed and retakes it. The project directions and rubrics, the Unit 1 supply list and the book list are linked at the top of the Grade 6 page.

ELA: Revising, Editing and the Personal Narrative Project

Your child revises the story using ARMS (Add, Remove, Move, Substitute), edits it using CUPS (Capitals, Usage, Punctuation, Spelling), reads it aloud from the Author's Chair, and publishes the final version. The checkpoint uses an original story and Robert Frost's "The Road Not Taken."

Ask at dinner: "What is the biggest change you made from your draft, and why?"

Try it together: Be the audience for the Author's Chair. Give Praise, Question, Polish: one thing that worked, one real question, one suggestion.

Signs it's clicking: They can tell revising (making it better) from editing (making it correct), and they choose feedback that fits their story. Signs they need help: They only fix spelling and call it revised. Ask them to show you one sentence they rewrote.

Math: GCF, LCM, Savings Goals and the Unit 1 Checkpoint

Your child uses factor trees to find the greatest common factor and least common multiple, solves real problems with them, and builds a savings plan for something they want.

Ask at dinner: "If you save the same amount each week, how many weeks until you can buy it? Why do we round up?"

Try it together: Review your child's savings plan and agree on a weekly check-in day.

Signs it's clicking: They can tell whether a problem calls for the GCF (equal groups) or the LCM (when things line up). Signs they need help: They mix up GCF and LCM, or think numbers like 51 are prime (51 = 3 × 17).

Life Science: The Investigation Project and the Unit 1 Checkpoint

Your child plans and runs a fair test at home with three trials, graphs the data, and writes a Claim, Evidence, Reasoning conclusion. They also learn why scientists repeat each other's work, through the story of NASA mathematician Katherine Johnson.

Ask at dinner: "What is the one thing you are changing, and what are you measuring?"

Try it together: Approve the project plan for safety, and help with timing or measuring if asked, without doing the thinking.

Signs it's clicking: Their conclusion uses real numbers with units and explains why the result happened. Signs they need help: They change two things at once, or leave out a result that surprised them. Remind them surprising data is still data.

World and American History: The Museum Exhibit Project and the Unit 1 Checkpoint

Your child learns how museums choose and label objects, discusses who owns the past (including the 2022 return of 29 Benin Bronzes to Nigeria by the Smithsonian), and builds a home museum exhibit that answers a big question about Unit 1.

Ask at dinner: "Whose story does your exhibit tell, and whose story might be missing?"

Try it together: Take the 3-minute tour and tell your child which object you will remember and why.

Signs it's clicking: Every object has a label with a story, and the whole exhibit answers one question. Signs they need help: The exhibit lists facts without a question, or labels are too long. Suggest two sentences per story.
