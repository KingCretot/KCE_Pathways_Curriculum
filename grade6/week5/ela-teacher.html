Unit 1: Stories That Shape Us (Narrative) · Week 5 · ELA · Teacher Plan

Unit 1 Checkpoint and Personal Narrative Project

Lesson length: 60 minutes live, plus the after-class interactive lesson

Objectives

  • Revise a draft using ARMS (Add, Remove, Move, Substitute).
  • Edit a draft using CUPS (Capitals, Usage, Punctuation, Spelling).
  • Give and use specific feedback, and read your work aloud with expression.
  • Publish the final Personal Narrative Project and score it with the rubric.
  • Show mastery of Unit 1 reading, vocabulary and conventions on the checkpoint (85 or higher).

Standards

  • ELA.6.C.1.2: Write personal or fictional narratives using narrative techniques, precise words and phrases, and figurative language.
  • ELA.6.C.1.5: Improve writing by planning, revising, and editing, considering feedback from adults and peers.
  • ELA.6.C.3.1: Follow the rules of standard English grammar, punctuation, capitalization, and spelling appropriate to grade level.
  • ELA.6.C.2.1: Present information orally, in a logical sequence, using nonverbal cues, appropriate volume, clear pronunciation, and appropriate pacing.
  • ELA.6.R.1.2: Analyze the development of stated or implied theme(s) throughout a literary text (checkpoint).
  • ELA.6.R.1.3: Explain the influence of multiple narrators and/or shifts in point of view in a literary text (checkpoint).
  • ELA.6.V.1.2: Apply knowledge of Greek and Latin roots and affixes to determine meanings of words and phrases in grade-level content (checkpoint).

Materials

  • The interactive lesson
  • The student's Week 4 draft (printed if possible)
  • Two highlighters or colored pencils (one for ARMS, one for CUPS)
  • The Unit 1 Projects page with the narrative rubric
  • Printable practice page

Vocabulary

  • revise: To make writing better by changing ideas, words, details or order.
  • edit: To make writing correct by fixing capitals, grammar, punctuation and spelling.
  • feedback: Specific comments from a reader that help a writer improve.
  • publish: To share a finished piece of writing with an audience.
  • reflection: Thinking and writing about what you learned and how you grew.
  • re-: A Latin prefix meaning again or back, as in revise (to see again) and rewrite.

Lesson flow

5 minReconnect
Run the Reconnect questions.
5 minHook
Discuss rewriting. Ask the student which part of their draft they are least happy with.
15 minARMS on the student's draft
Model ARMS on the Before/After example, then work through the student's draft together. Mark at least one change for each letter.
10 minCUPS
Model the CUPS read-through on one paragraph of the student's draft, one letter at a time.
10 minFeedback
Practice Praise, Question, Polish on the mentor text, then give the student feedback on their draft the same way.
10 minProject and checkpoint
Review the narrative project rubric together. Explain that the final story, the Author's Chair activity and the checkpoint are completed after class.
5 minClose
Set the date the final story is due and the date for the Author's Chair reading.

Key questions to ask

  • Is this change revising or editing?
  • Where can a reader feel your climax most?
  • What feedback will you use, and why?
  • How did you grow as a writer this unit?

Common mistakes to watch for

  • Thinking revision only means fixing spelling.
  • Using every suggestion instead of choosing the ones that fit the writer's goal.
  • Reading the checkpoint poem as a simple message to take the unusual path, missing that the roads were about the same.

Support (if the student is struggling)

  • Revise one paragraph at a time with the ARMS card beside it.
  • Read the draft aloud to the student so they hear where it stumbles.
  • Allow the student to dictate the reflection and then type it.

Challenge (if the student is ready for more)

  • Rewrite the opening paragraph two different ways and choose the stronger one.
  • Write a short paragraph explaining why many readers misunderstand "The Road Not Taken."

Background for the teacher

The Personal Narrative Project is scored with the five-trait rubric on the Unit 1 Projects page (4 points per trait, 20 total; convert to 100 by multiplying by 5). Mastery is 85 (17 of 20). The checkpoint uses an original KCE story and Robert Frost's "The Road Not Taken," first published in The Atlantic Monthly in August 1915 and collected in Mountain Interval (1916); it is in the public domain. Frost wrote the poem partly as a gentle joke about his friend Edward Thomas, who often regretted whichever path they had not taken on their walks. The Hemingway story about rewriting the ending of A Farewell to Arms 39 times comes from his 1958 Paris Review interview.

Sources

  • Poetry Foundation: Robert Frost, "The Road Not Taken."
  • The Paris Review, "Ernest Hemingway, The Art of Fiction No. 21" (1958).
  • CPALMS: Florida B.E.S.T. ELA Grade 6 benchmarks.

How the lesson runs

During the live lesson, the student shares their screen and answers the Reconnect questions and the Learn checks live while you teach. The Hands-On activity, Think Deeper and Exit Check are completed after class.

Scoring

The interactive lesson scores itself: full points on the first try, half points on the second try, then the answer and explanation appear. Mastery is 85. Written responses arrive in your results email with the score; score them with the rubric below before the student moves on.

Checkpoint scoring

Checkpoint questions are scored separately from the lesson. The student gets one try per question and the answers are not shown, so the checkpoint measures independent mastery and a retake stays fair. Your results email shows the auto-scored checkpoint points, a list of each item marked correct or MISSED, and the points available for the written items. Score the written items with their rubrics, then: checkpoint score = (auto points + rubric points) / total possible × 100. Mastery is 85. Below 85: teach a targeted review lesson on the parts missed (A to D), then the student retakes the checkpoint. Record both attempts in the Weekly Score Log.

Answer key: interactive lesson

Reconnect

  1. Punctuate by typing it with quotation marks, capitals and punctuation: are you ready asked Coach
    Answer: "Are you ready?" asked Coach.
    The question mark goes inside the closing quotation mark because the spoken words are the question.
  2. Revise with a strong verb and a precise noun: The dog went under the thing.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Strong verb replaces went; Precise noun replaces thing
    Model answer: The beagle wriggled under the porch steps.
  3. What does the root chron mean?
    Answer: time
    Chron means time, as in chronological.
  4. Name one transition that shows time has passed.
    Answer: For example: later that day
    Transitions guide the reader through time.
  5. In one or two sentences, what is your personal narrative about, and what is its climax?
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Names the true event; Identifies the climax
    Model answer: My story is about learning to jump off the high dive at the community pool. The climax is the moment I step off the board.

Learn 1: Revising With ARMS

  1. Name the ARMS move in each revision and explain why it improves the story: (a) went becomes scrambled ; (b) a paragraph about breakfast is deleted because it has nothing to do with the climax.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: (a) Substitute, with a reason (shows how the person moved); (b) Remove, with a reason (keeps the focus on what matters)
    Model answer: (a) Substitute: scrambled shows how the person moved, quickly and clumsily, so the reader can picture it. (b) Remove: the breakfast paragraph slowed the story and had nothing to do with the climax, so cutting it keeps the focus on the important moment.
  2. Revise this sentence by adding two sensory details and one thought: I walked into the new school.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Two different sensory details; A thought from the narrator; Still one moment, not a summary
    Model answer: The hallway smelled like floor polish and pencil shavings, and a locker slammed somewhere like a gunshot. I wondered if everyone could tell I had never been there before.

Learn 2: Editing With CUPS

  1. Edit this sentence by retyping it correctly: last summer my cousin and me visited atlanta
    Answer: Last summer, my cousin and I visited Atlanta.
    Capitalize Last and Atlanta; use I (subject), not me; add a period.
  2. This passage switches tense. Rewrite it all in past tense: I grabbed the rope. Then I swing out over the river and let go.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: All verbs in past tense: grabbed, swung, let go
    Model answer: I grabbed the rope. Then I swung out over the river and let go.
  3. In Everyone grabbed their towel and ran to his car, the pronoun his does not match. What pronoun should replace it? Type one word.
    Answer: their
    Keep pronouns consistent within the sentence.

Learn 3: Feedback That Helps

  1. Here is the Week 4 mentor Version B: The clock read 0:04. Dani's pass smacked into my palms, and suddenly the whole gym went quiet, like someone had pressed mute. I could smell the floor wax and feel my heartbeat in my ears. I bent my knees, the way Coach Ortiz drilled us a thousand times, and let the ball roll off my fingertips. It hung in the air forever. Then: swish. Write feedback for that writer using Praise, Question, Polish.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Specific praise naming a detail; A genuine question; A specific suggestion
    Model answer: Praise: The simile about the gym going quiet like someone pressed mute made me feel the silence. Question: What were you thinking right before you shot? Polish: You could add one thought in your own words so we know how you felt, not just what happened.

Hands-On

  1. Write down the feedback you received (Praise, Question, Polish) and explain which suggestion you used and how you changed your story.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Records feedback from at least one listener; Names the change made; Explains why the change improved the story
    Model answer: Praise: My mom liked how I described the water as brown and cold. Question: My brother asked how high the rope swing was. Polish: Mom said the ending felt fast. I added two sentences at the end about how I felt walking home and how I jumped again the next day, so the reader can see I changed.

Think Deeper

  1. Paste or type the final version of your personal narrative. Give it a title.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Ideas and focus: a true, focused story with a clear problem, climax and change; Organization: chronological order, transitions, an opening that hooks and an ending that reflects; Narrative craft: dialogue, sensory details, strong verbs and a slowed-down climax; Voice and point of view: consistent first person that sounds like the writer; Conventions: correct capitals, usage, punctuation (including dialogue) and spelling
    Model answer: (Teacher note: no single model fits a personal story. Score with the five-trait project rubric. A Level 4 story is 400 to 800 words, has a title, opens in the scene, uses at least three lines of correctly punctuated dialogue, spends the most words on the climax, and ends by reflecting on how the writer changed.)
  2. Reflection: Compare your Week 4 draft with your final story. Name two specific changes you made (use ARMS words), and explain what you learned about yourself as a writer.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Two specific revisions named with ARMS terms; Explains why each made the story better; Honest reflection on growth as a writer
    Model answer: First, I substituted weak verbs: "I went into the water" became "I plunged into the cold, brown water." Second, I added a paragraph at the end about jumping again the next day, which shows how I changed. I learned that my first draft tells what happened, but revising lets the reader feel it. I am better at details than endings, so next time I will plan my ending first.

Checkpoint: Part A: Read the story

  1. The Recipe Card An original story by KingCretot Experience The recipe card was soft as cloth, stained orange at one corner where a drop of sweet potato had landed years ago. Keisha held it up to the kitchen window. Her grandmother's handwriting slanted like it was running somewhere. "You can't even read half of it," said her brother Andre, leaning over her shoulder. "Just look one up online." Keisha didn't answer. Grandma Ruth had made her sweet potato pie every Thanksgiving for forty years, and this was the first Thanksgiving without her. A recipe from a website would not taste like Grandma's kitchen in Savannah, with the radio playing gospel and flour on every surface. She squinted. 2 cups mashed. 1 cup sugar. Nutmeg and then a word that looked like a tiny bird. Bake till it tells you. "Till it tells you?" Andre laughed. "Pies don't talk." Keisha mashed, measured and stirred. When she reached the bird-word, she called her great-aunt Lorraine. Aunt Lorraine laughed so hard she had to put the phone down. "That's not a word, baby. That's a pinch. Your grandmother drew a pinch." The pie went into the oven. Keisha sat on the floor and watched through the glass for almost an hour, until the center stopped jiggling and the edges cracked just a little, like the top of a loaf of bread. "It's telling me," she whispered. At dinner, Andre took a bite and stopped talking. Then he quietly asked for a second slice, and later, without being asked, he slid the recipe card into a plastic sleeve so it would never stain again. What is Keisha's main problem in the story?
    Answer: She wants to make her late grandmother's pie but cannot read parts of the handwritten recipe card.
    The recipe card is hard to read, and Keisha wants the pie to taste like Grandma's, not a website recipe.
  2. From what point of view is the story told? Type first person, third person limited or third person omniscient.
    Answer: third person limited
    The narrator uses she and her and shares only Keisha's thoughts and feelings.
  3. Explain why the moment Keisha whispers, "It's telling me," is the climax of the story.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric (3 points toward the checkpoint).
    Rubric: Identifies it as the turning point; Connects it to solving the problem (she understands Grandma's words and succeeds)
    Model answer: It is the turning point: after struggling to read the card, Keisha finally understands what Grandma meant by "bake till it tells you," and she knows the pie will be right.
  4. How does Andre change from the beginning to the end? Use two pieces of evidence from the story. Name one STEAL method the author uses to show the change.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric (4 points toward the checkpoint).
    Rubric: Beginning: doubtful or mocking ("Just look one up online", "Pies don't talk"); End: respectful, quiet, protects the card; Two pieces of text evidence; Names a STEAL method (actions, speech)
    Model answer: At first Andre mocks the recipe: he says "Just look one up online" and laughs that "Pies don't talk." By the end he is quiet and respectful. He asks for a second slice and, without being asked, puts the card in a plastic sleeve so it will never stain again. The author shows his change through his actions instead of telling us how he feels.
  5. Write a theme statement for "The Recipe Card" as a complete, universal sentence.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric (4 points toward the checkpoint).
    Rubric: Complete sentence; Universal, about life, not about Keisha; Fits the story (family memory, tradition, love lasting)
    Model answer: The people we love stay with us through the traditions we carry on.

Checkpoint: Part B: Read the poem

  1. The Road Not Taken by Robert Frost (first published in 1915 in The Atlantic Monthly and collected in Mountain Interval , 1916; in the public domain) Two roads diverged in a yellow wood, And sorry I could not travel both And be one traveler, long I stood And looked down one as far as I could To where it bent in the undergrowth; Then took the other, as just as fair, And having perhaps the better claim, Because it was grassy and wanted wear; Though as for that the passing there Had worn them really about the same, And both that morning equally lay In leaves no step had trodden black. Oh, I kept the first for another day! Yet knowing how way leads on to way, I doubted if I should ever come back. I shall be telling this with a sigh Somewhere ages and ages hence: Two roads diverged in a wood, and I— I took the one less traveled by, And that has made all the difference. In the poem, what does the choice between two roads stand for in real life?
    Answer: an important choice or decision in life
    The roads are an extended metaphor for choices in life.
  2. In the second and third stanzas, how different are the two roads really? Quote a line as evidence, then explain why this matters for understanding the last stanza.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric (4 points toward the checkpoint).
    Rubric: The roads are about the same; Quotes a line such as "Had worn them really about the same" or "both that morning equally lay"; Explains that the speaker will later tell the story as if his choice made all the difference, so the poem is about how we tell the story of our choices
    Model answer: The roads are really about the same. The speaker says the passing there "Had worn them really about the same." This matters because in the last stanza he imagines telling people one day that he took the road "less traveled by." The poem shows how we tell the story of our choices later, making them seem more different than they were.
  3. Which word in the last stanza suggests the speaker may have mixed feelings, or even some regret, about his choice? Type the word.
    Answer: sigh
    "I shall be telling this with a sigh" hints that the speaker is not simply proud; a sigh can show regret, relief or longing.

Checkpoint: Part C: Vocabulary

  1. Using roots, what does autobiography mean?
    Answer: the story of a person's life written by that person
    auto (self) + bio (life) + graph (write).
  2. Using roots, what does spectator mean?
    Answer: a person who watches
    spect means look or see.
  3. Using roots, what does interrupt mean?
    Answer: to break in between (stop someone while they are talking)
    inter (between) + rupt (break).
  4. Using roots, what does photograph literally mean?
    Answer: writing (or drawing) with light
    photo (light) + graph (write).

Checkpoint: Part D: Conventions

  1. Punctuate by typing it: this pie is perfect said Andre
    Answer: "This pie is perfect," said Andre.
    Quotation marks around the spoken words, a comma or exclamation point inside the closing mark, then the tag.
  2. Fix the pronoun error by retyping the sentence: Keisha and me baked the pie.
    Answer: Keisha and I baked the pie.
    Use I as part of the subject. Test it: "I baked the pie," not "me baked the pie."
  3. Revise this sentence using Add and Substitute, then name each move you made: I was really scared when I went up.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric (3 points toward the checkpoint).
    Rubric: Adds a sensory detail or thought; Substitutes a stronger verb for went; Names both moves correctly
    Model answer: My knees shook as I climbed the slippery ladder, and I thought, don't look down. (Add: shaking knees and the thought. Substitute: climbed instead of went.)

Answer key: printable page

  1. Explain the difference between revising and editing.
    Answer: Revising makes writing better (ideas, words, order); editing makes it correct (capitals, usage, punctuation, spelling).
  2. What do the letters in ARMS and CUPS stand for?
    Answer: Add, Remove, Move, Substitute; Capitals, Usage, Punctuation, Spelling
  3. Edit: yesterday me and jamal walked to the park he said lets race
    Answer: Yesterday Jamal and I walked to the park. He said, "Let's race."
  4. Revise with a strong verb and a sensory detail: The cat went across the kitchen.
    Answer: For example: The cat crept across the cold tile floor, its claws clicking.
  5. Write helpful feedback (Praise, Question, Polish) for a friend's story about a soccer game.
    Answer: For example: Praise: I could hear the crowd when you described the drums. Question: what did your coach say at halftime? Polish: slow down the moment you kicked the winning goal.
  6. Copy one sentence from your draft. Then rewrite it using one ARMS move. Name the move.
    Answer: For example: I went into the water. → I plunged into the cold, brown water. (Substitute and Add.)
