Unit 1: How We Know the Past · Week 5 · World and American History · Teacher Plan

Unit 1 Checkpoint and Museum Exhibit Project

Lesson length: 60 minutes live, plus the after-class interactive lesson

Objectives

  • Explain how museums choose, interpret and display evidence about the past.
  • Write clear museum labels with a title, place, date, material and story.
  • Discuss who owns the past and why some museums are returning objects to the countries they came from.
  • Plan and build a museum exhibit that answers a big question about Unit 1.
  • Show mastery of Unit 1 on the checkpoint (85 or higher).

Standards

  • SS.6.W.1.3: Interpret primary and secondary sources.
  • SS.6.W.1.4: Describe the methods of historical inquiry and how history relates to the other social sciences.
  • SS.6.W.1.5: Describe the roles of historians and recognize varying historical interpretations (historiography).
  • SS.6.W.1.6: Describe how history transmits culture and heritage and provides models of human character.
  • C3 Framework D4.2.6-8: Construct explanations using reasoning, correct sequence, examples, and details with relevant information and data, while acknowledging the strengths and weaknesses of the explanations.

Materials

  • The interactive lesson
  • Index cards or card stock for labels
  • A table, shelf or trifold board
  • The student's Unit 1 work and artifacts
  • The Unit 1 Projects page with the exhibit rubric
  • Printable practice page

Vocabulary

  • curator: A person who chooses, researches and arranges the objects in a museum exhibit.
  • exhibit: A collection of objects, images and labels arranged to tell a story or answer a question.
  • artifact: An object made or used by people in the past.
  • replica: An exact copy of an object, often used when the original is too fragile or valuable to display.
  • interpretation: An explanation of what evidence means. Different people may interpret the same evidence differently.
  • repatriation: Returning objects or remains to the country or community they were taken from.

Key questions to ask

  • What is your exhibit's big question?
  • Why did you choose this object over another?
  • Whose stories are told in your exhibit, and whose are missing?
  • What evidence supports each label?

Common mistakes to watch for

  • Thinking museum labels are simply facts with no interpretation.
  • Believing every museum object was obtained fairly.
  • Making an exhibit that lists facts instead of answering a question.

Support (if the student is struggling)

  • Provide a label template with blanks for each part.
  • Limit the exhibit to five objects with short labels.
  • Let the student dictate labels before typing.

Challenge (if the student is ready for more)

  • Add a section showing two different interpretations of the same evidence.
  • Research one artifact that has been repatriated and add a label about its journey home.

Background for the teacher

British forces attacked Benin City in February 1897 and removed thousands of objects. The Smithsonian's National Museum of African Art transferred ownership of 29 Benin Bronzes to Nigeria's National Commission for Museums and Monuments in October 2022. Germany returned 20 Benin Bronzes to Nigeria in December 2022. The checkpoint BCE/CE item accepts both 1,000 and 999, because there is no year 0. The Museum Exhibit Project is scored with the five-trait rubric on the Unit 1 Projects page (20 points; multiply by 5 for a score out of 100; mastery 85).

How the lesson runs

During the live lesson, the student shares their screen and answers the Reconnect questions and the Learn checks live while you teach. The Hands-On activity, Think Deeper and Exit Check are completed after class.

Scoring

The interactive lesson scores itself: full points on the first try, half points on the second try, then the answer and explanation appear. Mastery is 85. Written responses arrive in your results email with the score; score them with the rubric below before the student moves on.

Checkpoint scoring

Checkpoint questions are scored separately from the lesson. The student gets one try per question and the answers are not shown, so the checkpoint measures independent mastery and a retake stays fair. Your results email shows the auto-scored checkpoint points, a list of each item marked correct or MISSED, and the points available for the written items. Score the written items with their rubrics, then: checkpoint score = (auto points + rubric points) / total possible × 100. Mastery is 85. Below 85: teach a targeted review lesson on the parts missed (A to D), then the student retakes the checkpoint. Record both attempts in the Weekly Score Log.

Answer key: interactive lesson

Reconnect

  1. A diary written by a soldier during a war is what kind of source? Explain how you know.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Primary source; Created at the time by someone who was there
    Model answer: It is a primary source, because it was written at the time by someone who lived through the events.
  2. Why does Greenland look as big as Africa on a Mercator map? Which kind of projection shows their true sizes?
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Mercator stretches land near the poles; An equal-area projection such as Gall-Peters shows true size
    Model answer: Mercator stretches land more and more toward the poles, so Greenland looks huge. An equal-area projection like Gall-Peters shows that Africa is about 14 times larger.
  3. Name the site in Morocco where the oldest known Homo sapiens fossils were found.
    Answer: Jebel Irhoud
    About 300,000 years old.
  4. Pick one thing you learned this unit that changed how you think about the past. Explain the change.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Names a specific idea from Unit 1; Explains what they thought before and after
    Model answer: I used to think farming was invented in one place and spread. Now I know people on several continents, including Africa and the Americas, started farming on their own, which shows people everywhere were inventive.

Learn 1: How Museums Tell Stories

  1. The Blombos label says replica . What does that tell the visitor?
    Answer: the object is an exact copy, not the original
    A replica is an exact copy, used when the original is too fragile or valuable.
  2. Write a museum label (title, place and date, material, story in 2 to 3 sentences, and a question) for the Lomekwi stone tools. Use your Week 3 notes for their age and place.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Title; Place and date (Lomekwi, Kenya; about 3.3 million years ago); Material (stone); Story explains why it matters; A question for the visitor
    Model answer: Lomekwi Stone Tools. Lomekwi, near Lake Turkana, Kenya, about 3.3 million years ago. Stone. These are the oldest stone tools ever found, made before our own species existed. Someone struck these rocks on purpose to make sharp edges, which shows early hominins planned and solved problems. Question: what do you think these tools were used to cut?

Learn 2: Who Owns the Past?

  1. How many Benin Bronzes did the Smithsonian return to Nigeria in 2022?
    Answer: 29
    The National Museum of African Art returned 29 Benin Bronzes in October 2022.
  2. Give one argument for keeping an artifact in a museum far from where it was made and one argument for returning it. Which argument do you find stronger, and why?
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: One fair argument for keeping; One fair argument for returning; States a position with a reason; Respectful of both sides
    Model answer: Some say a big museum can protect the object and let millions of visitors from around the world learn from it. Others say it was taken by force, so it belongs to the community whose ancestors made it, and they should tell its story. I think the argument for returning is stronger when the object was stolen, because a museum should not profit from a theft, but museums can still borrow objects or make replicas.

Learn 3: Planning Your Museum Exhibit

  1. Write your big question, the five objects you plan to include, and one sentence about why each object helps answer the question.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: A clear big question; Five objects listed; Each object connected to the question; Uses evidence from at least three different weeks
    Model answer: Big question: Was farming a good trade for the first farmers? 1. My jar of hand-ground flour: shows how much daily work grinding took. 2. A drawing of a saddle quern: the tool women used for hours each day. 3. A printed photo of an ear of maize beside teosinte: shows how farmers changed plants. 4. A clay model of a Çatalhöyük house: shows crowded village life. 5. A chart comparing forager and farmer heights: evidence that early farmers were often less healthy.

Hands-On

  1. Type your introduction panel and all five object labels.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Introduction panel states the big question and answer; Five labels, each with title, place and date, material, story and question; Facts are accurate and drawn from Unit 1; Clear, short writing for visitors
    Model answer: Was Farming a Good Trade? Beginning about 11,000 years ago, people in at least seven regions of the world independently began to farm. Farming gave villages extra food and let towns grow, but the first farmers worked harder and were often less healthy than the foragers before them. Explore the evidence and decide for yourself. Label 1: Hand-Ground Flour. Made in my kitchen, 2026, recreating a method from about 10,000 years ago. Wheat berries. It took me 10 minutes to grind 2 tablespoons. One loaf would take about 4 hours. Question: who do you think did this work every day? (Labels 2 to 5 follow the same pattern.)
  2. After your tour, what did your visitors remember most, and what would you change if you built the exhibit again?
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Reports visitor feedback; Names a specific improvement
    Model answer: My grandmother remembered the flour jar most because she could not believe one loaf took four hours. My brother said the labels were too long, so next time I would cut each story to two sentences.

Think Deeper

  1. A curator has space for only one of two objects in an exhibit about early farming: a beautiful gold necklace from a ruler's tomb, or a worn grinding stone used by an ordinary woman. Which should the curator choose, and what does the choice say about whose history matters? Use evidence from Unit 1.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Makes a clear choice; Explains what each object reveals about the past; Discusses whose stories museums tell; Uses Unit 1 evidence (grinding, skeletons, specialists, inequality)
    Model answer: I would choose the grinding stone. The gold necklace shows that some people had great wealth, which connects to the inequality that grew after farming created surpluses. But the grinding stone tells the story of most people's lives. Skeletons from early farming villages show worn toes, knees and backs from hours of grinding each day. Museums have often shown the treasures of rulers, so choosing the grinding stone tells visitors that ordinary people's work is history too.

Checkpoint: Part A: Historians and sources

  1. A modern textbook chapter about ancient Kemet is what kind of source?
    Answer: a secondary source
    It was written long afterward by someone who studied primary sources.
  2. How many years apart are 800 BCE and 200 CE?
    Answer: 1,000 (999 if you account for there being no year 0)
    From 800 BCE to 1 BCE is 799 years, 1 BCE to 1 CE is 1 more (there is no year 0), and 1 CE to 200 CE is 199 more: 999 exactly. Rounding gives about 1,000, so both are accepted.
  3. Why should a historian compare more than one source about the same event? Give an example.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric (4 points toward the checkpoint).
    Rubric: Sources can be biased, incomplete or from one point of view; Example comparing two perspectives
    Model answer: One source may tell only one side or leave things out. For example, a British soldier's account of the 1897 attack on Benin City would describe it very differently from the memories of people in Benin.

Checkpoint: Part B: Maps and geography

  1. On a Mercator map, Greenland looks about the same size as Africa. Which is really larger, by about how many times, and why does Mercator distort it?
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric (3 points toward the checkpoint).
    Rubric: Africa; About 14 times; Mercator stretches areas near the poles
    Model answer: Africa is about 14 times larger. Mercator stretches land more and more toward the poles, so Greenland looks far bigger than it is.
  2. Timbuktu is at about 17°N, 3°W. Which two hemispheres is it in?
    Answer: Northern and Western
    North of the equator and west of the prime meridian.

Checkpoint: Part C: Human beginnings

  1. About how many million years old are the Lomekwi stone tools, the oldest known?
    Answer: about 3.3 million years
    About 3.3 million years, older than our own species.
  2. Use two pieces of evidence from Week 3 to explain why scientists say all living humans share African roots.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric (4 points toward the checkpoint).
    Rubric: Oldest Homo sapiens fossils found in Africa (Jebel Irhoud, Omo Kibish, Florisbad); Genetic evidence: Africa has the greatest human genetic diversity, or all humans share about 99.9% of DNA
    Model answer: The oldest Homo sapiens fossils, about 300,000 years old, come from Jebel Irhoud in Morocco and other African sites. DNA also shows that Africa has the greatest human genetic diversity, which means humans have lived there the longest before groups spread to other continents.

Checkpoint: Part D: The first farmers

  1. In which region of Africa was sorghum first domesticated?
    Answer: Sudan and the eastern Sahel
    Evidence from eastern Sudan dates to about 5,000 years ago.
  2. Explain one way farming helped early communities and one way it made life harder, with evidence.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric (4 points toward the checkpoint).
    Rubric: A benefit with evidence (surplus, specialists, towns); A cost with evidence (shorter stature, tooth decay, hard labor, disease)
    Model answer: Farming created a surplus of stored grain, which let towns like Çatalhöyük grow and let some people become potters and builders. But skeletons show many early farmers were shorter and had more tooth decay than foragers, because they ate only a few crops.
  3. A museum label says only: Bronze head, Nigeria. Rewrite it as a curator would, then name one important thing the original label left out.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric (4 points toward the checkpoint).
    Rubric: Title, place and date, material; A short story of why it matters; A visitor question; Names something left out (who made it, the 1897 attack, how the museum got it)
    Model answer: Bronze Head of an Oba. Kingdom of Benin, in present-day Nigeria, 1500s. Cast bronze. Benin artists made heads like this to honor kings and their ancestors. British soldiers took it during the attack on Benin City in 1897. Question: who do you think should decide where it lives today? The original label left out who made it and how the museum got it.

Answer key: printable page

  1. Name the five parts of a museum label.
    Answer: Title; place and date; material; story; question for the visitor
  2. Write a label for an object from your own home that tells something about your family's history.
    Answer: For example: Grandpa's Toolbox. Tampa, Florida, about 1975. Metal and wood. My grandfather carried this to every job as a carpenter for 40 years. Question: what tool do you think he used most?
  3. Define repatriation and give one example.
    Answer: Returning objects to the country or community they were taken from; for example, the Smithsonian returned 29 Benin Bronzes to Nigeria in 2022.
  4. What is the difference between a primary and a secondary source? Give an example of each from Unit 1.
    Answer: Primary: made at the time (a stone tool, a griot's account); secondary: made later by someone studying it (a textbook).
  5. Write the big question for your exhibit and list your five objects.
    Answer: For example: Was farming a good trade? Hand-ground flour; saddle quern drawing; teosinte and maize photo; Çatalhöyük clay house; forager and farmer height chart.
  6. Explain one way a museum can show more than one point of view.
    Answer: For example: include labels written with the community the object came from, or show two interpretations side by side.
