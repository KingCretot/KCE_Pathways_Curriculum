Unit 4: The Human Body · Week 19 · Life Science · Teacher Plan

Digestive and Nervous Systems

Lesson length: 60 minutes live, plus the after-class interactive lesson

Objectives

  • Trace the path of food through the digestive system and describe mechanical and chemical digestion.
  • Explain how the small intestine's structure helps it absorb nutrients.
  • Describe the parts of the nervous system (brain, spinal cord, nerves, neurons) and how signals travel.
  • Explain the difference between voluntary actions and reflexes.
  • Measure and analyze reaction time, and explain how the digestive and nervous systems interact with other systems to maintain homeostasis.

Standards

  • SC.6.L.14.5: Identify and investigate the general functions of the major systems of the human body (digestive, respiratory, circulatory, reproductive, excretory, immune, nervous, and musculoskeletal) and describe ways these systems interact with each other to maintain homeostasis.
  • SC.6.N.1.1: Define a problem from the sixth grade curriculum, use appropriate reference materials to support scientific understanding, plan and carry out scientific investigation of various types, such as systematic observations or experiments, identify variables, collect and organize data, interpret data in charts, tables, and graphics, analyze information, make predictions, and defend conclusions.
  • SC.6.N.3.4: Identify the role of models in the context of the sixth grade science benchmarks.
  • SC.6.N.2.3: Recognize that scientists who make contributions to scientific knowledge come from all kinds of backgrounds and possess varied talents, interests, and goals.

Materials

  • The interactive lesson
  • A gallon zipper bag, crackers, water, vinegar, old tights, scissors (adult), a bowl
  • A 30-cm ruler
  • Paper and pencil
  • Printable practice page

Vocabulary

  • mechanical digestion: Physically breaking food into smaller pieces, as by chewing or churning.
  • chemical digestion: Using enzymes and acids to break food into molecules the body can absorb.
  • enzyme: A protein that speeds up a chemical reaction, such as breaking down food.
  • villi: Tiny fingerlike folds lining the small intestine that absorb nutrients.
  • neuron: A nerve cell that carries electrical and chemical signals.
  • reflex: A fast, automatic response that doesn't wait for the brain to decide.
  • central nervous system: The brain and spinal cord.

Key questions to ask

  • Is this mechanical or chemical?
  • How does this structure fit its function?
  • Does this signal go to the brain or only to the spinal cord?
  • Which systems are working together here?

Common mistakes to watch for

  • Most digestion happens in the stomach (most chemical digestion and absorption happen in the small intestine).
  • Food passes through the liver and pancreas.
  • Nerves carry signals instantly.
  • Reflexes are controlled by the brain.

Support (if the student is struggling)

  • Label the digestive diagram together before the lesson.
  • Act out a reflex arc with three people passing a message.
  • Provide the reaction-time conversion chart.

Challenge (if the student is ready for more)

  • Research how helpful bacteria in the large intestine affect health.
  • Graph reaction time against the number of practice trials and describe the trend.

Background for the teacher

Beaumont's observations of St. Martin ran from 1825 to 1833 and were published in 1833. The small intestine is about 6 to 7 meters (20 to 23 feet) long in a living adult. Neuron estimates: about 86 billion (Azevedo et al., 2009). The fastest myelinated axons conduct at about 120 meters per second (about 268 miles per hour). The brain uses about 20 percent of resting energy. Alexa Canady completed her neurosurgery residency at the University of Minnesota (1977 to 1981), becoming the first Black woman neurosurgeon in the United States; she was board-certified in 1984 and was chief of neurosurgery at Children's Hospital of Michigan from 1987 to 2001. Reaction-time chart: t = square root of (2d ÷ 9.8 m/s²), rounded.

Answer key: interactive lesson

Reconnect

  1. Which blood vessels have walls one cell thick so materials can pass in and out?
    Answer: capillaries
    They also line the villi you'll meet today.
  2. Tiny air sacs in the lungs give a huge surface area. What are they called?
    Answer: alveoli
    Watch for another huge surface today.
  3. During exercise, which body system detects rising carbon dioxide and speeds up your breathing?
    Answer: the nervous system
    Today you study it in detail.
  4. Last week you learned blood carries oxygen and sugar to cells. Where do you think the sugar comes from, and how does it get into the blood?
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: From food, broken down by digestion; Absorbed into the blood (through the intestine walls)
    Model answer: It comes from the food we eat. Digestion breaks food down into sugar molecules, which pass through the intestine walls into the blood.

Learn 1: The Digestive Path

  1. Mechanical or chemical?
    Answer: mechanical; chemical; mechanical
    Mechanical changes size; chemical changes molecules.
  2. Put the path of food in order.
    Answer: mouth → esophagus → stomach → small intestine → large intestine
    The liver and pancreas help but food never passes through them.
  3. If you chew a plain cracker (gluten-free if you have a wheat allergy) for a full minute without swallowing, it starts to taste slightly sweet. Explain why, using the vocabulary.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: An enzyme in saliva breaks down starch; into sugar, which tastes sweet (chemical digestion)
    Model answer: Saliva contains an enzyme that breaks the cracker's starch into sugar molecules. That's chemical digestion beginning in the mouth, and the sugar tastes sweet.

Learn 2: Absorption: The Small Intestine

  1. What are the tiny fingerlike folds that absorb nutrients in the small intestine called?
    Answer: villi
    Each holds capillaries.
  2. Compare villi in the small intestine with alveoli in the lungs. How are their structures alike, and why?
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Both create huge surface area and have thin walls next to capillaries; Because both exchange materials with the blood (nutrients; oxygen and carbon dioxide)
    Model answer: Both villi and alveoli create a huge surface area with very thin walls wrapped in capillaries. That's because both have the job of moving materials into the blood: nutrients in the intestine, oxygen in the lungs.

Learn 3: The Nervous System

  1. Which part of the brain controls balance and coordination?
    Answer: the cerebellum
    The cerebrum handles thinking and voluntary movement.
  2. What is the tiny gap between two neurons called?
    Answer: a synapse
    Chemical messengers cross it.
  3. Why is it important that the brain stem controls breathing and heartbeat automatically, instead of the cerebrum controlling them on purpose?
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: You'd have to remember to breathe and beat your heart every moment, even while asleep; Automatic control keeps you alive without thinking (homeostasis)
    Model answer: If you had to choose to breathe and make your heart beat, you'd die the moment you fell asleep or got distracted. Automatic control from the brain stem keeps these vital jobs going without thinking.

Learn 4: Reflexes and Reaction Time

  1. Pulling your hand off a hot pan before you feel pain is an example of a ___.
    Answer: reflex
    The spinal cord responds before the brain decides.
  2. In a ruler-drop test, the ruler fell 20 centimeters before Ana caught it, which equals a reaction time of about 0.20 seconds. Her brother caught it after 15 centimeters, about 0.17 seconds. How much faster was her brother, in seconds?
    Answer: 0.03 seconds
    0.20 − 0.17 = 0.03 seconds.
  3. Why does a reflex skip the brain? Explain how this helps keep you safe.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: A shorter path (spinal cord only) is faster; Speed reduces injury, like a smaller burn
    Model answer: Going only to the spinal cord and back is a much shorter path than going to the brain, so the response is faster. Pulling away a fraction of a second sooner means a smaller burn.

Hands-On

  1. Describe what happened at each step of the digestion model and which body part each step represents. Name one way the model is NOT like real digestion.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Describes mouth, stomach and intestine steps with matching body parts; Identifies mechanical and chemical parts; A limit (no real enzymes, no villi or blood, vinegar is weaker than stomach acid, much faster)
    Model answer: Breaking crackers was chewing in the mouth, and the water was saliva. Squeezing the bag with vinegar was the stomach churning with acid; the crackers turned to mush. Squeezing the tights was peristalsis in the small intestine, and the liquid in the bowl was like absorbed nutrients. The model isn't like real digestion because there are no real enzymes, no villi or blood vessels, and real digestion takes hours.
  2. Record your 10 ruler-drop results, find the average for each hand, and convert to seconds. Was one hand faster? Did you improve with practice? Explain why using the nervous system.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Ten results and two correct averages; Converted to seconds with the chart; Compares hands and trials; Explains: signals travel eye → brain → spinal cord → muscle; practice and the dominant hand may speed it up
    Model answer: Right hand: 14, 12, 15, 11, 13 cm, average 13 cm, about 0.16 seconds. Left hand: 18, 17, 16, 19, 15 cm, average 17 cm, about 0.19 seconds. My right (writing) hand was faster, and I got a little faster with practice. The signal goes from my eyes to my brain, down my spinal cord and to my hand muscles, and practice helps my brain respond faster.

Think Deeper

  1. Trace what happens to the starch in a slice of bread from the moment you see it on your plate until its sugar reaches a muscle cell in your leg. Name at least four body systems and how they work together.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Nervous: seeing the food, signals to start digestion, controlling chewing and swallowing; Digestive: mechanical and chemical digestion of starch to sugar; absorption in villi; Circulatory: blood carries sugar to the leg; Respiratory (oxygen to use the sugar) or muscular (chewing, peristalsis); Correct sequence
    Model answer: My nervous system sees the bread and signals my mouth to make saliva. My muscular system helps me chew while saliva's enzyme starts breaking starch into sugar. Peristalsis pushes it down my esophagus, my stomach churns it, and in my small intestine enzymes finish turning the starch into sugar. The sugar passes through the villi into capillaries, where my circulatory system carries it to a muscle cell in my leg. My respiratory system brings in the oxygen the cell needs to release energy from that sugar.
  2. William Beaumont's experiments on Alexis St. Martin taught the world how digestion works. Was it right for Beaumont to do these experiments? Consider what we learned and whether St. Martin truly chose to take part. Explain your position and acknowledge the other view.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: A clear position; Uses facts: St. Martin's poverty, inability to read, servant contract; the scientific value; Discusses informed consent; Acknowledges the other view
    Model answer: I think the experiments were wrong as they were done, even though they taught us a lot. St. Martin was poor, couldn't read, and signed a contract to be Beaumont's servant, so it's not clear he understood or freely agreed. Some people say the discoveries saved many lives and St. Martin did get medical care and pay. That's true, but scientific discoveries don't justify using someone who can't truly say no. That's why researchers today must get informed consent.

Exit Check

  1. Where are most nutrients absorbed into the blood?
    Answer: the small intestine
    Through the villi.
  2. The brain and spinal cord together make up the ___ nervous system.
    Answer: central
    Nerves throughout the body are the peripheral nervous system.
  3. What kind of protein speeds up the breakdown of food?
    Answer: an enzyme
    Saliva, the stomach and the pancreas all make enzymes.
  4. Explain the difference between mechanical and chemical digestion, with an example of each.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Mechanical: breaking into smaller pieces (chewing, churning); Chemical: enzymes or acid changing food into smaller molecules (saliva on starch, stomach acid on protein)
    Model answer: Mechanical digestion breaks food into smaller pieces, like chewing. Chemical digestion uses enzymes or acid to change food into new, smaller molecules, like saliva turning starch into sugar.

Answer key: printable page

  1. List the organs food passes through, in order, and one job of each.
    Answer: Mouth (chew, saliva starts starch), esophagus (peristalsis), stomach (acid, churning), small intestine (finish digestion, absorb), large intestine (absorb water).
  2. Compare mechanical and chemical digestion.
    Answer: Mechanical breaks food into smaller pieces; chemical uses enzymes and acid to make smaller molecules.
  3. Explain how villi help the small intestine do its job.
    Answer: They create a huge surface area with thin walls and capillaries, so nutrients are absorbed quickly.
  4. Name the three main parts of the brain and a job of each.
    Answer: Cerebrum (thinking, voluntary movement), cerebellum (balance, coordination), brain stem (breathing, heart rate).
  5. Describe the path of a reflex signal when you touch something sharp.
    Answer: Sensory neuron to spinal cord to motor neuron to muscle; the brain is told afterward.
  6. A ruler fell 25 cm in a reaction test. Use the chart to give the reaction time.
    Answer: About 0.23 seconds.
