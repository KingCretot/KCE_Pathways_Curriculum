Unit 4: Making the Case (Argument) · Week 19 · ELA · Teacher Plan

Writing an Argumentative Essay

Lesson length: 60 minutes live, plus the after-class interactive lesson

Objectives

  • Describe the structure of an argumentative essay: introduction, body paragraphs, counterclaim paragraph and conclusion.
  • Write an introduction with a hook, background and a clear claim (thesis).
  • Select relevant, credible evidence from sources and cite it in your own words.
  • Use transitions to connect ideas and paragraphs, and write a conclusion that does more than repeat.
  • Revise a draft using a checklist, and use the roots scrib/script and graph.

Standards

  • ELA.6.C.1.3: Write and support a claim using logical reasoning, relevant evidence from sources, elaboration, and a logical organizational structure with varied transitions.
  • ELA.6.C.3.1: Follow the rules of standard English grammar, punctuation, capitalization, and spelling appropriate to grade level.
  • ELA.6.C.4.1: Conduct research to answer a question, drawing on multiple reliable and valid sources, and refocusing the inquiry when appropriate.
  • ELA.6.C.1.5: Improve writing by planning, revising, and editing, considering feedback from adults and peers.
  • ELA.6.V.1.2: Apply knowledge of Greek and Latin roots and affixes to determine meanings of words and phrases in grade-level content.

Materials

  • The interactive lesson
  • A printed model essay, scissors, 4 highlighters, about 20 sticky notes
  • A wall or poster board
  • Paper and pencil or a computer
  • Printable practice page

Vocabulary

  • thesis: The sentence in the introduction that states the essay's claim.
  • hook: An opening that grabs the reader's attention: a question, story, surprising fact or vivid scene.
  • paraphrase: To restate a source's information in your own words.
  • cite: To name the source of information you use.
  • transition: A word or phrase that connects ideas: first, in addition, however, as a result.
  • call to action: A closing sentence that urges the reader to do something.
  • revise: To improve a draft's ideas, organization and wording.

Key questions to ask

  • What is each paragraph's job?
  • Is the thesis arguable, specific and clear?
  • Does the source really say that?
  • What will you revise first, ideas or commas?

Common mistakes to watch for

  • Revising means fixing spelling.
  • A conclusion should repeat the introduction word for word.
  • Any source that agrees with you is good evidence.
  • Paraphrasing means changing a few words.

Support (if the student is struggling)

  • Provide a five-box essay planner.
  • Allow the essay to be dictated, then typed or written by the student.
  • Give sentence starters for citations: "According to ..."

Challenge (if the student is ready for more)

  • Find and evaluate one additional credible source on the cursive debate.
  • Write a second counterclaim paragraph answering a different objection.

Background for the teacher

Florida's B.E.S.T. ELA standards include cursive benchmarks in grades 3 to 5 (for example, ELA.3.C.1.1, ELA.4.C.1.1, ELA.5.C.1.1). James and Engelhardt (2012), Trends in Neuroscience and Education, studied preschoolers' brain activation after printing, typing and tracing letters. Mueller and Oppenheimer (2014), Psychological Science; a 2021 replication by Urry and colleagues found smaller, nonsignificant differences. The model essay's reference is to the Corporation for National and Community Service (now AmeriCorps) report "Volunteering as a Pathway to Employment" (2013), which found that unemployed people who volunteered had 27 percent higher odds of finding a job; the senior-center anecdote is invented for the model.

Sources

  • Florida Department of Education, B.E.S.T. Standards for English Language Arts (2020).
  • James, K. H., and Engelhardt, L. (2012), The effects of handwriting experience on functional brain development in pre-literate children.
  • Mueller, P. A., and Oppenheimer, D. M. (2014), The pen is mightier than the keyboard; Urry, H. L., et al. (2021), replication.
  • National Archives, America's Founding Documents.

How the lesson runs

During the live lesson, the student shares their screen and answers the Reconnect questions and the Learn checks live while you teach. The Hands-On activity, Think Deeper and Exit Check are completed after class.

Reconnect

    Answer: concession
    Then you rebut.
  2. Paine compared America to a child who outgrows milk. What kind of comparison is that?
    Answer: an analogy
    Week 18.
  3. Which fallacy twists the other side's argument into a weaker version?
    Answer: a straw man
    Always state the counterclaim fairly.
  4. Write the three-part frame for a counterclaim paragraph using transition words.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Some people argue ... It is true that ... However ...
    Model answer: Some people argue that ... It is true that ... However, ...

Learn 1: Studying a Model Essay

  1. Which sentence-type ends paragraph 1 and states the essay's claim? Type the vocabulary word.
    Answer: the thesis
    "Homeschool families should make regular community service part of their school year ..."
  2. What kind of hook does the model essay use: a question, a surprising statistic or a short scene?
    Answer: a short scene (a story)
    Kids sorting cans at the food pantry.
  3. What are the two reasons in body paragraphs 2 and 3? What type of evidence supports each?
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Reason 1: service builds real-world skills, supported by a report (expert/organization); Reason 2: service connects homeschoolers to neighbors, supported by an example/anecdote
    Model answer: Reason 1 is that service builds real-world skills, supported by a report from the Corporation for National and Community Service. Reason 2 is that service connects homeschoolers to their community, supported by an anecdote about the senior center.
  4. How is the conclusion different from just repeating the introduction?
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Restates the claim in new words; Explains why it matters and adds a call to action
    Model answer: It restates the claim in new words ("turns students into active citizens"), explains why it matters, and ends with a call to action: choose a cause and start serving.

Learn 2: Introductions and Thesis Statements

  1. Rewrite this weak thesis so it is arguable, specific and clear: "Field trips are interesting."
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Takes a position someone could disagree with; Specific, with reasons previewed
    Model answer: Homeschool families should take at least one field trip a month, because hands-on visits make lessons memorable and connect students with experts.
  2. Use the roots.
    Answer: manuscript; autograph
    manu = hand; auto = self.

Learn 3: Using Sources Honestly

  1. Which source would best support a reason about reading historical documents? Type its letter.
    Answer: Source D
    The founding documents and old family records are in cursive.
  2. A student writes: "Science proves that cursive makes kids better readers (Source B)." Explain what is wrong with this, and rewrite the sentence honestly.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Source B studied printing letters by hand, not cursive, and in preschoolers; "proves" overstates it; An honest rewrite that matches what the study found
    Model answer: Source B studied preschoolers printing letters, not cursive, and one study doesn't "prove" anything. Honest version: "A 2012 Indiana University study found that when young children practiced writing letters by hand, their brains' reading areas were more active than when they typed (Source B), which suggests handwriting may support early reading."
  3. Paraphrase Source E in one sentence of your own words.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Keeps the meaning: people mostly type; time could go to other skills; printing is enough; Uses own words, not copied phrases
    Model answer: Because most adults write on screens today, some critics think class time would be better spent on typing or coding, since printing is fine for daily notes.

Learn 4: Transitions, Conclusions and Revision

  1. Write one transition word or phrase that signals a contrast.
    Answer: however (also: on the other hand, but, yet, in contrast, nevertheless)
    Contrast transitions turn the thinking; furthermore and in addition just add.
  2. Write your argumentative essay: Should students still learn cursive handwriting? Use at least two sources from the packet with honest citations, include a counterclaim paragraph, and follow the five-paragraph shape (at least 300 words).
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Introduction with hook, background and a clear thesis; Two body paragraphs, each with a reason, cited evidence and explanation; A fair counterclaim, concession and rebuttal; A conclusion that restates in new words and adds a call to action; Honest use of sources; transitions; edited for conventions
    Model answer: [1] Pull a letter from your great-grandmother out of an old shoebox, and you may find you can't read a word of it. Florida still requires students to learn cursive in grades 3 to 5 (Source A), but some people say the skill is outdated. Schools should keep teaching cursive, because it lets students read historical documents and because writing by hand may help students learn. [2] First, cursive opens the door to history. The original Declaration of Independence and Constitution at the National Archives are handwritten in cursive, and so are many family letters and diaries from before the 1990s (Source D). A student who can read cursive can study these documents directly instead of depending on someone else's typed copy. [3] In addition, writing by hand may support learning. A 2012 Indiana University study found that when preschoolers practiced printing letters by hand, the reading areas of their brains became more active than when they typed (Source B). That study was about printing, not cursive, but it suggests handwriting matters. A 2014 study also found that students who took notes by hand understood a lecture better, though later studies found smaller effects (Source C). Even with those limits, the research suggests that handwriting is worth keeping as one tool for learning. [4] Some people argue that adults now write mostly on keyboards, so class time should go to typing or coding (Source E). It is true that typing is an important skill. However, cursive takes only a small part of the school day in grades 3 to 5, and students can learn both. [5] Cursive is more than fancy handwriting: it is a key to our nation's history and our families' stories. Schools should keep teaching it, and families can start tonight by reading an old letter together.

Hands-On

  1. Which sentences were hardest to place in the essay puzzle, and what clues (transitions, topic) helped you? What feedback did your family member give on your outline, and what will you change?
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Names hard sentences and the clues used; Reports specific feedback; Describes a planned revision
    Model answer: The hardest sentence was "Even two hours a month can make a difference," but the word "However" earlier in the paragraph told me it belonged in the counterclaim paragraph. My mom said my second reason was weaker because it had no source, so I'll add Source C and explain its limits honestly.

Think Deeper

  1. Choose one paragraph from your draft. Copy it, then revise it using the checklist. Explain three specific changes you made and why each makes the paragraph stronger.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Original and revised paragraph included; Three specific changes (claim clarity, evidence, explanation, citation, transitions, word choice); Explains how each change strengthens the argument
    Model answer: Original: "Cursive is good for history. Old stuff is in cursive. So we should learn it." Revised: "First, cursive gives students access to history. The original Declaration of Independence and Constitution are handwritten in cursive (Source D), and so are many family letters from before the 1990s. Students who can read cursive can study these documents themselves instead of depending on someone else's typed version." Changes: (1) I added the transition "First." (2) I replaced "old stuff" with specific evidence and cited Source D. (3) I added an explanation of why reading original documents matters.
  2. Rank Sources A to E from most to least useful for YOUR essay, and explain your top choice and your bottom choice using relevance and credibility.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: A complete ranking; Top choice explained with relevance and credibility; Bottom choice explained, noting a limit
    Model answer: D, A, C, E, B. D is most useful because my strongest reason is reading historical documents, and the National Archives is very credible. B is least useful for me because it studied preschoolers printing letters, not cursive, so I'd have to be careful not to stretch it.

Exit Check

  1. What is the sentence that states an essay's claim called?
    Answer: the thesis
    It usually ends the introduction.
  2. Restating a source's information in your own words is called ___.
    Answer: paraphrasing
    Then cite the source.
  3. The root scrib/script means ___.
    Answer: write
    describe, manuscript.
  4. Why is it dishonest to make a source sound like it says more than it does?
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: It misleads the reader; the evidence doesn't really support the claim; Readers who check will lose trust in the writer
    Model answer: It tricks the reader into believing evidence that isn't really there, and anyone who checks the source will stop trusting the writer.

Answer key: printable page

  1. List the five parts of an argumentative essay and the job of each.
    Answer: Introduction (hook, background, thesis); body 1 and body 2 (reason, evidence, explanation); counterclaim (fair view, concession, rebuttal); conclusion (restate, why it matters, call to action).
  2. Write a strong thesis for: Should families eat dinner together most nights?
    Answer: Model: Families should eat dinner together at least five nights a week, because shared meals build conversation skills and healthier eating habits.
  3. Paraphrase and cite Source D.
    Answer: Model: The National Archives holds the original Declaration of Independence and Constitution, which are handwritten in cursive (Source D).
  4. Name four transitions and when to use each.
    Answer: First (begin a list), In addition (add a point), However (contrast), As a result (show effect).
  5. Write a conclusion for the cursive essay that does more than repeat the thesis.
    Answer: Model: Cursive is more than fancy handwriting; it is a key to our nation's history and our families' stories. Schools should keep teaching it, and families can start by reading an old letter together.
  6. What is plagiarism, and how do you avoid it?
    Answer: Using someone else's words or ideas without credit; paraphrase in your own words, quote exactly with quotation marks, and cite sources.
