Unit 4: The Mediterranean World · Week 19 · World and American History · Teacher Plan

Carthage and Roman Africa

Lesson length: 60 minutes live, plus the after-class interactive lesson

Objectives

  • Describe the founding of Carthage by Phoenician settlers and its growth into a North African trading power.
  • Explain the Phoenicians' contributions to Mediterranean trade and the alphabet.
  • Explain the causes and effects of the Punic Wars, including Hannibal's campaign.
  • Describe Roman Africa and identify African-born Romans who shaped the empire.
  • Evaluate Livy's account of Hannibal as a source written by an enemy, long after the events.

Standards

  • SS.6.W.3.1: Analyze the cultural impact the ancient Phoenicians had on the Mediterranean world with regard to colonization (Carthage), exploration, maritime commerce (purple dye, tin), and written communication (alphabet).
  • SS.6.W.3.9: Explain the impact of the Punic Wars on the development of the Roman Empire.
  • SS.6.W.3.12: Explain the causes for the growth and longevity of the Roman Empire.
  • SS.6.G.2.6: Explain the concept of cultural diffusion, and identify the influences of different ancient cultures on one another.
  • SS.6.W.1.3: Interpret primary and secondary sources.

Materials

  • The interactive lesson
  • Paper, pencil, a fine marker
  • A Phoenician alphabet chart
  • Optional: calcium carbonate chalk, a seashell or eggshell, white vinegar, water
  • A calculator for checking
  • Printable practice page

Vocabulary

  • Phoenicians: Seafaring traders from the eastern Mediterranean coast (modern Lebanon) who spread an early alphabet.
  • Punic: From the Roman word for Carthaginian; the Punic Wars were Rome's wars with Carthage.
  • Amazigh (Berber): The Indigenous peoples of North Africa, including the Numidians.
  • Numidia: An Amazigh kingdom west of Carthage, famous for its cavalry.
  • province: A territory outside Italy ruled by a Roman governor.
  • breadbasket: A region that produces large amounts of grain for others.
  • siege: Surrounding a city to cut off supplies and force it to surrender.

Key questions to ask

  • Who wrote this account, when and for whom?
  • How did geography shape the Punic Wars?
  • How did Africans shape the Roman Empire?
  • Why does it matter that no Carthaginian history survives?

Common mistakes to watch for

  • Carthage was a European or Middle Eastern city (it was an African city with Phoenician and Amazigh roots).
  • Hannibal was defeated in Italy.
  • The Roman Empire was only European.
  • Rome salted the earth at Carthage (a modern legend with no ancient source).

Support (if the student is struggling)

  • Provide a map of the Mediterranean to label.
  • Use a timeline strip for the three Punic Wars.
  • Read the Livy excerpt with the glossary, one sentence at a time.

Challenge (if the student is ready for more)

  • Research the archaeology of Carthage's circular harbor (UNESCO World Heritage Site).
  • Read about the Numidian king Jugurtha and compare him with Masinissa.

Background for the teacher

Carthage's traditional founding date of 814/813 BCE comes from Greek writers; archaeology confirms settlement by the late 9th or 8th century BCE. Hannibal set out in 218 BCE with about 37 elephants (Polybius and Appian); army size estimates vary widely. Cannae: August 216 BCE. Zama: 202 BCE. Carthage destroyed in 146 BCE; the story that Rome salted the earth is a modern invention. Appian (Punica 96) describes the circular naval harbor with sheds for 220 ships; archaeologists estimate roughly 170 to 220. Mago's agricultural treatise was translated into Latin by order of the Senate (Pliny, Natural History 18.22). Terence (about 195 to 159 BCE); the line "Homo sum, humani nihil a me alienum puto" is from Heauton Timorumenos (The Self-Tormentor); translation by KCE. Septimius Severus reigned 193 to 211 CE; the Constitutio Antoniniana (212 CE). Augustine's mother Monica is traditionally described as Amazigh (Berber). Grain estimates for imperial Rome vary (about 200,000 to 400,000 tons a year); ship capacities ranged widely, with large grain ships around 340 tons or more. The Livy excerpt follows Spillan and Edmonds's 1849 translation (Project Gutenberg).

Answer key: interactive lesson

Reconnect

  1. In the Roman Republic, what were the two elected leaders who served one-year terms called?
    Answer: consuls
    Each could veto the other.
  2. Which leader became Rome's first emperor in 27 BCE?
    Answer: Augustus
    He defeated Antony and Cleopatra VII of Egypt first.
  3. Last week's map marked a North African city across the sea from Sicily as Rome's rival. What was it?
    Answer: Carthage
    Today's lesson.
  4. In the Republic, a dictator held power for at most six months. Why might a long war against a powerful enemy put the Republic's limits on power under strain?
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Long wars need steady leadership and big armies; Generals and leaders may stay in power longer or armies become loyal to generals
    Model answer: A long war needs the same leaders and armies for years, so generals can build armies loyal to them and stay powerful longer than the rules allow.

Learn 1: Carthage: An African Trading Power

  1. From which Phoenician city did the founders of Carthage come?
    Answer: Tyre
    On the coast of modern Lebanon.
  2. Earlier writing systems, like Egyptian hieroglyphs and Mesopotamian cuneiform, used hundreds of signs. Why was an alphabet of about 22 letters such a powerful tool for traders?
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Few signs, each for a sound, so it was quick to learn; More people (merchants, not just trained scribes) could read and write records, contracts and letters
    Model answer: With only about 22 letters, each standing for a sound, people could learn to read and write much faster than with hundreds of signs. Merchants could keep their own records and write contracts without hiring trained scribes.
  3. Look at the harbor drawing. Why would hiding the naval harbor behind the merchant harbor be a smart design?
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Enemies and visitors couldn't see the size of the war fleet; It protected the warships and kept trade separate from the navy
    Model answer: Visitors and enemy spies sailing into the merchant harbor couldn't see how many warships Carthage had, and the fleet was protected deep inside the city.

Learn 2: The Punic Wars

  1. Put these events in order.
    Answer: Carthage is founded → First Punic War begins → Hannibal crosses the Alps → Battle of Zama → Rome destroys Carthage
    814 (traditional), 264, 218, 202 and 146 BCE.
  2. Which Numidian king helped Rome defeat Hannibal at Zama?
    Answer: Masinissa
    His cavalry was decisive.
  3. Explain two effects of the Punic Wars on Rome.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Two valid effects (control of the Mediterranean; new provinces in Spain and Africa; great wealth and enslaved captives; a growing gap between rich and poor that strained the Republic)
    Model answer: Rome became the strongest power in the Mediterranean and took over Spain and North Africa. The flood of wealth and enslaved people also made rich landowners richer while small farmers lost out, which helped weaken the Republic.

Learn 3: Livy on Hannibal: Reading an Enemy's Account

  1. Give two reasons a historian should read Livy's account of Hannibal carefully.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Livy was Roman, Carthage's enemy; He wrote nearly 200 years later; No Carthaginian side survives to compare
    Model answer: Livy was a Roman writing about Rome's greatest enemy, so he might exaggerate or shape the story for Roman readers. He also wrote almost 200 years later, and no Carthaginian account survives to compare it with.
  2. Design a simple, safe test you could do at home to see whether vinegar affects limestone or chalk. What would you observe, and what wouldn't your test prove about Hannibal?
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: A safe test (a piece of chalk or limestone in vinegar vs. in water); Expected observation (bubbles, crumbling of chalk); Limit: it doesn't show an army could carry enough vinegar or that Alpine rock is limestone; fire was also used
    Model answer: Put a piece of calcium carbonate chalk (or a seashell or clean eggshell) in a cup of vinegar and another in water. The one in vinegar should fizz and slowly crumble. But that doesn't prove Hannibal's army did it: the Alpine rock might not be limestone, the army probably couldn't carry enough vinegar, and the fire did much of the work.

Learn 4: Roman Africa

  1. Which emperor, born in Leptis Magna in North Africa, began ruling in 193 CE?
    Answer: Septimius Severus
    Founder of the Severan dynasty.
  2. The Roman province around Carthage gave its name to an entire continent. What name?
    Answer: Africa
    The province of Africa.
  3. Terence was born in Carthage, enslaved, freed and became a famous writer in Rome. His character says, "I am human; I think nothing human is foreign to me." What does this mean, and why might it be especially powerful coming from Terence?
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Meaning: all human experiences concern me; we share a common humanity; Powerful because Terence was an outsider, African-born and once enslaved, yet speaks for all humanity
    Model answer: It means that anything that happens to any person matters to me, because we're all human. It's powerful from Terence because he was an outsider in Rome, born in Africa and once enslaved, yet he became a voice for everyone's shared humanity.

Hands-On

  1. Paste or describe your Phoenician message and its translation. What was hardest about reading a writing system with no vowels, and how did context help?
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: A message and translation; A thoughtful reflection about missing vowels and using context
    Model answer: I wrote "MT M T TH PRK" for "Meet me at the park," right to left. Without vowels, "PRK" could be park or pork, but my brother guessed park because "meet me at" made sense. Context does the work the vowels would do.
  2. Show your grain fleet calculations. What does the number of ships tell you about how important Africa was to Rome?
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: 300,000 ÷ 350 ≈ 857 shiploads; 857 ÷ 180 ≈ 4.8, about 5 ships a day; Interpretation: Rome depended heavily on Africa; losing that grain would mean hunger
    Model answer: 300,000 ÷ 350 ≈ 857 shiploads. Over 180 days, 857 ÷ 180 ≈ 4.8, about 5 ships every day. Rome depended on Africa so much that if the grain ships stopped, the city would go hungry, which gave North Africa real power in the empire.

Think Deeper

  1. Hannibal stayed in Italy for about 15 years and won great battles there but still lost the war. Using evidence, explain why. Consider geography, allies and Rome's government.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Geography: far from home, hard to resupply across the sea and Alps; Allies: most Italian allies stayed loyal to Rome; Numidia's Masinissa helped Rome; Rome's resources and refusal to surrender; Scipio attacked Africa; Uses evidence from the lesson
    Model answer: Hannibal was far from home, and getting soldiers and supplies across the sea or over the Alps was very hard. Most of Rome's Italian allies stayed loyal, so Rome could keep raising new armies even after losing at Cannae. Then Scipio attacked Carthage itself, forcing Hannibal home, and the Numidian king Masinissa's cavalry helped Rome win at Zama. Winning battles wasn't enough without supplies, allies and a home base.
  2. Some people imagine the Roman Empire as a European empire. Using at least three pieces of evidence from this lesson, argue that Africa was central to the Roman world.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: A clear claim; Three pieces of evidence (Carthage as Rome's great rival; Africa and Egypt as breadbasket; Roman Carthage one of the largest cities; Septimius Severus; Terence; Augustine; Caracalla's citizenship law; the name Africa); Explains how each shows Africa's importance
    Model answer: Africa was central to the Roman world. First, the African city of Carthage was Rome's greatest rival, and Hannibal nearly conquered Italy. Second, after Rome won, North Africa, including Egypt, became the breadbasket that fed the city of Rome, with roughly five grain ships arriving a day during the sailing season. Third, Africans led and shaped the empire: Septimius Severus, born in Libya, became emperor; Terence, born in Carthage, became a great playwright; and Augustine of Hippo became one of history's most important thinkers. Even the continent's name comes from the Roman province of Africa.

Exit Check

  1. What were the three wars between Rome and Carthage called?
    Answer: the Punic Wars
    From the Roman word for Carthaginian.
  2. Which Carthaginian general crossed the Alps with elephants in 218 BCE?
    Answer: Hannibal
    He won at Cannae in 216 BCE.
  3. Which seafaring people spread an early alphabet across the Mediterranean?
    Answer: the Phoenicians
    Carthage was founded by Phoenician settlers.
  4. Why was North Africa called Rome's breadbasket, and why did that make it important?
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: It supplied much of the grain that fed Rome; Rome depended on it; losing it would cause hunger
    Model answer: North Africa, including Egypt, grew much of the grain that fed the city of Rome, so Rome depended on Africa to avoid hunger.

Answer key: printable page

  1. Who founded Carthage, and where was it?
    Answer: Phoenician settlers from Tyre; on the coast of North Africa in modern Tunisia.
  2. Why was the Phoenician alphabet important?
    Answer: About 22 letters for sounds, easy to learn; adapted by Greeks and Romans; ancestor of our alphabet.
  3. Summarize the cause and result of each Punic War.
    Answer: First: fight over Sicily, Rome won Sicily. Second: Hannibal invaded Italy, Rome won at Zama with Masinissa's help. Third: Rome besieged and destroyed Carthage in 146 BCE.
  4. Why should historians be careful with Livy's account of Hannibal?
    Answer: He was Roman, wrote about 200 years later, and no Carthaginian account survives.
  5. Name three North Africans who shaped the Roman world and what each did.
    Answer: Terence (playwright), Septimius Severus (emperor), Augustine of Hippo (Christian thinker); also Masinissa (ally at Zama). Septimius Severus's son Caracalla extended citizenship.
  6. Explain why North Africa was called Rome's breadbasket.
    Answer: North Africa, including Egypt, supplied much of the grain that fed the city of Rome.
