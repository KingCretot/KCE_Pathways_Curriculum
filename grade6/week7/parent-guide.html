Grade 6 · Week 7 · Parent Guide

Week 7: Kemet in Africa, Cells Up Close, Dividing Fractions and Text Structures

This week your child weighs the evidence that ancient Egypt (Kemet) was an African civilization and learns how some writers tried to separate Egypt from Africa, explores the parts of an animal cell, divides fractions to figure out servings and cost per serving, and learns how authors organize information.

How scoring works: each lesson scores itself. Your child gets two tries per question: full points on the first try, half points on the second, then the answer and explanation appear. A score of 85 or higher means the lesson is mastered. Written answers go to Jonathan, who scores them with a rubric.

Time this week: About 60 minutes of live lessons per subject, plus 30 to 45 minutes of after-class work per subject.

Gather this week: 12 or more sticky notes or index cards, tape, markers in two colors, a wall, door or poster board, and a map of Africa (Social Studies); index cards, tape, a marker, a small toy or ball and optional yarn (Science); three pantry packages with nutrition labels and their prices, measuring cups, and paper and pencil (Math); five sheets of paper, index cards, scissors, tape, markers and a recipe, manual or news article from home (ELA).

Heads up: Social Studies this week discusses racism in how history was written, and Science tells the story of Henrietta Lacks, whose cells were taken without her permission in 1951. Both are handled with care and focus on evidence and fairness. Your child may want to talk about them with you.

ELA: Text Structures

Your child learns five ways authors organize information: sequence, cause and effect, compare and contrast, problem and solution, and description. They read short original passages about Cheikh Anta Diop, Florida's springs, Kush and solar lamps, then rewrite facts in a new structure.

Ask at dinner: "Is this recipe (or news story) organized as steps, causes and effects, or a problem and solution? How can you tell?"

Try it together: Pick a text at home and hunt for signal words like because, however, first and as a result.

Signs it's clicking: They name the structure and point to signal words that prove it. Signs they need help: They pick a structure from one word without reading the whole passage.

Math: Dividing Fractions and Mixed Numbers

Your child learns what dividing by a fraction means (how many of these fit in that?), divides fractions and mixed numbers using reciprocals, and finds the number of servings in a package and the cost per serving.

Ask at dinner: "How many servings are really in this bag for our family, and what does each one cost?"

Try it together: Do the pantry servings investigation together with real packages and prices.

Signs it's clicking: They flip the divisor, not the first number, and check by multiplying. Signs they need help: They expect division to always give a smaller number. Ask: how many quarters are in 5?

Life Science: Inside an Animal Cell

Your child learns the jobs of the cell membrane, nucleus, ribosomes, ER, Golgi, mitochondria, lysosomes and vacuoles, traces how a cell makes and ships a protein, and learns about Henrietta Lacks and why consent matters in science.

Ask at dinner: "If your body were a city, what would the mitochondria be, and why?"

Try it together: Be the visitor on your child's walk-through of the cell around your home.

Signs it's clicking: They explain how organelles depend on each other, not just what each one is called. Signs they need help: They memorize names without jobs. Ask, what would go wrong if this part stopped working?

World and American History: Kemet as an African Civilization

Your child examines six kinds of evidence (geography, archaeology, culture, language, written sources and biology) that ancient Egypt was an African civilization, learns how writers in the 1800s separated Egypt from Africa for racist reasons, and meets scholars Cheikh Anta Diop and Théophile Obenga. They also learn what a single ancient genome can and cannot prove.

Ask at dinner: "Which piece of evidence convinced you most that Kemet was African? Why?"

Try it together: Listen to your child present their Evidence Wall and ask one challenging question.

Signs it's clicking: They back up the claim with several kinds of evidence and can explain the limits of each. Signs they need help: They rely on one piece of evidence, like art colors. Ask what other kinds of evidence agree.
