Unit 2: Cells, the Building Blocks of Life · Week 7 · Life Science · Teacher Plan

Inside an Animal Cell

Lesson length: 60 minutes live, plus the after-class interactive lesson

Objectives

  • Identify the main parts of an animal cell and describe the job of each.
  • Explain how organelles work together, such as in making and shipping a protein.
  • Compare a cell to a system you know (a city, school or factory) and explain the limits of the comparison.
  • Explain how a cell's shape relates to its job.
  • Describe the story of Henrietta Lacks and the ethics of using human cells in research.

Standards

  • SC.6.L.14.4: Compare and contrast the structure and function of major organelles of plant and animal cells, including cell wall, cell membrane, nucleus, cytoplasm, chloroplasts, mitochondria, and vacuoles.
  • SC.6.L.14.3: Recognize and explore how cells of all organisms undergo similar processes to maintain homeostasis, including extracting energy from food, getting rid of waste, and reproducing.
  • SC.6.N.3.4: Identify the role of models in the context of the sixth grade science benchmarks.
  • SC.6.N.2.3: Recognize that scientists who make contributions to scientific knowledge come from all kinds of backgrounds and possess varied talents, interests, and goals.
  • NGSS MS-LS1-2: Develop and use a model to describe the function of a cell as a whole and ways the parts of cells contribute to the function.

Materials

  • The interactive lesson
  • Index cards, tape and a marker
  • A small toy or ball
  • Printable practice page

Vocabulary

  • organelle: A tiny structure inside a cell that does a specific job.
  • cell membrane: The thin, flexible outer layer that controls what enters and leaves the cell.
  • cytoplasm: The jelly-like fluid that fills the cell and holds the organelles.
  • nucleus: The control center of the cell, which holds the DNA.
  • DNA: The molecule that carries the instructions for building and running a living thing.
  • mitochondria: Organelles that release energy from food for the cell to use (singular: mitochondrion).
  • ribosome: A tiny organelle that builds proteins.
  • protein: A large molecule that does much of the work in cells, such as building structures and speeding up chemical reactions.

Key questions to ask

  • What job does this organelle do, and what depends on it?
  • How do organelles work together?
  • Where does this analogy break down?
  • Why does consent matter in science?

Common mistakes to watch for

  • Thinking the cell membrane is a rigid wall (that is the plant cell wall, next week).
  • Believing mitochondria make energy from nothing (they release energy stored in food).
  • Thinking all cells look alike.
  • Treating an analogy as an exact description.

Support (if the student is struggling)

  • Provide a labeled diagram with a job card for each organelle.
  • Use color coding: one color per organelle on the diagram and the walk-through signs.
  • Focus first on five organelles: membrane, nucleus, cytoplasm, ribosome, mitochondria.

Challenge (if the student is ready for more)

  • Research why mitochondria have their own DNA and what that suggests about their origin.
  • Read about the 2023 settlement in the Henrietta Lacks case and summarize what changed.

Background for the teacher

Henrietta Lacks (1920 to 1951) was treated at Johns Hopkins in 1951; cells from her tumor were taken without her knowledge, as was common practice then, and became the first immortalized human cell line (HeLa). HeLa cells were used in testing the Salk polio vaccine and in countless research projects. In 2013 the National Institutes of Health reached an agreement with the Lacks family about access to her genome data, and in 2023 the family settled a lawsuit with Thermo Fisher Scientific. Handle the topic with respect; the focus is consent and fairness. Mature mammalian red blood cells lack a nucleus.

Answer key: interactive lesson

Reconnect

  1. State the three parts of the cell theory.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Living things are made of cells; The cell is the basic unit of life; Cells come from existing cells
    Model answer: All living things are made of cells, the cell is the basic unit of life, and all cells come from other cells.
  2. What is the total magnification with a 10× eyepiece and a 40× objective?
    Answer: 400 ×
    10 × 40 = 400×.
  3. A cell looks 8 mm wide at 400×. How wide is it really, in millimeters?
    Answer: 0.02 mm
    8 ÷ 400 = 0.02 mm.
  4. Which scientist showed the importance of the cell's outer surface in fertilization?
    Answer: Ernest Everett Just
    Ernest Everett Just studied the cell surface of marine eggs at Woods Hole.

Learn 1: The Boundary and the Control Center

  1. Explain why a cell needs a membrane that lets some things in but not others.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Needs to take in useful things (water, oxygen, food); Needs to keep out harmful things and keep its contents in
    Model answer: The cell needs to take in useful things like water, oxygen and food, but it must keep harmful things out and keep its own important parts from leaking away.
  2. Which organelle holds the cell's DNA?
    Answer: nucleus
    The nucleus is the control center that holds DNA.
  3. Why do scientists call the nucleus the control center of the cell? What would happen to a cell that lost its nucleus?
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: It holds DNA, the instructions for everything the cell does; Without it the cell could not make new proteins or divide, and would eventually die
    Model answer: The nucleus holds the DNA, which has the instructions for everything the cell does. A cell that lost its nucleus could not make new proteins the way it normally does or divide to make new cells, so it would eventually stop working.

Learn 2: The Workers: Making and Shipping Proteins

  1. Put these in the order a protein travels and describe each step: Golgi apparatus, ribosome, cell membrane, endoplasmic reticulum.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Ribosome builds it; ER carries it; Golgi packages it; Membrane ships it out
    Model answer: First the ribosome builds the protein, then the endoplasmic reticulum carries it through the cell, then the Golgi apparatus packages and labels it, and finally it leaves through the cell membrane.
  2. Which organelle releases energy from food for the cell?
    Answer: mitochondria
    Mitochondria release energy from food.
  3. Heart muscle cells contain far more mitochondria than skin cells. Explain why, using what you know about each cell's job.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Heart muscle works constantly and needs lots of energy; Mitochondria release energy, so more mitochondria supply more energy
    Model answer: Heart muscle cells contract every second of your life, so they need a huge amount of energy. Mitochondria release energy from food, so having more of them gives heart cells the energy they need. Skin cells do less work, so they need fewer.

Learn 3: Models, Analogies and Their Limits

  1. Create your own analogy comparing a cell to a school. Match at least four organelles to parts of a school, and explain one way your analogy does not fit.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Four sensible matches with reasons; Names a real limit of the analogy
    Model answer: The nucleus is the principal's office with the plans, ribosomes are the classrooms where things are made, the Golgi is the front office that sends things out, and the cell membrane is the front door with a guard. One way it does not fit: a school cannot split into two identical schools, but a cell can divide.
  2. Which kind of cell has long branches to carry signals through the body?
    Answer: nerve cell (neuron)
    Nerve cells have long branches to carry signals.
  3. Mature human red blood cells have no nucleus. Use the idea that shape fits the job to explain why that might help them.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Without a nucleus there is more room for hemoglobin to carry oxygen; Allows a flexible disk shape to squeeze through narrow vessels
    Model answer: Without a nucleus, a red blood cell has more room to carry oxygen, and it can be a flexible, flat disk that bends to squeeze through the narrowest blood vessels.

Learn 4: Spotlight: Henrietta Lacks and HeLa Cells

  1. Henrietta Lacks's cells led to life-saving discoveries, but they were taken without her permission. Why does consent matter in science, even when the research helps many people?
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: People have the right to decide what happens to their own bodies and information; Respect, trust and fairness (including sharing benefits); Recognizes both the benefits and the wrong done
    Model answer: Consent matters because every person has the right to decide what happens to their own body and cells. Even though HeLa cells helped create vaccines and treatments, taking them without asking was wrong. When scientists ask permission and treat people fairly, people can trust science, and families are not left out of the benefits.

Hands-On

  1. List each organelle, the home location you chose, its job and why the location fits.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: All eight organelles; Correct jobs; Sensible reasons for locations
    Model answer: Nucleus: my desk with the family calendar, because it holds the plans. Ribosome: the kitchen counter, where things are made. ER: the hallway, the path things travel. Golgi: the mail basket, where things are packaged to send. Mitochondria: the pantry, where the energy (food) is. Lysosome: the trash and recycling bin. Vacuole: the hall closet for storage. Cell membrane: the front door, which decides what comes in and out.
  2. What question did your visitor ask, and how did you answer it? What part of your model does not match a real cell?
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Reports a question and an accurate answer; Names a real limit of the model
    Model answer: My sister asked why the protein had to go to the Golgi. I explained the Golgi packages and labels proteins so they reach the right place. One limit: in a real cell all of this happens at the same time, millions of times, in a space far too small to see.

Think Deeper

  1. Choose two organelles. For each, predict what would happen to a cell if that organelle stopped working, and explain why. Then explain why scientists say a cell is a system.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Two accurate predictions with reasons; Explains that parts depend on each other (system)
    Model answer: If the mitochondria stopped working, the cell would run out of energy, and every other job would slow down and stop. If the lysosomes stopped working, waste and worn-out parts would pile up and damage the cell. A cell is a system because its parts depend on one another: the ribosomes need energy from mitochondria, and the Golgi needs proteins from the ER, so a problem in one part affects the whole cell.
  2. Today, many hospitals ask patients for permission before using leftover tissue in research. Write two rules you think scientists should follow when using human cells, and explain why each rule is fair.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Two clear rules (ask consent, explain how cells will be used, protect privacy, share benefits, let people say no); Explains fairness for each
    Model answer: Rule 1: Always ask permission and explain how the cells will be used, because people have the right to decide about their own bodies. Rule 2: Protect people's names and medical information, because their families deserve privacy, which Henrietta Lacks's family did not get for many years.

Exit Check

  1. Which organelle builds proteins?
    Answer: ribosome
    Ribosomes build proteins.
  2. Which part controls what enters and leaves the cell?
    Answer: cell membrane
    The cell membrane is selectively permeable.
  3. Explain how the ER and the Golgi apparatus work together.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: ER carries proteins; Golgi packages and ships them
    Model answer: The ER carries newly built proteins through the cell to the Golgi, which packages and labels them and sends them where they are needed.
  4. Give one example of how a cell's shape fits its job.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Accurate example (red blood cell disk, nerve cell branches, muscle cell length)
    Model answer: Nerve cells have long branches so they can carry signals over long distances in the body.
  5. What is the name of the cell line that came from Henrietta Lacks?
    Answer: HeLa
    HeLa, from the first two letters of her first and last names.

Answer key: printable page

  1. Match each organelle to its job: nucleus, mitochondria, ribosome, Golgi apparatus, lysosome, cell membrane.
    Answer: Nucleus: holds DNA, control center; mitochondria: release energy; ribosome: builds proteins; Golgi: packages and ships; lysosome: breaks down waste; cell membrane: controls what enters and leaves.
  2. Describe the path of a protein from instructions to leaving the cell.
    Answer: Nucleus (DNA instructions) → ribosome (builds) → ER (carries) → Golgi (packages) → cell membrane (ships out).
  3. Draw a simple animal cell and label five parts.
    Answer: Drawing with membrane, cytoplasm, nucleus, mitochondria and ribosomes labeled.
  4. Why do muscle cells have many mitochondria?
    Answer: Muscles work hard and need lots of energy; mitochondria release energy from food.
  5. Write one cell analogy and one way it does not fit.
    Answer: For example: the nucleus is like a principal's office; but a school cannot divide into two schools.
  6. Why is the story of Henrietta Lacks important for science today?
    Answer: Her cells were taken without permission; it led to rules about consent, privacy and fairness in research.
