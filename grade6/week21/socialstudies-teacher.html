Unit 5: Empires and Global Trade · Week 21 · World and American History · Teacher Plan

Ghana and the Trans-Saharan Trade

Lesson length: 60 minutes live, plus the after-class interactive lesson

Objectives

  • Describe the geography of West Africa (Sahara, Sahel, savanna) and how it shaped trade.
  • Explain how the trans-Saharan trade in gold and salt worked, including the role of the camel and Amazigh traders.
  • Explain how the kings of Ghana gained power and wealth by taxing and controlling trade.
  • Analyze an outside account of Ghana by the geographer al-Bakri.
  • Explain causes of Ghana's decline.

Standards

  • SS.6.G.2.1: Explain how major physical characteristics, natural resources, climate, and absolute and relative locations have influenced settlement, interactions, and the economies of ancient civilizations of the world.
  • SS.6.G.2.6: Explain the concept of cultural diffusion, and identify the influences of different ancient cultures on one another.
  • SS.6.E.3.4: Describe the relationship among civilizations that engage in trade, including the benefits and drawbacks of voluntary trade.
  • SS.6.W.1.5: Describe the roles of historians and recognize varying historical interpretations (historiography).

Materials

  • The interactive lesson
  • About 40 white beans or paper squares and 40 yellow beads or pennies
  • A bowl, paper and pencil
  • Printable practice page

Vocabulary

  • Sahara: The largest hot desert on Earth, covering most of northern Africa.
  • Sahel: The dry grassland strip on the southern edge of the Sahara.
  • savanna: Tropical grassland with scattered trees, south of the Sahel.
  • Soninke: The West African people who founded the kingdom of Ghana, which they called Wagadu.
  • silent barter: Trading goods without speaking, by leaving goods and waiting for an offer in return.
  • tribute: Payments demanded by a ruler from people under his control.

Key questions to ask

  • What did each region have, and what did it need?
  • How did Ghana profit without owning the mines?
  • Who wrote this account, and how did they know?
  • What made the trade possible, and what did it cost people?

Common mistakes to watch for

  • Medieval Ghana was in the same place as the modern country of Ghana.
  • Africa had no great kingdoms before Europeans arrived.
  • The trade carried only gold and salt.
  • The Sahara is only sand.

Support (if the student is struggling)

Challenge (if the student is ready for more)

  • Research the archaeology of Koumbi Saleh.
  • Compare the Soninke legend of Wagadu and the serpent Bida with the historical causes of Ghana's decline.

Background for the teacher

Medieval West African kingdoms fall outside Florida's Grade 6 NGSSS world history range (which ends around 500 CE); KCE's scope teaches them in Unit 5, and the standards above cover the geography, trade and source skills. Dates: Ghana's origins are uncertain (often given as about 300 to 400 CE); al-Bakri completed his Book of Routes and Realms in 1068 in Córdoba, using traders' reports. The 200,000-soldier figure comes from al-Bakri and is likely exaggerated. The Almoravid conquest of Ghana around 1076 is a long-standing view now questioned by historians such as Conrad and Fisher (1982). The idea that salt traded weight for weight with gold is reported in some accounts but was not a fixed rate. Silent barter is described by Herodotus (about Carthaginians on Africa's Atlantic coast) and by later Arabic writers; historians debate details. Al-Bakri's account is summarized in KCE's own words; published English translations are under copyright.

Answer key: interactive lesson

Reconnect

  1. Name one Roman province that Rome called its breadbasket.
    Answer: North Africa, including Egypt (the province of Africa)
    Week 19.
  2. What is the name for the Indigenous peoples of North Africa, including the Numidians?
    Answer: Amazigh (Berber)
    Amazigh traders crossed the Sahara with camel caravans.
  3. The Persian Royal Road used relay stations every day's journey. What did a road like that help a ruler control?
    Answer: a large empire (messages, armies, trade)
    Ghana's kings controlled trade routes instead.
  4. In Unit 4, Carthage and Rome fought over control of Mediterranean trade. Why would controlling a trade route make a kingdom rich and powerful?
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Rulers can tax goods that pass through; Wealth pays for armies, officials and building
    Model answer: A kingdom that controls a trade route can tax every load of goods that passes through, and that money pays for armies, officials and cities.

Learn 1: West Africa's Geography

  1. Name the region.
    Answer: Sahara; savanna (and forest); Sahel
    Each region had something the others needed.
  2. Why was the Sahel a good place for a trading kingdom to grow? Use the map.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: It lies between the desert salt and the southern gold, where caravans meet; Has water (Niger, Senegal Rivers) and farmland/grazing to feed people and animals
    Model answer: The Sahel sits right between the salt of the Sahara and the gold of the south, so caravans and traders met there. It also has rivers like the Niger and Senegal and grassland for farming and herding, so a city there could feed its people and the traders' camels.

Learn 2: Camels, Caravans and the Gold-Salt Trade

  1. Which animal made regular crossings of the Sahara possible?
    Answer: the camel
    Widely used by about 300 CE.
  2. A caravan has 1,200 camels, each carrying about 300 pounds of salt. About how many pounds of salt does the caravan carry?
    Answer: 360000 pounds
    1,200 × 300 = 360,000 pounds, about 180 tons.
  3. Why might the gold miners have used silent barter instead of trading face to face? Give two possible reasons.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: To keep the location of the gold mines secret; Language differences, safety or to avoid conflict
    Model answer: The miners may have wanted to keep the location of their gold fields secret so no one could take them over. Silent trading also worked even when the two sides didn't speak the same language, and it avoided fights.

Learn 3: The Kingdom of Ghana

  1. What did the Soninke people call their kingdom?
    Answer: Wagadu
    Ghana was the king's title.
  2. Why did the king of Ghana keep all the gold nuggets for himself? Explain the economic reasoning.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Controlling the supply kept gold scarce; Scarcity kept its value (price) high, protecting the kingdom's wealth
    Model answer: If everyone could sell big nuggets, there would be so much gold that its value would drop. By keeping the nuggets and letting traders sell only gold dust, the king controlled the supply, kept gold scarce and kept its price high, which protected his kingdom's wealth.
  3. A trader brings in 40 loads of salt. The tax is 1 dinar of gold per load coming in. He also takes 40 loads of salt out of Ghana to sell farther south, taxed 2 dinars per load. How many dinars does he pay in all?
    Answer: 120 dinars
    40 × 1 + 40 × 2 = 40 + 80 = 120 dinars.

Learn 4: Al-Bakri's Account and Ghana's Decline

  1. Al-Bakri never visited Ghana. Explain one reason his account is valuable and one reason a historian should be careful with it.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Valuable: detailed, written close to the time (1068), from traders who saw it; Careful: secondhand reports, possible exaggeration, outsider view; check with archaeology at Koumbi Saleh and oral traditions
    Model answer: His account is valuable because it was written in 1068, while Ghana was powerful, and it is based on reports from traders who had been there. But he heard the details secondhand, so some may be exaggerated, and he was an outsider. Historians can check it against archaeology at Koumbi Saleh and Soninke oral traditions.
  2. Name two causes of Ghana's decline and explain how one of them weakened the kingdom's power.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Two causes (Almoravid conflict, drought/overgrazing, new gold fields shifting trade, rebellions); Explains how one weakened Ghana (less trade means less tax money; drought means less food)
    Model answer: Drought and the discovery of new gold fields farther east both hurt Ghana. When trade routes shifted east to the new gold, fewer caravans passed through Ghana, so its kings collected less tax money to pay soldiers and officials, and the kingdom grew weaker.

Hands-On

  1. Record the exchange rates in the silent barter round and the Ghana round, and how much the king collected. Who ended up with the most power, and why?
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Exchange rates recorded; King's collection recorded; Explains that controlling trade (taxes) gave the king wealth and power without mining or producing anything
    Model answer: Silent barter: 10 salt for 6 gold after four rounds of offers. Ghana round: the king collected 7 gold in taxes. The king ended up with a lot of wealth even though he didn't mine gold or dig salt, because every trade had to pass through his land. Controlling the trade gave him the most power.

Think Deeper

  1. Compare how Ghana and Rome (Week 18) grew wealthy. How were their sources of wealth alike and different?
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Alike: both controlled trade routes and collected taxes or tribute; Different: Rome conquered and ruled many provinces and seized wealth; Ghana mainly taxed trade between salt and gold producers it did not own; Uses specific evidence from both
    Model answer: Both Ghana and Rome grew rich by controlling trade and collecting taxes and tribute. Rome conquered lands around the Mediterranean, took wealth and enslaved people as spoils, and collected grain and taxes from provinces like Africa. Ghana, by contrast, didn't own the salt mines or the gold fields; it grew rich mostly by taxing the trade that passed between them and controlling the supply of gold nuggets. Rome's wealth came largely from conquest; Ghana's came largely from being the middleman.
  2. The trans-Saharan trade also carried enslaved people north. Why is it important to include this fact when telling the story of Ghana's wealth, and how does it change your view of the trade?
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Telling the whole truth: trade brought wealth but also caused great human suffering; A thoughtful reflection on how prosperity and injustice can exist together
    Model answer: Leaving it out would make the trade seem only like an exchange of goods, when real people were captured and sold. Including it tells the whole truth: Ghana's trade brought great wealth and connected Africa to the wider world, but it also caused terrible suffering. It reminds me that a society can be impressive and still do great wrong.

Exit Check

  1. What two main goods were traded across the Sahara: gold and what?
    Answer: salt
    Salt went south; gold went north.
  2. What was the capital of the kingdom of Ghana?
    Answer: Koumbi Saleh
    Two towns: the king's and the merchants'.
  3. Which West African kingdom replaced Ghana by about 1240?
    Answer: Mali
    You will study Mali next week.
  4. How did the kings of Ghana become rich without owning the gold or salt mines?
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: They taxed trade passing through their land and controlled the gold supply (nuggets)
    Model answer: They taxed every load of salt and gold that passed through Ghana and kept the gold nuggets for themselves.

Answer key: printable page

  1. Describe the Sahara, the Sahel and the savanna and what each provided in the trade.
    Answer: Sahara: desert with salt mines; Sahel: grassland where caravans met and Ghana grew; savanna: grasslands near gold fields.
  2. Why was the camel important to the trans-Saharan trade?
    Answer: It could carry heavy loads, go days without water and walk on sand, making regular crossings possible.
  3. Explain how Ghana's kings grew rich.
    Answer: They taxed salt coming in and goods going out, and kept all gold nuggets to keep gold scarce.
  4. What did al-Bakri describe, and why must historians read him carefully?
    Answer: The king's golden court, drum, Muslim officials; he relied on traders' reports and never visited.
  5. Name three causes of Ghana's decline.
    Answer: Conflict with the Almoravids, drought and overgrazing, new gold fields shifting trade, rebellions.
  6. Where was medieval Ghana compared with the modern country of Ghana?
    Answer: Northwest of it, in parts of today's Mauritania and Mali.
