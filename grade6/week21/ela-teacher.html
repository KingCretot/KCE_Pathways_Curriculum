Unit 5: Novel Study · Week 21 · ELA · Teacher Plan

Launching the Novel: Setting and Context

Lesson length: 60 minutes live, plus the after-class interactive lesson

Objectives

  • Explain how setting (time, place and social conditions) shapes a story's characters and conflicts.
  • Describe the historical context of the Klondike Gold Rush, including the Indigenous people who made the first strike.
  • Analyze how the opening chapter establishes Buck's character through setting and contrast.
  • Identify point of view and how the narrator gives us Buck's perspective.
  • Use context clues and the roots prim and civ to understand challenging words.

Standards

  • ELA.6.R.1.1: Explain how the setting impacts conflict and characterization in a literary text.
  • ELA.6.R.1.2: Analyze the development of stated or implied theme(s) throughout a literary text.
  • ELA.6.R.1.3: Explain the influence of narrator(s) and point of view on a literary text.
  • ELA.6.V.1.3: Apply knowledge of context clues, figurative language, word relationships, reference materials, and/or background knowledge to determine and clarify the meaning of multiple-meaning and unknown words and phrases, appropriate to grade level.
  • ELA.6.V.1.2: Apply knowledge of Greek and Latin roots and affixes to determine meanings of words and phrases in grade-level content.
  • ELA.K12.EE.1.1: Cite evidence to explain and justify reasoning.

Materials

  • The interactive lesson
  • The Call of the Wild (Project Gutenberg or library)
  • Poster board, markers, a bathroom scale, household items, a backpack or box
  • Printable practice page

Vocabulary

  • setting: When and where a story happens, including its social conditions.
  • historical context: The real events and conditions of the time a story is set or written.
  • contrast: A strong difference between two things placed side by side.
  • third-person narrator: A storyteller outside the story who uses he, she, it and they.
  • primitive: Early, basic or wild; the opposite of civilized in London's novel.
  • foreshadowing: Hints about what will happen later in a story.
  • stampede: In gold-rush history, a sudden rush of people to a new gold strike.

Key questions to ask

  • How does this setting shape what Buck feels and does?
  • What does the narrator let us know that a dog couldn't say?
  • Whose land was the Klondike?
  • What clues hint at what will happen next?

Common mistakes to watch for

  • Setting is just background.
  • The Yukon was empty land.
  • Only white miners found and dug gold.
  • A dog story is a simple children's story.

Support (if the student is struggling)

Challenge (if the student is ready for more)

  • Read the Tr'ondëk Hwëch'in's own account of the gold rush on their First Nation's website (with a parent).
  • Compare London's description of Buck with a photo of a St. Bernard and a Scotch shepherd (collie).

Background for the teacher

Content note: The Call of the Wild contains frequent violence against dogs (beatings, dogfights, deaths), period racial and ethnic labels, and a stereotyped portrayal of an Indigenous people (the fictional "Yeehats") in Chapter VII. KCE chose the novel for its strong setting, character change and themes, and will teach the portrayal critically in Week 24. Parents should preview chapters. The lesson's discovery details follow Yukon government and Parks Canada sources: gold was found on Rabbit (later Bonanza) Creek on August 16 or 17, 1896, by Keish (Skookum Jim Mason), Káa Goox (Dawson Charlie) and George Carmack, with Shaaw Tláa (Kate Carmack) present; accounts differ on who found the first gold. The ships Excelsior and Portland reached San Francisco and Seattle in July 1897. Estimates: about 100,000 set out, about 30,000 to 40,000 reached Dawson. The North-West Mounted Police "ton of goods" rule was in force from 1898. Chief Isaac moved the Tr'ondëk Hwëch'in to Moosehide in 1897. The Gutenberg text spells "gravelled"; it is kept as printed.

Reconnect

    Answer: thesis

  2. Douglass used painful contrasts and irony. What device points out a painful contradiction to make a point?
    Answer: irony
    Week 17.
  3. Paine compared America to a growing child. What is that kind of comparison called?
    Answer: an analogy
    Week 18.
  4. In Unit 4 every claim needed evidence. How might that habit help you when you write about a novel?
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Claims about a novel (character, theme) need quotations or details from the text as evidence
    Model answer: When I say something about a character or a theme, I'll need to back it up with a quotation or detail from the novel, just like evidence in an argument.

Learn 1: Meet the Novel and Its Setting

  1. In what year does the story begin?
    Answer: 1897 (the fall)
    "in the fall of 1897, when the Klondike strike dragged men from all the world into the frozen North."
  2. The narrator uses he and Buck, not I. What point of view is this?
    Answer: third person
    A narrator outside the story.
  3. How might Jack London's own life have helped him write this novel? Give two connections.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: He went to the Klondike in 1897 and saw the setting firsthand; His hard, working childhood may connect to struggle and survival
    Model answer: London went to the Klondike in 1897 himself, so he knew the cold, the trails and the sled dogs firsthand. He also grew up poor and worked hard jobs as a child, so he understood what it's like to struggle to survive.

Learn 2: Reading Chapter I: Buck the King

  1. Find two details from the excerpt that show Buck thinks of himself as a king. Explain what they suggest about his character.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Two accurate details ("over this great demesne Buck ruled"; "king over all creeping, crawling, flying things ... humans included"; free to roam the whole estate); Explains: proud, confident, used to comfort and respect
    Model answer: London writes that "over this great demesne Buck ruled" and that he was "king over all creeping, crawling, flying things of Judge Miller's place, humans included." These details show Buck is proud and confident, and used to comfort and respect. He has never had to struggle.
  2. The first sentence says "trouble was brewing." Hints like this about what will happen later are called ___.
    Answer: foreshadowing
    It prepares readers for Buck's kidnapping.
  3. Use the roots.
    Answer: primary; civilian
    prim = first; civ = citizen.

Learn 3: The Real Klondike

  1. Which First Nation's homeland was the Klondike?
    Answer: the Tr'ondëk Hwëch'in (Hän-speaking First Nation)
    Chief Isaac led them during the rush.
  2. Using the historical context, explain why dogs like Buck were stolen and sold north in 1897.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Thousands of stampeders needed strong dogs to haul heavy supplies over snow; Dogs were worth a lot of money, so people stole and sold them
    Model answer: Thousands of gold seekers had to haul a ton of supplies over snow and ice, so they desperately needed big, strong dogs. Dogs sold for hundreds of dollars, so people like Manuel stole dogs and sold them north for profit.
  3. Many gold-rush stories describe the Yukon as an empty wilderness. Why is that description inaccurate, and why does it matter?
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: It was the homeland of the Tr'ondëk Hwëch'in and other First Nations; Indigenous people made the first strike; Matters: erasing Indigenous people hides their history and the harm the rush caused
    Model answer: The Yukon was the homeland of the Tr'ondëk Hwëch'in and other First Nations, and a Tagish man, Keish, was one of the people who made the first strike. Calling it empty erases the people who lived there and hides how the rush took over their fishing camp and land.

Learn 4: Setting Shapes Character

  1. What lesson does Buck learn from the man in the red sweater? Quote a phrase, and explain how this lesson is the opposite of Buck's life at Judge Miller's.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Lesson: a man with a club must be obeyed; might makes right (primitive law); Quoted phrase; Contrast: at Judge Miller's he ruled and was respected; now he is ruled by force
    Model answer: Buck learns that "a man with a club was a lawgiver, a master to be obeyed." At Judge Miller's place, Buck was a king who ruled freely and was respected. Now he must obey whoever holds power, which is the opposite of the comfortable, orderly world he came from.
  2. Based on Chapter I, predict how Buck might change as the story continues. Use one detail from the text to support your prediction.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: A reasonable prediction about change; Supported by a detail (he met the lesson "halfway," "latent cunning ... aroused," foreshadowing)
    Model answer: I predict Buck will become tougher and more cunning. The text says he met the lesson "halfway" and that "all the latent cunning of his nature" was "aroused," which suggests wild instincts are waking up inside him.

Hands-On

  1. How did carrying 25 pounds feel after 5 trips? Show your calculation for moving 2,000 pounds at 50 pounds per trip. How does this help explain why dogs were so valuable?
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Describes the experience; 2,000 ÷ 50 = 40 trips; Connects to dogs' value: dogs could haul heavy loads, saving many trips
    Model answer: By the fifth trip my legs were burning and I was breathing hard. 2,000 ÷ 50 = 40 trips over the pass, and each trip was miles of steep, icy trail. A team of dogs could pull hundreds of pounds on a sled, saving dozens of trips, so dogs were worth a fortune.
  2. List the places on your setting map and the three California quotations you added. Which quotation best shows the contrast with the North?
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Places listed; Three accurate quotations; A choice with reasoning about contrast
    Model answer: Places: Santa Clara Valley, San Francisco, Seattle, Skagway, Dyea, Chilkoot Pass, Dawson. Quotations: "sun-kissed Santa Clara Valley," "wide cool veranda," "wide-spreading lawns." "Sun-kissed" shows the contrast best, because the North is described as "Arctic darkness" and "frost."

Think Deeper

  1. Explain how Jack London uses setting in Chapter I to show who Buck is at the start of the novel and to prepare readers for change. Use at least two quotations.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: A clear claim about setting and character; Two accurate quotations; Explains the contrast between California and the club yard or the North; Mentions foreshadowing or the coming change
    Model answer: London uses the California setting to show Buck as a comfortable, proud ruler, and then breaks that setting apart to show that Buck's life is about to change. Buck lives in the "sun-kissed Santa Clara Valley" on a huge estate where "over this great demesne Buck ruled." The warm, orderly place matches his confident, royal personality. But from the first sentence London warns that "trouble was brewing," and by the end of the chapter, the man in the red sweater has taught Buck that "a man with a club was a lawgiver." The shift from sunlit lawns to a brutal yard shows that Buck will have to become a very different dog to survive.
  2. Imagine you are a member of the Tr'ondëk Hwëch'in in 1898, watching tens of thousands of stampeders arrive at your fishing camp. Write a short journal entry (in your own words) about what you see and how it affects your community. Base it on facts from the lesson.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Accurate facts (fishing camp overrun, Chief Isaac moving the people downriver, thousands of newcomers); A thoughtful, respectful perspective; Clear writing
    Model answer: Summer, 1898. Boats and rafts keep coming down the river, more people than I can count. They have built tents and shacks all over the place where we dried our salmon every summer. Chief Isaac says we will move our camp downriver to Moosehide, where we can keep our families together. The newcomers only talk about gold. They don't know this land or the songs our grandparents taught us here. I worry about the animals and the fish with so many people. But we will keep our songs and our ways, and we will remember.

Exit Check

  1. Where does Buck live at the start of the novel? (Name the valley.)
    Answer: the Santa Clara Valley, California
    At Judge Miller's place.
  2. What metal causes the rush to the Northland?
    Answer: gold
    "a yellow metal."
  3. The root prim means ___.
    Answer: first
    primitive, primary.
  4. Why does a writer's choice of setting matter? Use Buck as an example.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Setting shapes what characters must face and how they change; Buck's change from California comfort to the brutal North
    Model answer: The setting decides what problems a character faces. Buck was a proud king in sunny California, but in the harsh North he will have to change to survive.

Answer key: printable page

  1. Describe the setting at the start of the novel: time, place and Buck's life.
    Answer: Fall 1897, Judge Miller's estate in the Santa Clara Valley, California; Buck rules the estate as a proud, respected dog.
  2. Who made the first Klondike gold strike in 1896?
    Answer: Keish (Skookum Jim Mason), Káa Goox (Dawson Charlie) and George Carmack, with Shaaw Tláa (Kate Carmack).
  3. Why were dogs like Buck stolen and sold north?
    Answer: Stampeders needed strong dogs to haul supplies over snow; dogs were worth a lot of money.
  4. Quote and explain the lesson Buck learns from the man in the red sweater.
    Answer: "A man with a club was a lawgiver, a master to be obeyed": force rules in this new world.
  5. What is foreshadowing? Give the example from the first sentence.
    Answer: Hints about later events; "trouble was brewing."
  6. Use the roots: what do primitive and civilized have to do with prim and civ?
    Answer: Primitive: early, first, wild (prim = first); civilized: of cities and citizens (civ).
