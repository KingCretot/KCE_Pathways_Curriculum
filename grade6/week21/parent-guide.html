Week 21: Unit 5 Begins: Exponents, a New Novel, Ecosystems and the Kingdom of Ghana

Welcome to Unit 5. Math starts algebra with exponents, the order of operations and simple interest on savings. ELA begins a five-week novel study of Jack London's The Call of the Wild (1903), starting with its setting and the real Klondike Gold Rush. Science zooms out from the human body to ecosystems. Social Studies travels to West Africa and the kingdom of Ghana, which grew rich from the gold and salt trade.

How scoring works: each lesson scores itself. Your child gets two tries per question: full points on the first try, half points on the second, then the answer and explanation appear. A score of 85 or higher means the lesson is mastered. Written answers go to Jonathan, who scores them with a rubric.

Time this week: About 60 minutes of live lessons per subject, plus a hands-on activity, Think Deeper questions and an Exit Check for each. ELA also needs time to read Chapter I of the novel (about 30 minutes).

Gather this week: A bag of uncooked rice (or dried beans), a muffin tin, egg carton or 10 small cups, optionally a kitchen scale, a deck of playing cards (face cards removed), a family member, and paper and pencil (Math); a large sheet of paper or poster board, markers or colored pencils, a bathroom scale, household items to pack (canned food, a bag of rice or flour, blankets, a pot), a backpack or box, paper and pencil, and a copy of The Call of the Wild (free online or from the library) (ELA); string or yarn (about 12 feet) and 4 sticks or pencils, or a hula hoop, an outdoor thermometer, a ruler, a magnifying glass, a notebook and pencil, a cup of water, gloves, and optionally a phone or camera (Science); about 40 small items for salt (white beans, rice packets or white paper squares), about 40 small items for gold (yellow beads, pennies or yellow paper squares), a bowl, paper and pencil, 3 or more family members (one person can play two roles), and optionally a stopwatch (Social Studies).

Heads up: About the novel: The Call of the Wild includes frequent violence against dogs (including a beating in Chapter I), period racial and ethnic labels, and a stereotyped portrayal of an Indigenous people in the last chapter, which KCE will teach critically. Please preview each chapter. A free audiobook (LibriVox) is a good option for reading together. Science fieldwork goes outdoors: go along, watch for fire ants, poison ivy and snakes, use gloves and insect repellent (applied by an adult), and don't collect in state parks. Rice, beans and small beads are choking hazards for young children and pets.

ELA: Launching the Novel: Setting and Context

Your child begins The Call of the Wild, studies how London uses setting to show who Buck is, learns about the Klondike Gold Rush, including the Tagish and Tlingit people who made the first strike and the Tr'ondëk Hwëch'in First Nation whose homeland it was, and tries carrying a stampeder's load.

Ask at dinner: "How is Buck's life in California different from what's coming?"

Try it together: Read Chapter I together or listen to it on a free audiobook.

Signs it's clicking: They connect a setting detail to a character trait. Signs they need help: They retell the plot instead of analyzing. Ask, "What does that detail show about Buck?"

Math: Exponents and Order of Operations

Your child writes and evaluates powers (including fractions, decimals and negative bases), applies the full order of operations, and calculates simple interest on savings, with a preview of compound interest.

Ask at dinner: "What does the exponent touch? Which operation comes next?"

Try it together: Do the rice-doubling challenge together and guess how many cups a whole bag would fill.

Signs it's clicking: They explain why 24 ÷ 4 × 2 is 12, not 3. Signs they need help: They think 5² means 5 × 2. Have them write out 5 × 5.

Life Science: Living and Nonliving Parts of an Ecosystem

Your child sorts biotic and abiotic factors, learns the levels of ecology from organism to biosphere, explores how water, salt, temperature and sunlight decide who lives where in Florida (springs, mangroves, the Everglades, reefs), studies limiting factors and carrying capacity, and surveys two patches of ground.

Ask at dinner: "What nonliving things does this plant or animal need?"

Try it together: Go along on the quadrat survey and compare a sunny and a shady spot.

Signs it's clicking: They explain why manatees crowd into springs in winter. Signs they need help: They call a fallen log abiotic. Ask, "Was it ever alive?"

World and American History: Ghana and the Trans-Saharan Trade

Your child learns West Africa's geography, how camel caravans led by Amazigh traders carried salt south and gold north, how the Soninke kingdom of Ghana (Wagadu) grew rich by taxing that trade, what the geographer al-Bakri wrote about Ghana's king, and why Ghana declined.

Ask at dinner: "How could a king get rich without owning any mines?"

Try it together: Play the trans-Saharan trading game as a family.

Signs it's clicking: They explain how the nugget rule kept gold valuable. Signs they need help: They think medieval Ghana is the modern country of Ghana. Show them it was farther northwest.
