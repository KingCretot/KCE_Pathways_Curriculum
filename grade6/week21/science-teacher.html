Unit 5: Ecosystems · Week 21 · Life Science · Teacher Plan

Living and Nonliving Parts of an Ecosystem

Lesson length: 60 minutes live, plus the after-class interactive lesson

Objectives

  • Distinguish biotic (living) and abiotic (nonliving) factors in an ecosystem.
  • Describe the levels of ecological organization: organism, population, community, ecosystem, biosphere.
  • Explain how abiotic factors determine which organisms can live in an ecosystem.
  • Identify limiting factors and explain carrying capacity.
  • Investigate the biotic and abiotic factors of a local area with a field survey.

Standards

  • SC.7.L.17.3: Describe and investigate various limiting factors in the local ecosystem and their impact on native populations, including food, shelter, water, space, disease, parasitism, predation, and nesting sites.
  • SC.7.L.17.2: Compare and contrast the relationships among organisms such as mutualism, predation, parasitism, competition, and commensalism.
  • SC.6.L.14.1: Describe and identify patterns in the hierarchical organization of organisms from atoms to molecules to cells to tissues to organs to organ systems to organisms.
  • SC.6.N.1.1: Define a problem from the sixth grade curriculum, use appropriate reference materials to support scientific understanding, plan and carry out scientific investigation of various types, such as systematic observations or experiments, identify variables, collect and organize data, interpret data in charts, tables, and graphics, analyze information, make predictions, and defend conclusions.
  • NGSS MS-LS2-1: Analyze and interpret data to provide evidence for the effects of resource availability on organisms and populations of organisms in an ecosystem.

Materials

  • The interactive lesson
  • String and 4 sticks (or a hula hoop), outdoor thermometer, ruler, magnifying glass
  • A cup of water, gloves, notebook
  • Printable practice page

Vocabulary

  • ecosystem: All the living things in an area together with the nonliving parts of their environment.
  • biotic factor: A living or once-living part of an ecosystem, such as plants, animals, fungi and bacteria.
  • abiotic factor: A nonliving part of an ecosystem, such as sunlight, water, temperature, soil and air.
  • population: All the members of one species living in an area.
  • community: All the populations of different species living in an area.
  • limiting factor: Anything that keeps a population from growing larger, such as food, water, space or predators.
  • carrying capacity: The largest population an ecosystem can support over time.

Key questions to ask

  • Is it living, once living or nonliving?
  • Which abiotic factor makes this place right for this organism?
  • What is limiting this population?
  • What would happen if one factor changed?

Common mistakes to watch for

  • Abiotic factors don't matter to living things.
  • Dead things are abiotic.
  • Populations can grow forever if there is food.
  • Swamps are useless land.

Support (if the student is struggling)

  • Show photos of each Florida ecosystem.
  • Provide a quadrat data table template.

Challenge (if the student is ready for more)

  • Read the first chapter of The Everglades: River of Grass (borrow from the library).
  • Research the Comprehensive Everglades Restoration Plan and what it aims to fix.

Background for the teacher

Florida's NGSSS place ecosystem benchmarks (SC.7.L.17.x) in Grade 7; KCE's Grade 6 scope teaches them in Unit 5 as planned, and the Grade 7 course should build on this unit rather than repeat it. Douglas's book was published in 1947; Everglades National Park was dedicated December 6, 1947. Most Florida springs stay about 68 to 72°F (Blue Spring is about 73°F); manatees experience cold stress below about 68°F. The Florida scrub-jay is federally listed as threatened (1987) and is found only in Florida. The Miccosukee Tribe of Indians of Florida and the Seminole Tribe of Florida have lands in and near the Everglades.

Answer key: interactive lesson

Reconnect

  1. Week 16: Put these levels in order from smallest to largest.
    Answer: cell → tissue → organ → organ system → organism
    Today you keep zooming out past the organism.
  2. Keeping stable internal conditions is called ___.
    Answer: homeostasis
    Ecosystems also have balances that can be upset.
  3. Which gas do your lungs take in that plants release during photosynthesis?
    Answer: oxygen
    A connection between you and ecosystems.
  4. In Unit 4 you learned body systems depend on one another. Predict one way a plant and an animal in a Florida swamp might depend on each other.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: A reasonable dependence (food, shelter, oxygen, seed spreading, pollination, nutrients from waste)
    Model answer: A bird might eat a plant's berries and drop the seeds somewhere new, helping the plant spread while the plant feeds the bird.

Learn 1: Biotic and Abiotic Factors

  1. Biotic or abiotic?
    Answer: biotic; abiotic; biotic; abiotic
    A rotting log was once alive, so it is biotic.
  2. Is fire a biotic or abiotic factor? Explain, and describe one way fire affects living things in a Florida longleaf pine forest.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Abiotic (nonliving), though it burns biotic material; An effect: clears brush, helps longleaf seeds sprout, keeps forest open for animals like gopher tortoises, returns nutrients
    Model answer: Fire is abiotic because it is not alive, even though it burns living material. In longleaf pine forests, natural fires clear out shrubs, help longleaf seedlings grow, and keep the forest open so animals like gopher tortoises can find the plants they eat.

Learn 2: Levels of Ecology

  1. All the manatees living in Blue Spring State Park form a ___.
    Answer: population
    One species in one area.
  2. Manatees, fish, eelgrass and turtles in the spring together form a ___.
    Answer: community
    Many populations of different species.
  3. What is the difference between a community and an ecosystem? Use a Florida spring as your example.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: A community is only the living things; An ecosystem adds the abiotic factors (water, temperature, sunlight, rock); Spring example used
    Model answer: A community is just the living things in the spring: manatees, fish, eelgrass and turtles. The ecosystem includes all those plus the nonliving parts, like the 72°F water, the sunlight and the limestone the spring flows from.

Learn 3: Abiotic Factors Decide Who Lives Where

  1. Explain why manatees gather in Florida springs during winter. Name the abiotic factor and describe what could happen to manatees without the springs.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Abiotic factor: water temperature (springs stay about 72°F); Without warm water, manatees can suffer cold stress (illness or death) when the Gulf or Atlantic gets cold
    Model answer: Springs stay about 72°F all year, while the ocean can get cold in winter. Manatees can't handle cold water for long; it can make them sick or even kill them, so they crowd into warm springs to survive.
  2. A spring stays at 72°F. On a January morning the nearby river is 58°F. How many degrees warmer is the spring?
    Answer: 14 degrees
    72 − 58 = 14.

Learn 4: Limiting Factors and Carrying Capacity

  1. The largest population an ecosystem can support over time is its ___.
    Answer: carrying capacity
    Set by limiting factors.
  2. Name the main limiting factor for Florida scrub-jays and explain how people could help their population recover.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Limiting factor: oak scrub habitat (nesting sites, acorns), lost to development; A way to help: protect or restore scrub, controlled burns to keep scrub healthy, limit development
    Model answer: Their main limiting factor is habitat: they can only live in oak scrub, which has been cleared for buildings and groves. People can help by protecting the scrub that's left, restoring cleared land and using controlled burns to keep the scrub low and healthy.

Hands-On

  1. Make a table comparing your two quadrats: biotic factors found, and abiotic measurements. Which square had more kinds of living things? Which abiotic factor do you think made the biggest difference, and why?
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Data for both quadrats (biotic list and abiotic measurements); Compares diversity; Identifies an abiotic factor and explains its effect with evidence
    Model answer: Sunny square: 2 kinds of grass, dandelions, 6 ants, 1 beetle; air 84°F, ground 96°F, sandy soil, water soaked in in 8 seconds. Shady square under an oak: moss, 3 kinds of plants, fallen leaves, 2 earthworms, a mushroom, pill bugs; air 79°F, ground 75°F, darker soil, water soaked in in 30 seconds. The shady square had more kinds of living things. I think shade made the biggest difference: it kept the ground cooler and the soil moist, which worms, moss and fungi need.

Think Deeper

  1. In the 1900s, canals drained large parts of the Everglades. Using what you learned about biotic and abiotic factors, predict how draining water (an abiotic change) would affect at least three living things in the ecosystem.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Identifies water as the abiotic factor changed; Three predicted effects on organisms (fish lose habitat; wading birds lose food and nesting; alligators lose ponds; sawgrass replaced by other plants; fires spread; peat soil dries); Shows connections between organisms (a chain of effects)
    Model answer: Draining the water would shrink the shallow sheet of water fish and apple snails live in, so their populations would fall. Wading birds like wood storks feed on those fish, so they would lose food and nest less. Alligators dig ponds that hold water in the dry season; with less water overall, those ponds would dry up, hurting the animals that depend on them. Drier land also burns more easily, so fires could damage plants that need wet conditions.
  2. Marjory Stoneman Douglas called the Everglades a "river of grass" instead of a swamp. Why do you think that new description helped convince people to protect it? Connect to what you learned about argument in Unit 4.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: "River" suggests flowing, living, valuable; "swamp" suggests useless land; Connects to persuasion: a vivid phrase (emotion) backed by scientific facts (logic)
    Model answer: A "swamp" sounds like useless land to drain, but a "river of grass" sounds alive and moving, something worth protecting. Douglas used a vivid phrase, an emotional appeal, but she backed it with facts about how the water flows from Lake Okeechobee to Florida Bay, a logical appeal, just like the strong arguments we studied in Unit 4.

Exit Check

  1. Is sunlight a biotic or abiotic factor?
    Answer: abiotic
    It is not alive.
  2. All the populations living in an area together form a ___.
    Answer: community
    Add the abiotic factors and you have an ecosystem.
  3. Anything that keeps a population from growing larger is a ___ factor.
    Answer: limiting
    Food, water, space, predators, disease.
  4. Give one example of a biotic factor depending on an abiotic factor in a Florida ecosystem.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: An accurate example (manatees and warm spring water; corals and sunlight; sawgrass and flooding; mangroves and salt water)
    Model answer: Manatees depend on the warm 72°F water of Florida's springs to survive the winter.

Answer key: printable page

  1. Define biotic and abiotic factors and give two Florida examples of each.
    Answer: Biotic: living or once living (alligator, sawgrass). Abiotic: nonliving (spring water temperature, sunlight).
  2. List the five levels of ecology from smallest to largest with an example of each.
    Answer: Organism (one manatee), population (all manatees in a spring), community (all living things there), ecosystem (plus water, sunlight, rock), biosphere (all of Earth).
  3. Explain how one abiotic factor decides which organisms live in a Florida ecosystem.
    Answer: Example: salt water lets mangroves grow on coasts where most trees can't.
  4. What is a limiting factor? Give three examples.
    Answer: Something that keeps a population from growing: food, water, space, shelter, predators, disease.
  5. What is carrying capacity?
    Answer: The largest population an ecosystem can support over time.
  6. Why are Florida scrub-jays threatened?
    Answer: Their oak scrub habitat has been cleared for development and agriculture.
