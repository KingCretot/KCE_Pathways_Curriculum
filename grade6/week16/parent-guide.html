Week 16: Unit 4 Begins: Integers, Arguments, the Body's Building Blocks and Persia

Welcome to Unit 4, the start of the second half of the year. Math introduces negative numbers through elevations, temperatures and bank balances. ELA begins the argument unit with claims, reasons and evidence, including Susan B. Anthony's 1873 speech for women's right to vote. Science goes deep into how cells build tissues, organs and systems. Social Studies explores the Persian Empire, an empire of many peoples.

How scoring works: each lesson scores itself. Your child gets two tries per question: full points on the first try, half points on the second, then the answer and explanation appear. A score of 85 or higher means the lesson is mastered. Written answers go to Jonathan, who scores them with a rubric.

Time this week: About 60 minutes of live lessons per subject, plus a hands-on activity, Think Deeper questions and an Exit Check for each.

Gather this week: Painter's tape, sidewalk chalk or 21 sticky notes, a marker, a family member, paper and pencil, and optionally an indoor/outdoor thermometer (Math); 4 sheets of paper and tape for corner signs, about 10 index cards, a marker, a family member or two, and paper and pencil (ELA); 7 containers that nest inside one another (bowls, boxes or cups) or 7 sheets of paper of different sizes, sticky notes and a marker, a stalk of napa cabbage or a white carnation, a clear glass or jar of water, blue or red food coloring, a knife (used by an adult) and a cutting board, a magnifying glass, and paper and pencil (Science); a written message on a folded paper, a stopwatch or phone timer, painter's tape or chalk to mark stations, paper, pencil and colored pencils, a world map, atlas or globe, and optionally family members or friends as relay riders (Social Studies).

Heads up: Science: start the food-coloring test on the first day, because it needs 24 hours. Food coloring stains skin and fabric, so use old clothes; an adult does all cutting, and the stained plant should not be eaten. Social Studies includes running a relay: choose a clear, safe path away from streets and driveways, and walk through doorways and corners. ELA's argument lesson mentions drowning statistics for young children in a practice essay about swim lessons; if water is a sensitive topic in your family, you may want to read it together.

ELA: Claims, Reasons and Evidence

Your child tells facts, opinions and arguable claims apart, finds the claim, reasons and evidence in a practice argument about swim lessons, judges evidence as relevant, sufficient and credible, analyzes how Susan B. Anthony used the Constitution as evidence in 1873, learns the roots cred, ver and contra, and writes a Claim-Reason-Evidence-Explanation paragraph.

Ask at dinner: "What's your evidence, and how does it prove your reason?"

Try it together: Play Four Corners at dinner with a family claim, such as "Every family should have a game night once a week."

Signs it's clicking: They explain why one personal story is weaker evidence than a fact from a credible source. Signs they need help: They stop after the evidence. Ask, "So what? How does that prove your point?"

Math: Integers, Opposites and Absolute Value

Your child uses positive and negative numbers to describe elevations, temperatures and money, places integers on a number line, compares and orders them, finds opposites and absolute value, and tracks a bank balance that goes below zero.

Ask at dinner: "What does zero mean in this situation, and which side of zero is this number on?"

Try it together: Check a weather app for a cold city and compare its temperature with yours using "less than" and "greater than."

Signs it's clicking: They explain that -8 is less than -3 because it is farther left on the number line. Signs they need help: They think -8 is greater than -3 because 8 is greater than 3. Have them place both on a number line.

Life Science: Levels of Organization

Your child traces the levels from atoms and molecules to cells, tissues, organs, organ systems and organisms, learns how cells with the same DNA become specialized, studies the four main types of animal tissue, sees how organ systems work together, and watches a plant's xylem carry colored water.

Ask at dinner: "Which tissues are working when you scrape your knee?"

Try it together: Look at the cut cabbage stalk or flower stem together with a magnifying glass after 24 hours.

Signs it's clicking: They explain that an organ is made of several tissue types, and give an example. Signs they need help: They think different body cells have different DNA. Remind them each cell uses different parts of the same instructions.

World and American History: Persia: An Empire of Many Peoples

Your child locates the Persian Empire, learns how Cyrus the Great built it and how he treated conquered peoples, studies how Darius organized it with governors called satraps, coins, a shared language and the Royal Road, reads Herodotus on the Persian messengers and Ezra 1:2-3 on Cyrus, explores Persepolis and Zoroastrianism, and debates how historians should judge Cyrus.

Ask at dinner: "Was Persian tolerance kindness, a smart strategy or both?"

Try it together: Run the relay together and compare the walking time with the relay time.

Signs it's clicking: They ask who wrote a source and why before trusting it. Signs they need help: They mix up Persians and Arabs. Explain that Persians speak Persian (Farsi), a different language, and are the main people of Iran.
