Unit 4: The Human Body · Week 16 · Life Science · Teacher Plan

Levels of Organization

Lesson length: 60 minutes live, plus the after-class interactive lesson

Objectives

  • Describe the full hierarchy of organization from atoms and molecules to cells, tissues, organs, organ systems and organisms.
  • Identify the four main types of animal tissue and explain how each tissue's structure fits its function.
  • Explain how cells become specialized, even though nearly every cell in the body has the same DNA.
  • Explain how an organ is built from several tissues and how organ systems interact to maintain homeostasis.
  • Compare levels of organization in plants and animals, and investigate a plant's vascular tissue.

Standards

  • SC.6.L.14.1: Describe and identify patterns in the hierarchical organization of organisms from atoms to molecules to cells to tissues to organs to organ systems to organisms.
  • SC.6.L.14.5: Identify and investigate the general functions of the major systems of the human body (digestive, respiratory, circulatory, reproductive, excretory, immune, nervous, and musculoskeletal) and describe ways these systems interact with each other to maintain homeostasis.
  • SC.6.L.14.3: Recognize and explore how cells of all organisms undergo similar processes to maintain homeostasis, including extracting energy from food, getting rid of waste, and reproducing.
  • NGSS MS-LS1-3: Use argument supported by evidence for how the body is a system of interacting subsystems composed of groups of cells.

Materials

  • The interactive lesson
  • 7 nesting containers or papers, sticky notes, a marker
  • A napa cabbage stalk or white carnation, a glass, food coloring
  • A knife (adult use), cutting board, magnifying glass
  • Printable practice page

Vocabulary

  • specialized cell: A cell with a particular structure and job, such as a nerve cell or a red blood cell.
  • differentiation: The process by which unspecialized cells become specialized cells.
  • stem cell: An unspecialized cell that can divide and become different kinds of cells.
  • tissue: A group of similar cells that work together to do one job.
  • organ: A structure made of two or more tissue types that work together, such as the heart or a leaf.
  • organ system: A group of organs that work together to do a major job, such as the digestive system.
  • xylem and phloem: Plant vascular tissues: xylem carries water up from the roots; phloem carries sugars made in the leaves.

Key questions to ask

  • What can this level do that the level below cannot?
  • How does this structure fit its function?
  • Which tissues build this organ?
  • Which systems are working together here?

Common mistakes to watch for

  • Cells are not alive; only organisms are.
  • Different body cells have different DNA.
  • Blood is not a tissue.
  • Each organ system works on its own.

Support (if the student is struggling)

Challenge (if the student is ready for more)

  • Compare the number of organ systems in a frog and a human.

Background for the teacher

Cell counts: Sender, Fuchs and Milo (2016) estimated about 30 trillion human cells in a reference adult, of which about 84% (roughly 25 trillion) are red blood cells, and a similar number of bacteria. Estimates of distinct human cell types range from about 200 (classical histology) to many hundreds with modern single-cell methods. Bone marrow produces roughly 2 to 3 million red blood cells per second. The 11 organ systems commonly listed: integumentary, skeletal, muscular, nervous, endocrine, circulatory (cardiovascular), lymphatic/immune, respiratory, digestive, urinary/excretory and reproductive. Napa cabbage and white carnations show xylem staining within a few hours; celery was used in Week 9 for osmosis, so it is not repeated here.

Answer key: interactive lesson

Reconnect

  1. Keeping a stable internal balance, such as body temperature, is called ___.
    Answer: homeostasis
    A characteristic of life from Week 11.
  2. Humans are in which kingdom?
    Answer: Animalia
    Many-celled eukaryotes that eat other organisms.
  3. Cells with a nucleus are called eukaryotic. Cells with no nucleus, like bacteria, are called ___.
    Answer: prokaryotic
    Week 15.
  4. In Unit 2 you learned that cells are the basic unit of life. Name one job a single cell can do and one job it cannot do alone in your body.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: A job a single cell does (uses energy, makes proteins, divides, takes in nutrients); A job that needs many cells (pumping blood, thinking, walking, digesting a meal)
    Model answer: A single cell can use energy and make proteins, but one cell alone cannot pump blood around the body; that takes the whole heart.

Learn 1: From Atoms to Organisms

  1. Put these levels in order from smallest to largest.
    Answer: atom → molecule → cell → tissue → organ → organ system → organism
    Each level is built from the one before it.
  2. What is the smallest level of organization that is alive?
    Answer: the cell
    Atoms and molecules are not alive by themselves.
  3. Explain the idea of an emergent property using an example from your body that is not the heart or the brain.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: A new ability that appears only when parts work together at a higher level; A valid example (stomach digesting a meal, lungs breathing, legs walking, eyes seeing)
    Model answer: One stomach cell can make a little acid or mucus, but only the whole stomach, with its muscle squeezing and its lining releasing acid and enzymes, can churn and break down a meal. Digesting a meal is an ability that emerges at the organ level.

Learn 2: Specialized Cells: Same DNA, Different Jobs

  1. What is the name for the process by which unspecialized cells become specialized cells?
    Answer: differentiation
    Cells "differ" by using different genes.
  2. A nerve cell and a muscle cell in your arm have the same DNA. Why are they so different?
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Each cell uses (switches on) different parts of the DNA instructions; So they make different proteins and develop different shapes and jobs
    Model answer: They have the same instructions but use different parts of them. The nerve cell switches on genes for carrying signals, and the muscle cell switches on genes for proteins that contract, so they build different shapes and do different jobs.
  3. Explain how one feature of a red blood cell's structure fits its function.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: A structure (small flexible disk, dent in the middle, no nucleus, full of hemoglobin); Linked to its function (fitting through tiny vessels, more surface or room for carrying oxygen)
    Model answer: A red blood cell is small and flexible, so it can bend to squeeze through the tiniest blood vessels and deliver oxygen everywhere in the body.

Learn 3: The Four Tissue Types

  1. Name the tissue type.
    Answer: connective; nervous; epithelial
    Blood has cells in a liquid matrix, so it counts as connective tissue.
  2. Which type of muscle tissue pumps the heart?
    Answer: cardiac muscle
    Cardiac muscle never gets tired the way skeletal muscle does.
  3. Use the stomach to explain why an organ needs more than one type of tissue.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Names at least three tissues in the stomach and their jobs; Explains that digestion needs all the jobs working together
    Model answer: The stomach's epithelial lining releases acid and enzymes, its smooth muscle churns the food, connective tissue holds the layers together, and nervous tissue tells it when to work. No single tissue could digest a meal; the stomach needs all of those jobs at once.

Learn 4: Systems That Work Together, in Animals and Plants

  1. Which plant vascular tissue carries water up from the roots?
    Answer: xylem
    Phloem carries sugars.
  2. During a long run, name two organ systems that work together and explain how their teamwork helps maintain homeostasis.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Two correct systems (muscular, respiratory, circulatory, nervous, integumentary); How they cooperate (more oxygen delivered, heat released, signals sent) to keep conditions stable
    Model answer: The respiratory system breathes faster to bring in more oxygen, and the circulatory system pumps that oxygen to working muscles faster. Together they keep the muscles supplied, so the body's oxygen level stays balanced even during hard exercise.
  3. A human body has about 30 trillion cells. About 25 trillion of them are red blood cells. About what percent of your cells are red blood cells? Round to the nearest whole percent.
    Answer: 83 %
    25 ÷ 30 ≈ 0.83, about 83%. Red blood cells are tiny but extremely numerous.

Hands-On

  1. Describe your observations at 1, 4 and 24 hours, and what you saw in the cut stem. What tissue carried the color, and in which direction?
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Observations at three times; Describes the colored dots or lines in the cut stem; Identifies xylem carrying water (and color) upward from the cut end toward the leaves or petals
    Model answer: At 1 hour nothing showed. At 4 hours thin blue lines appeared in the cabbage's white rib. At 24 hours the leaf edges were tinted blue. When my dad cut the stem I counted about 14 blue dots. Those are bundles of xylem, which carried the colored water up from the bottom toward the leaves.
  2. For each of the seven levels in your nesting model, write one example from your own body. Then explain why the cabbage stalk counts as an organ, not a tissue.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Seven correct examples in order; Explains that the stalk contains several tissue types (dermal, ground, vascular) working together
    Model answer: Atom: oxygen. Molecule: water. Cell: red blood cell. Tissue: cardiac muscle. Organ: heart. Organ system: circulatory system. Organism: me. The cabbage stalk is an organ because it contains more than one tissue: a dermal outer layer, ground tissue and the xylem and phloem in its vascular bundles.

Think Deeper

  1. Some people compare the body to a city: cells are workers, tissues are teams, organs are buildings and organ systems are city services. Explain where this comparison works well and one place where it breaks down.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Explains how the levels map onto the city (accurately); Identifies at least one real limit of the analogy (workers can change jobs freely; city parts are not all made from one original cell with the same instructions; bodies grow and repair themselves; emergent properties); Uses correct vocabulary
    Model answer: The comparison works because, like workers in teams, cells of the same kind group into tissues, and different teams work together in buildings the way tissues build organs. City services such as water and power resemble organ systems like the circulatory system, and the whole city depends on all of them. It breaks down because city workers can change jobs, while most specialized cells cannot; and every cell in the body came from one original cell with the same DNA, which is nothing like how a city is built.
  2. When a person receives a kidney transplant, doctors replace an organ, not individual cells. Using levels of organization, explain why replacing the whole organ makes more sense than replacing kidney cells one at a time.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: The kidney's function depends on many tissues arranged in a precise structure; Individual cells cannot do the organ's job (emergent property); Organization at the organ level cannot be rebuilt cell by cell (at least not with today's technology)
    Model answer: A kidney filters blood because millions of cells are arranged into tissues and tiny structures that work together in a precise pattern. Individual kidney cells alone cannot filter blood; that ability only emerges when they are organized into the whole organ. Doctors cannot build that organization one cell at a time, so replacing the whole organ, with all its tissues already arranged, is the way to restore the function.

Exit Check

  1. Which tissue type covers and lines body surfaces, such as your skin?
    Answer: epithelial tissue
    Tightly packed cells protect and line.
  2. Which plant tissue carries sugars from the leaves to the rest of the plant?
    Answer: phloem
    Xylem carries water up; phloem carries sugars.
  3. What type of cell is still unspecialized and can become other kinds of cells?
    Answer: a stem cell
    Bone marrow stem cells make blood cells.
  4. Explain the difference between a tissue and an organ, with one example of each.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: A tissue is a group of similar cells doing one job; an organ is made of two or more tissue types working together; Correct examples
    Model answer: A tissue is a group of similar cells doing one job, like cardiac muscle. An organ is made of several tissues working together, like the heart, which has muscle, nervous, connective and epithelial tissue.

Answer key: printable page

  1. List the levels of organization from atom to organism, with a human example for each.
    Answer: Atom (oxygen), molecule (water), cell (muscle cell), tissue (muscle tissue), organ (heart), organ system (circulatory), organism (a person).
  2. Name the four main animal tissue types and one example of each.
    Answer: Epithelial (skin), connective (bone, blood), muscle (cardiac), nervous (brain).
  3. Explain differentiation in your own words.
    Answer: Unspecialized cells become specialized cells by using different parts of the same DNA.
  4. What is an emergent property? Give an example.
    Answer: An ability that appears only at a higher level, such as a heart pumping blood (not possible for one cell).
  5. Compare xylem and phloem.
    Answer: Xylem carries water and minerals up from roots; phloem carries sugars from leaves.
  6. Name two organ systems that work together when you are hot, and explain how.
    Answer: Nervous system senses heat and signals; integumentary system sweats; circulatory system sends blood to the skin to release heat.
