Unit 4: Making the Case (Argument) · Week 16 · ELA · Teacher Plan

Claims, Reasons and Evidence

Lesson length: 60 minutes live, plus the after-class interactive lesson

Objectives

  • Tell the difference between a fact, an opinion and an arguable claim.
  • Identify the claim, reasons and evidence in an argument.
  • Name types of evidence (facts and statistics, examples, expert testimony, anecdotes) and judge whether evidence is relevant, sufficient and credible.
  • Analyze how Susan B. Anthony used the Constitution as evidence in her 1873 argument.
  • Use the roots cred, ver and contra, and write a claim supported by a reason and evidence.

Standards

  • ELA.6.R.2.4: Track the development of an argument, identifying the types of reasoning used.
  • ELA.6.C.1.3: Write and support a claim using logical reasoning, relevant evidence from sources, elaboration, and a logical organizational structure with varied transitions.
  • ELA.6.V.1.2: Apply knowledge of Greek and Latin roots and affixes to determine meanings of words and phrases in grade-level content.
  • ELA.6.C.2.1: Present information orally, in a logical sequence, using nonverbal cues, appropriate volume, clear pronunciation, and appropriate pacing.
  • ELA.K12.EE.1.1: Cite evidence to explain and justify reasoning.

Materials

  • The interactive lesson
  • 4 sheets of paper, tape, about 10 index cards, a marker
  • Paper and pencil
  • Printable practice page

Vocabulary

  • claim: The main point an argument wants the reader to accept; it must be arguable.
  • reason: A statement that explains why the claim is true.
  • evidence: Facts, statistics, examples or expert words that prove a reason.
  • anecdote: A short personal story used as evidence; vivid but weak by itself.
  • credible: Trustworthy and believable, such as a source from a respected expert or organization.
  • counterclaim: An opposing point of view that an argument answers.
  • suffrage: The right to vote in political elections.

Key questions to ask

  • Is this arguable, or is it a fact or a preference?
  • What reason supports the claim?
  • Is the evidence relevant, sufficient and credible?
  • Where is the explanation?

Common mistakes to watch for

  • An argument means arguing angrily.
  • Any opinion is a claim.
  • A vivid story proves a point by itself.
  • Evidence explains itself.

Support (if the student is struggling)

  • Give a color code: claim (blue), reasons (green), evidence (gold), explanation (red).
  • Provide sentence starters: "According to ..." and "This shows that ..."
  • Read the Anthony excerpt aloud twice.

Challenge (if the student is ready for more)

  • Read the full Anthony speech and find two more pieces of evidence she used.
  • Find a real editorial in a local newspaper and map its claim, reasons and evidence.

Background for the teacher

Susan B. Anthony voted in Rochester, New York, on November 5, 1872, was arrested on November 18, and in 1873 delivered her speech (often titled "Is It a Crime for a Citizen of the United States to Vote?") in towns across Monroe County (and Ontario County after the trial was moved). Judge Ward Hunt directed a guilty verdict in June 1873 and fined her $100, which she never paid. Printed versions of the speech differ slightly; the excerpt follows the Internet Modern History Sourcebook text, which prints "tranquillity" (the engrossed Constitution reads "Tranquility"); the spelling is kept as printed, with a gloss. The library model's evidence paraphrases the BLS American Time Use Survey finding that the share of employed people working on an average weekday is far higher than on a weekend day. The Nineteenth Amendment was ratified in 1920; the Voting Rights Act of 1965 targeted discriminatory practices, and Native Americans in some states (for example, New Mexico and Arizona) won the vote only in 1948. Swimming facts: CDC, Drowning Facts (drowning is the leading cause of death for children ages 1 to 4; formal swim lessons can reduce drowning risk); Florida Department of Health, Drowning Prevention (drowning is the leading cause of unintentional death for Florida children ages 1 to 4). The swimming essay is a KCE practice text; the cousin anecdote is invented for practice.

Answer key: interactive lesson

Reconnect

  1. "The kitten's purr was a tiny motor." Simile or metaphor?
    Answer: metaphor
    It compares without like or as.
  2. What is the name of a 14-line poem with three quatrains and a couplet?
    Answer: a sonnet
    abab cdcd efef gg.
  3. The Constitution promises "the blessings of liberty." Using the root liber, what does liberty mean? Answer in one or two words.
    Answer: freedom
    liber means free, so liberty is freedom.
  4. In your poetry analysis, what is the difference between retelling a text and analyzing it? This difference matters for arguments too.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Retelling says what happens; analysis explains how and why the writer creates meaning
    Model answer: Retelling just says what happens. Analysis explains how the writer creates meaning and why it matters.

Learn 1: Fact, Opinion or Claim?

  1. Type fact, opinion or claim for this statement: "Every town should build a public pool."
    Answer: an arguable claim
    Reasonable people could disagree, and evidence (cost, safety, health) can support either side.
  2. Type fact, opinion or claim for this one: "The Atlantic Ocean borders Florida's east coast."
    Answer: a fact
    It can be checked on any map.
  3. Use the roots.
    Answer: verdict; credible
    ver (true) + dict (say); cred (believe) + ible (able to be).
  4. Turn this personal opinion into an arguable claim that evidence could support: "Summer is the best season."
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: A position others could disagree with; Evidence could support it (not just preference)
    Model answer: "Florida schools should start summer break in early May, because the hottest weeks make classrooms harder to cool and learning harder to focus on."

Learn 2: Reasons and Types of Evidence

  1. In the swimming argument, which paragraph states the writer's claim? Type the paragraph number.
    Answer: paragraph 1
    "Every child in Florida should learn to swim before the age of seven."
  2. Paragraph 4 tells about the writer's cousin swimming across a lake at scout camp. What type of evidence is that?
    Answer: an anecdote
    A short personal story.
  3. Which paragraph has the strongest evidence, 2 or 4? Explain using the tests relevant, sufficient and credible.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Paragraph 2 is stronger; Its evidence comes from credible organizations (CDC, Florida Department of Health) and is directly relevant; Paragraph 4 is one anecdote: vivid but not sufficient by itself
    Model answer: Paragraph 2 is stronger. Its facts come from credible sources, the CDC and the Florida Department of Health, and they directly support the reason that drowning is a danger. Paragraph 4 is one cousin's story; it is relevant, but one anecdote is not sufficient to prove anything about all children.
  4. Paragraph 5 answers a counterclaim, an opposing view. What do some families say about lessons?
    Answer: that lessons are too expensive
    The writer answers it with free and low-cost lessons. You will practice counterclaims more in Week 18.
  5. Paragraph 4's reason is "swimming opens doors." Suggest one more piece of evidence that would make it stronger, and name its type.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: A realistic piece of evidence that supports this reason; Correctly names its type
    Model answer: The writer could add a statistic about how many Florida jobs, like lifeguarding or marine science, require swimming. That would be a fact or statistic, stronger than one story.

Learn 3: Anthony's Argument: The Constitution as Evidence

  1. What document did Anthony use as her main evidence?
    Answer: the U.S. Constitution (its preamble)
    She quotes "We, the people."
  2. Explain Anthony's reasoning: how does the phrase "we, the people" support her claim that women had the right to vote?
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: "The people" means everyone, not only men; If women are part of the people who formed the Union, they share its rights, including voting
    Model answer: The Constitution says "we, the people," not "we, the male citizens." Women are people, so they were part of the people who formed the Union. If the Union was formed to secure liberty for the whole people, women should have the same rights, including the right to vote.
  3. Anthony writes "not we, the white male citizens; nor yet we, the male citizens; but we, the whole people." Which word does she repeat, and why would repeating it persuade listeners?
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Names the repeated word we; Explains the effect: it includes the listeners, builds a rhythm and stresses that the people means everyone
    Model answer: She repeats "we." Repeating it pulls the listeners in, as if they are all part of the same "we," and the rhythm makes the point stick: the whole people, not just men, formed the Union.
  4. The Nineteenth Amendment (1920) was a huge victory, but the lesson says many Americans still could not vote until 1965 or later. Why is it important to include that fact when telling the history of voting rights?
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Telling the whole truth: voting rights expanded in stages, and some groups were excluded longer; Recognizes the struggles of Black Americans, Native Americans and others
    Model answer: It shows that history happened in steps, not all at once. Saying "women got the vote in 1920" leaves out the Black, Native American, Asian American and Latino citizens who were still blocked for decades. Including it tells the whole, true story.

Learn 4: Writing a Claim with a Reason and Evidence

  1. In the model paragraph, which part explains how the evidence proves the reason? Type the name of the part.
    Answer: the explanation
    The last sentence connects the evidence to the reason.
  2. Write your own C-R-E-E paragraph on this claim or one of your own: "Every homeschool family should take one field trip a month." You may use a fact you know is true or an example; name where it comes from.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: A clear claim; A reason with "because"; Evidence with a source or a specific example; An explanation that connects evidence to the reason
    Model answer: Every homeschool family should take one field trip a month, because students remember what they see in person. When my family visited the Mote Aquarium in Sarasota, I watched a manatee being fed, and I still remember that manatees eat about 10% of their body weight a day, a fact the aquarium's guide told us. Seeing it in person made the fact stick far better than reading it, so regular field trips would help homeschoolers remember more of what they learn.

Hands-On

  1. Write your chosen claim, the corner you stood in at first, your two strongest and two weakest cards, and why. Did the evidence move you to a different corner?
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Claim and starting position; Two strongest and two weakest cards named; Explains strength with relevant, sufficient or credible; Reflects on whether the evidence changed the position
    Model answer: Claim: students ages 11 to 13 should have a set bedtime on school nights. I started in AGREE. Strongest: "The American Academy of Sleep Medicine says children 6 to 12 need 9 to 12 hours of sleep and teens 13 to 18 need 8 to 10" (credible, relevant to ages 11 to 13) and "Students who sleep too little often have trouble paying attention in class" (a relevant fact). Weakest: "Everyone knows kids need sleep" (no source) and "My friend says bedtimes are for babies" (not credible). The evidence moved me to STRONGLY AGREE.

Think Deeper

  1. Compare the swimming argument and Anthony's argument. Which uses evidence more effectively? Explain using at least one quotation from each and the tests relevant, sufficient and credible.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: A clear judgment; Accurate quotation from each text; Applies relevant, sufficient and credible; Considers the purpose and audience of each
    Model answer: Both arguments use credible evidence, but Anthony's is more powerful. The swimming argument quotes the CDC, saying drowning "is the leading cause of death for children ages 1 to 4," which is credible and relevant, but it also leans on a single anecdote about a cousin. Anthony uses the most credible evidence possible for her audience, the Constitution itself: "We, the people of the United States." Her reasoning, "not we, the male citizens ... but we, the whole people," turns the nation's own words into proof. For citizens deciding what the law means, that evidence is hard to argue with.
  2. Here is a weak argument: "Kids should get paid for good grades. Everyone wants money, and my neighbor's son got an A after his parents paid him." Identify two problems with its evidence, then rewrite it as a stronger short argument (you may change the claim).
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Identifies weak evidence: a generalization with no source ("everyone"), a single anecdote; A rewrite with a clear claim, reason and better evidence or reasoning; Explains how the rewrite is stronger
    Model answer: Problem 1: "Everyone wants money" is a generalization with no evidence. Problem 2: one neighbor's son is a single anecdote, not sufficient. Rewrite: "Families should reward effort, not grades, because effort is something students control. A student who studies hard but earns a B has still learned the habit that leads to success, and rewarding effort encourages that habit for every subject." This is stronger because the reason is logical and it doesn't depend on one story.

Exit Check

  1. Type fact, opinion or claim: "Our county should add bike lanes to every main road."
    Answer: an arguable claim
    Reasonable people could disagree, and evidence can support it.
  2. What type of evidence is this? "According to the National Weather Service, Florida has more lightning strikes per square mile than most states."
    Answer: a fact or statistic from an expert organization
    Verifiable information from a credible organization.
  3. The root contra means ___.
    Answer: against
    contradict, contrary.
  4. Why does an argument need an explanation after its evidence?
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: The explanation shows how the evidence proves the reason; without it the reader must guess the connection
    Model answer: The explanation shows how the evidence proves the reason. Without it, the reader has to guess why the fact matters.

Answer key: printable page

  1. Label each as fact, opinion or claim: (1) Tampa is in Florida. (2) Chocolate is delicious. (3) Our state should fund more public pools.
    Answer: (1) fact; (2) opinion; (3) claim.
  2. Name four types of evidence and give an example of each.
    Answer: Facts and statistics (the CDC drowning statistic); expert testimony (a pediatrician's statement); examples (a city that added free lessons); anecdotes (the cousin's story).
  3. What are the three tests for strong evidence? Define each.
    Answer: Relevant (supports the reason), sufficient (enough), credible (trustworthy source).
  4. Explain Susan B. Anthony's claim and evidence in your own words.
    Answer: Claim: women already had the right to vote as citizens. Evidence: the Constitution begins "We, the people," which includes women.
  5. Write a C-R-E-E paragraph for this claim: "Our town should plant more shade trees."
    Answer: Model: Our town should plant more shade trees, because shade keeps streets and sidewalks cooler. According to the U.S. Environmental Protection Agency, shaded surfaces can be 20 to 45°F cooler than unshaded ones. Cooler sidewalks make it safer for children and older adults to walk outside in a Florida summer, so more trees would protect people's health.
  6. Use the roots: what do credible, verify and contradict mean?
    Answer: Credible: believable (cred); verify: prove true (ver); contradict: speak against (contra + dict).
