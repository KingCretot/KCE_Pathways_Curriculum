Unit 4: The Mediterranean World · Week 16 · World and American History · Teacher Plan

Persia: An Empire of Many Peoples

Lesson length: 60 minutes live, plus the after-class interactive lesson

Objectives

  • Locate the Persian Empire and explain how geography and roads connected its regions.
  • Explain how Cyrus the Great built the empire and how his policy toward conquered peoples helped him hold it.
  • Describe how Darius I organized the empire with satrapies, tribute, coins, a shared official language and the Royal Road.
  • Describe the basic beliefs of Zoroastrianism and the art and purpose of Persepolis.
  • Analyze primary sources (Herodotus and the Book of Ezra) and compare how historians have interpreted Cyrus.

Standards

  • SS.6.W.3.2: Explain the causes and effects of the Persian and Peloponnesian Wars (background: this week builds the Persian side before the Greek wars in Week 17).
  • SS.6.W.2.9: Identify key figures and basic beliefs of the Israelites and determine how these beliefs compared with those of others in the geographic area.
  • SS.6.G.2.5: Interpret how geographic boundaries invite or limit interaction with other regions and cultures.
  • SS.6.G.2.6: Explain the concept of cultural diffusion, and identify the influences of different ancient cultures on one another.
  • SS.6.W.1.3: Interpret primary and secondary sources.
  • SS.6.W.1.5: Describe the roles of historians and recognize varying historical interpretations (historiography).

Materials

  • The interactive lesson
  • A folded paper dispatch, timer, tape or chalk
  • Paper, pencil, colored pencils
  • A world map, atlas or globe
  • Printable practice page

Vocabulary

  • empire: A group of lands and peoples ruled by one government or ruler.
  • satrapy: A province of the Persian Empire, ruled by a governor called a satrap.
  • tribute: Payment in money or goods that a ruler demands from the peoples he controls.
  • tolerance: Allowing people to keep beliefs and customs different from your own.
  • Royal Road: A road of about 1,600 miles from Sardis to Susa, with stations for royal messengers.
  • Zoroastrianism: The religion of many Persians, founded by the prophet Zarathustra, which teaches the struggle between good and evil.
  • daric: A gold coin issued by the Persian kings, used across the empire.

Key questions to ask

  • How do you control people who speak different languages and worship different gods?
  • Who wrote this source, when, and why?
  • Was Persian tolerance kindness, strategy or both?
  • What do the Persepolis carvings leave out?

Common mistakes to watch for

  • Persians were the same as the Arabs (Persian is an Indo-European language; the Arab conquest of Iran came in the 630s to 650s CE, about 1,000 years after the Achaemenid Empire fell).
  • Cyrus did not conquer anyone by force.
  • Herodotus is either completely reliable or completely made up.
  • Ancient empires were all ruled the same way.

Support (if the student is struggling)

  • Give a blank map to label with Sardis, Susa, Persepolis, Babylon and Memphis.
  • Read the Herodotus passage aloud twice and paraphrase sentence by sentence.
  • Pre-teach saith, hath and skilfully.

Challenge (if the student is ready for more)

  • Read Herodotus 5.52-54 on the Royal Road stations and check his arithmetic.
  • Research the Persepolis Fortification Tablets and what they reveal about women workers' rations.

Background for the teacher

Dates follow the British Museum and the Encyclopaedia Iranica: Cyrus defeats Astyages of Media about 550 BCE, takes Sardis about 547 (some scholars prefer a later date), and Babylon in October 539; Cambyses conquers Egypt in 525; Darius I reigns 522-486; Alexander burns Persepolis in 330. Herodotus (3.89) lists 20 satrapies (nomoi) for Darius; Darius's own inscriptions list 23 to 30 lands. The phrase "eyes and ears of the king" comes from Xenophon (Cyropaedia 8.2.10-12); Herodotus 1.114 mentions "the King's Eye." Herodotus 5.52-53 gives 111 stations and 13,500 stades (about 1,500 to 1,600 miles) traveled at 150 stades a day in 90 days. The Herodotus 8.98 text follows Macaulay's 1890 translation (Project Gutenberg), keeping his British spelling "skilfully" with a gloss. The New York General Post Office (James A. Farley Building) inscription was adapted from Herodotus by William Mitchell Kendall. The Apadana east staircase includes a delegation usually identified as Ethiopians (Kushites) bringing an elephant tusk, a vessel and an okapi or giraffe; Herodotus (3.17-25) describes Cambyses' failed expedition against them. The Cyrus Cylinder (British Museum 90920) is quoted here only in paraphrase. Its description as a "charter of human rights" was popularized in the 1960s and 1970s; scholars such as Irving Finkel and Amélie Kuhrt stress its Mesopotamian royal-propaganda conventions. Zoroastrian population estimates vary (roughly 100,000 to 200,000).

Answer key: interactive lesson

Reconnect

  1. Name one religion that spread along the Silk Roads through cultural diffusion.
    Answer: Buddhism (also Christianity, Manichaeism and later Islam)
    Buddhism traveled from India to China along the Silk Roads.
  2. Which Chinese dynasty opened the Silk Roads to the west, beginning about 138 BCE?
    Answer: the Han
    Zhang Qian's journeys under Emperor Wu.
  3. The Mandate of Heaven was the idea that heaven gave a ruler the right to ___ as long as he ruled justly.
    Answer: rule
    Week 13.
  4. In Unit 3 you saw how trade routes connected faraway peoples. Give one way a good road helps a ruler control a large territory.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Moves armies, messages, officials or tax payments quickly
    Model answer: A good road lets a ruler send messages and soldiers quickly, so he can stop a rebellion before it spreads.

Learn 1: Cyrus the Great Builds an Empire

  1. Put these events in order from earliest to latest.
    Answer: Cyrus defeats the Medes → Cyrus conquers Lydia → Cyrus conquers Babylon → Cambyses conquers Egypt
    About 550, 547, 539 and 525 BCE.
  2. Which city did Cyrus conquer in 539 BCE, freeing the exiles of Judah to return home?
    Answer: Babylon
    Ezra 1 records the decree.
  3. In Ezra, Cyrus says the LORD God of heaven gave him his kingdoms. In the Cyrus Cylinder, he credits Babylon's god Marduk. What does this tell you about how Cyrus spoke to different peoples, and why might that be a smart way to rule?
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: He honored each people's own god or beliefs (or the writers presented him that way); This won loyalty and made him look like a rightful ruler, so fewer people rebelled
    Model answer: Cyrus, or the people writing about him, presented him as chosen by each people's own god. That was smart because conquered peoples were more likely to accept a ruler who respected their religion, so they were less likely to rebel.

Learn 2: Darius Organizes the Empire

  1. What was the title of the governor of a Persian province?
    Answer: satrap
    A province was a satrapy.
  2. Darius's tools for running the empire.
    Answer: daric; Aramaic
    A shared coin and a shared written language tied the empire together.
  3. Why did Darius have separate generals and royal inspectors report to him, instead of letting each satrap control everything in his province?
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: To keep any satrap from becoming too powerful or rebelling; Several officials checking one another gave the king accurate information
    Model answer: If one satrap controlled the army, the money and the courts, he could rebel and make himself king. Having separate officials report to Darius meant they checked one another, and the king heard the truth about each province.

Learn 3: The Royal Road and Herodotus

  1. The Royal Road was about 1,600 miles long. A traveler on foot took about 90 days. About how many miles a day is that? Round to the nearest whole mile.
    Answer: 18 miles
    1,600 ÷ 90 ≈ 17.8, about 18 miles a day. Herodotus's own figure, 150 stades, is about 17 miles.
  2. According to Herodotus, how much of the journey is each man and horse responsible for?
    Answer: one day's journey
    "each man and horse appointed for a day's journey"
  3. Herodotus was Greek, and Persia had invaded Greece. Why does it matter to a historian that his description of the Persian messengers is full of admiration?
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: A source that admires an enemy is more believable on that point (less likely to be exaggerated in Persia's favor); Still compares with other sources because Herodotus repeated stories he could not check
    Model answer: Herodotus had no reason to flatter the Persians, so when he admires their messenger system, his praise is more believable. A historian would still check it against Persian records, like the Persepolis tablets, because Herodotus repeated stories he heard.

Learn 4: Persepolis, Zoroastrianism and Many Peoples

  1. Zoroastrians worship Ahura Mazda. What does the name mean?
    Answer: the Wise Lord
    Ahura (lord), Mazda (wise).
  2. Which Black African kingdom of the Nile stayed largely independent of Persia, and appears bringing gifts on the Persepolis carvings?
    Answer: Kush (Nubia)
    Persian inscriptions call them Kushiya; the Greeks said Ethiopians.
  3. The Persepolis staircases show many peoples bringing gifts, with no battles. What message did Darius want visitors to take away? Is that the whole truth about the empire?
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Message: the king rules a peaceful, united world of many peoples who honor him; Not the whole truth: the empire was built by conquest, collected tribute by force and faced revolts
    Model answer: Darius wanted visitors to see that all peoples honor the king and live together peacefully under him. That is not the whole truth: the empire was built by conquest, the "gifts" were often required tribute, and some peoples, like the Egyptians and Babylonians, revolted.

Hands-On

  1. Record your walk time and relay time, how many times faster the relay was, and what that means for the 90-day journey on the Royal Road.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Both times recorded; Correct comparison (walk time ÷ relay time); Applies the ratio to 90 days with correct arithmetic
    Model answer: Walking: 2 minutes 30 seconds (150 seconds). Relay: 50 seconds. 150 ÷ 50 = 3, so the relay was 3 times faster. At that rate, the 90-day walk would take 90 ÷ 3 = 30 days. Real Persian riders on fresh horses were even faster, about a week.
  2. Describe your four satrapies, the tribute and custom for each, and your two loyalty rules. Which of Darius's methods did you copy?
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Four satrapies with tribute and a kept custom; Two loyalty rules; Connects at least one rule to a real Persian method (inspectors, separate generals, roads, tolerance)
    Model answer: Kitchen satrapy (satrap: my brother) pays tribute of one snack a day and keeps its custom of Sunday pancakes. Garage satrapy (satrap: my dad) pays tribute of one chore a week and keeps its custom of loud music. Bedroom satrapy (satrap: my sister) pays tribute of folded laundry and keeps its quiet hours. Backyard satrapy (satrap: the dog) pays tribute of guarding the gate and keeps its custom of digging. Rule 1: a royal inspector (me) checks each satrapy once a week. Rule 2: every satrapy keeps its own customs as long as it pays tribute. I copied Darius's inspectors, the eyes and ears of the king, and Cyrus's tolerance.

Think Deeper

  1. The Cyrus Cylinder has sometimes been called "the first charter of human rights." Many historians disagree: they say it is royal propaganda, written in an old Babylonian style that praised a new king, and that Cyrus still conquered peoples and demanded tribute. Take a side, using evidence from this lesson, and answer one point from the other side.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: A clear position; At least two pieces of evidence (the Cylinder, Ezra, tribute, conquest, Darius's methods); Answers a counterargument; Shows understanding that sources were written for a purpose
    Model answer: I agree with the historians who say the Cylinder is mostly propaganda. It was written to make Cyrus look like Marduk's chosen ruler to the Babylonians, in the same style earlier kings used. Cyrus still conquered Babylon by war and collected tribute. Some say the return of the exiles of Judah, recorded in Ezra, proves he respected rights. That is real evidence of tolerance, but letting a people go home was also a smart way to win loyalty on the empire's western frontier. Tolerance used as a tool of power is not the same as a charter of rights for everyone.
  2. The Persians let many conquered peoples keep their religions, languages and laws. The Assyrians you studied in Unit 2 often deported whole peoples and destroyed temples. Which approach do you think made an empire last longer, and why? Use evidence and reasoning.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: A clear judgment; Explains how tolerance reduced rebellion and won loyalty (or argues the other side with reasons); Uses at least one piece of evidence from the lesson; Acknowledges limits (Persia still faced revolts and fell to Alexander)
    Model answer: Tolerance made an empire last longer. Peoples who could keep their gods and customs had less reason to rebel, and leaders like Cyrus gained support, as the exiles of Judah show. The Persian Empire lasted about 220 years and held together more peoples than any empire before it. Tolerance did not prevent every revolt, since Egypt and Babylon rose up, and it could not stop Alexander's army, but it helped Persia hold dozens of peoples together for generations.

Exit Check

  1. Who founded the Persian Empire around 550 BCE?
    Answer: Cyrus the Great
    Cyrus II.
  2. What did the Persians call a province of their empire?
    Answer: a satrapy
    Ruled by a satrap.
  3. Which road linked Sardis to Susa?
    Answer: the Royal Road
    About 1,600 miles.
  4. Name one way the Persian kings kept their huge empire united.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: A correct method (satraps and inspectors, roads and messengers, tolerance, coins, a shared official language)
    Model answer: They let conquered peoples keep their religions, which reduced rebellions.

Answer key: printable page

  1. Name the two cities at each end of the Royal Road and explain why the road mattered.
    Answer: Sardis and Susa; it let messengers, officials and armies move quickly to control the empire.
  2. How did Cyrus treat many conquered peoples, and why was that a useful policy?
    Answer: He let them keep their religions and customs if they paid tribute; it won loyalty and reduced rebellion.
  3. List four ways Darius organized the empire.
    Answer: Satrapies with satraps; inspectors and separate officials; tribute and the daric coin; Aramaic as a shared language; the Royal Road and Nile-Red Sea canal.
  4. What are the main teachings of Zoroastrianism?
    Answer: Worship of Ahura Mazda, the Wise Lord; each person chooses between truth and the lie; good thoughts, good words, good deeds.
  5. Why must historians read Herodotus carefully?
    Answer: He was Greek, writing after Persia invaded Greece, and repeated stories he could not check.
  6. Who were the Kushites on the Persepolis staircases, and what was their relationship to Persia?
    Answer: The Black African people of Kush (Nubia), up the Nile from Egypt; they brought gifts and stayed largely independent.
