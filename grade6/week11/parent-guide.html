Week 11: Mesopotamia, What Makes Something Alive, Unit Rates and Figurative Language

Unit 3 begins. This week your child travels to Mesopotamia, where writing began; learns the characteristics that all living things share; finds unit rates for speed, pace and prices; and reads poems by Christina Rossetti, Carl Sandburg, Emily Dickinson and Paul Laurence Dunbar to see how figurative language creates tone.

How scoring works: each lesson scores itself. Your child gets two tries per question: full points on the first try, half points on the second, then the answer and explanation appear. A score of 85 or higher means the lesson is mastered. Written answers go to Jonathan, who scores them with a rubric.

Time this week: About 60 minutes of live lessons per subject, plus 30 to 45 minutes of after-class work per subject.

Gather this week: Air-dry clay or play dough, a chopstick or popsicle stick with a triangular end (an adult can trim it), and a rolling pin or glass (Social Studies); 3 packets of active dry yeast, sugar, warm and cold water, 3 plastic bottles of the same size, 3 balloons, a funnel, measuring cups and spoons, and a tape measure (Science); a timer, a tape measure or a measured 20-meter path, a book, and two grocery items of different sizes or their shelf tags (Math); index cards, a notebook and a window or safe outdoor spot to observe the weather (ELA).

Heads up: ELA reads Dunbar's "Sympathy," which uses the image of a caged bird injuring itself on its bars to express the pain of lost freedom, and connects it to racism in Dunbar's time. Your child may want to talk about it. Math asks your child to count heartbeats after a minute of jumping jacks; skip the exercise if your child has a health reason to. Pour the yeast mixtures down the sink afterward; do not drink them.

ELA: Figurative Language

Your child learns personification, hyperbole, idiom and imagery alongside simile and metaphor, and explains how figurative language creates tone in four classic poems. They write a poem that turns a Florida storm into a character.

Ask at dinner: "What tone does this poem have, and which words create it?"

Try it together: Listen to your child's weather poem and tell them the tone you hear.

Signs it's clicking: They explain what a comparison shows, not just name the device. Signs they need help: They call every comparison a simile. Ask: does it use like or as?

Math: Unit Rates

Your child finds unit rates (miles per hour, beats per minute, pages per hour), including with fractions, uses both unit rates of a ratio, compares rates, and learns how shrinkflation and cost per use affect shopping.

Ask at dinner: "How much for one?"

Try it together: Help your child count their heart rate and time their walking pace, then check a product in your pantry for shrinkflation.

Signs it's clicking: They choose the unit rate that answers the question and know a bigger pace number means slower. Signs they need help: They divide in the wrong order. Ask: miles per hour means miles for each what?

Life Science: Characteristics of Living Things

Your child learns seven characteristics of life, the levels of organization from cell to organism, and homeostasis; debates hard cases like fire, viruses and a tardigrade frozen for 30 years; and tests whether yeast is alive with the balloon investigation.

Ask at dinner: "Is fire alive? Prove it either way."

Try it together: Watch the yeast balloons grow together and ask your child which characteristic of life the balloon shows.

Signs it's clicking: They check all seven characteristics, not just one or two. Signs they need help: They say anything that moves is alive. Ask about a robot vacuum.

World and American History: Mesopotamia and the First Writing

Your child compares life on the Tigris and Euphrates with life on the Nile, learns how writing grew from counting tokens to cuneiform, meets Enheduanna, the earliest known named author, Gilgamesh and Hammurabi's laws, and writes numbers in base 60. They make a clay tablet in cuneiform.

Ask at dinner: "Why did people first start writing?"

Try it together: Try to read the numbers on your child's clay tablet using their chart.

Signs it's clicking: They explain that writing began with record keeping and compare Mesopotamia with Kemet. Signs they need help: They think writing began with stories. Ask what the oldest tablets recorded.
