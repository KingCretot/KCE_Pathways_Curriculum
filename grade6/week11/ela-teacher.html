Unit 3: The Power of Poetry · Week 11 · ELA · Teacher Plan

Figurative Language

Lesson length: 60 minutes live, plus the after-class interactive lesson

Objectives

  • Identify and explain simile, metaphor, personification, hyperbole, idiom and imagery.
  • Explain how figurative language contributes to the tone and meaning of a poem.
  • Analyze personification and implied metaphor in poems by Rossetti and Sandburg.
  • Trace an extended metaphor and a symbol in poems by Dickinson and Dunbar.
  • Use the roots sym, path, hyper and phor, and write an original poem using figurative language.

Standards

  • ELA.6.R.3.1: Explain how figurative language contributes to tone and meaning in text(s).
  • ELA.6.R.1.2: Analyze the development of stated or implied theme(s) throughout a literary text.
  • ELA.6.V.1.2: Apply knowledge of Greek and Latin roots and affixes to determine meanings of words and phrases in grade-level content.
  • ELA.6.C.1.2: Write personal or fictional narratives using narrative techniques, precise words and phrases, and figurative language.
  • ELA.K12.EE.1.1: Cite evidence to explain and justify reasoning.

Materials

  • The interactive lesson
  • Index cards, a notebook
  • A window or a safe outdoor spot for weather observation
  • Printable practice page

Vocabulary

  • figurative language: Words used in a non-literal way to create a picture or feeling.
  • literal: Meaning exactly what the words say.
  • personification: Giving human qualities or actions to something that is not human.
  • hyperbole: Exaggeration on purpose, for effect.
  • idiom: A common saying whose meaning is different from the meaning of its words.
  • symbol: Something concrete that stands for a bigger idea, such as a dove for peace.
  • tone: The speaker's attitude toward the subject, shown through word choice (hopeful, angry, playful).
  • roots sym, path, hyper, phor: sym = together (sympathy); path = feeling (empathy); hyper = over, beyond (hyperbole); phor = carry (metaphor: "carry across").

Key questions to ask

  • What is being compared, and why that comparison?
  • What tone do you hear, and which words create it?
  • What does this concrete thing stand for?
  • How would the poem change if this word were different?

Common mistakes to watch for

  • Every comparison is a simile.
  • Figurative language is just decoration (it creates meaning and tone).
  • Tone is the same as mood (tone is the speaker's attitude; mood is the reader's feeling).
  • Idioms mean what their words say.

Support (if the student is struggling)

  • Provide a toolkit card with one example of each device.
  • Read each poem aloud twice before discussing.
  • Use a two-column chart: quotation / what it shows.

Challenge (if the student is ready for more)

  • Read Langston Hughes's "Dreams" and compare its metaphors with Dickinson's.
  • Find an idiom in another language your family speaks and explain its literal and real meaning.

Background for the teacher

All four poems are in the public domain: Rossetti, Sing-Song (1872); Sandburg, Chicago Poems (1916); Dickinson, written about 1861; the text printed is the public-domain first printing (Poems, Second Series, 1891), whose editors regularized her dashes; Dunbar, Lyrics of the Hearthside (1899). Dunbar (1872 to 1906) was born in Dayton, Ohio, to Joshua and Matilda Dunbar, both formerly enslaved in Kentucky. Maya Angelou's I Know Why the Caged Bird Sings (1969) takes its title from "Sympathy." The blood imagery in Dunbar's second stanza may need a brief pause for discussion.

Answer key: interactive lesson

Reconnect

  1. Week 10: what is the one sentence that states the central idea of a whole informational article called?
    Answer: the thesis
    The thesis states the main idea every section supports.
  2. An informational article and a poem can both be about the ocean. How are their purposes different?
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Article: explain and inform with facts; Poem: express feelings, create images, share a perspective through language
    Model answer: An informational article explains facts about the ocean. A poem uses images and feelings to show how the ocean seems or what it means to the speaker.
  3. Week 3: In "Mother to Son," Langston Hughes compares life to a staircase. What is that kind of comparison called, and what does the "crystal stair" stand for?
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Extended metaphor; Crystal stair: an easy, comfortable, privileged life (which the mother has not had)
    Model answer: It is an extended metaphor. The crystal stair stands for an easy, smooth life, which the mother has never had.
  4. Week 10 root review: the root dict means "say." Using pre- (before) + dict, what does it mean to predict something?
    Answer: to say what will happen before it happens
    pre (before) + dict (say). Today: four Greek roots about language and feeling.

Learn 1: The Figurative Language Toolkit

  1. Name the figurative language in each sentence.
    Answer: metaphor; hyperbole; personification
    He IS a tornado (metaphor); a thousand times is an exaggeration (hyperbole); stars cannot wink (personification).
  2. Rewrite "The wind blew" twice: once with personification that creates a gentle tone, and once with personification that creates a frightening tone. Explain which words changed the tone.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Gentle personification (e.g. the wind hummed a lullaby); Frightening personification (e.g. the wind clawed at the windows); Explains the word choices that create each tone
    Model answer: Gentle: The wind hummed through the pines like a lullaby. Frightening: The wind howled and clawed at the windows. Hummed and lullaby feel soft and calm, while howled and clawed make the wind sound like an attacking animal.
  3. Explain the meaning of the idiom "to be on the same page." Then describe how someone learning English might misunderstand it.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Meaning: to agree or understand the same way; Literal misunderstanding: reading the same page of a book
    Model answer: It means two people agree or understand something the same way. Someone learning English might think it means reading the same page of a book.

Learn 2: Personification and Implied Metaphor: Rossetti and Sandburg

  1. In "Who Has Seen the Wind?", what do the personified leaves and trees help the reader understand about the wind, which no one can see?
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: We know the wind by its effects: trembling leaves and bowing trees; The personification makes the wind seem powerful or important, like someone the trees respect or fear
    Model answer: They show that we can know the wind only by what it does: the leaves tremble and the trees bow. Making the trees bow like people suggests the wind is powerful, like a ruler passing by.
  2. List three details Sandburg uses to turn the fog into a cat, and explain what the comparison tells you about how fog moves.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Three details: little cat feet, sits looking, silent haunches, moves on; Fog arrives silently and softly, stays for a while, and leaves on its own
    Model answer: The fog comes on little cat feet, sits looking over the harbor, rests on silent haunches and moves on. Like a cat, fog arrives quietly, stays still for a while and then leaves whenever it wants.
  3. Sandburg never says "the fog is a cat"; he only shows it with details. What is this kind of metaphor called?
    Answer: an implied metaphor
    An implied metaphor shows the comparison without stating it.

Learn 3: Extended Metaphor and Tone: Dickinson

  1. Why do you think Dickinson chose a small bird, not an eagle or a lion, to represent hope? Use details from the poem.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: A small bird is gentle and easy to overlook, yet keeps singing; it "kept so many warm"; Hope is quiet and modest but surprisingly strong; it survives storms and "never asked a crumb"
    Model answer: A small bird is quiet and easy to overlook, just like hope inside a person. But this little bird keeps singing through gales and storms and asks for nothing, so Dickinson shows that hope is gentle but surprisingly strong.
  2. Describe the tone of the poem in one or two words, and quote two words or phrases that create that tone.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: A fitting tone word (hopeful, grateful, admiring, warm, comforting); Two supporting quotations (sweetest, kept so many warm, never stops, never asked a crumb)
    Model answer: The tone is grateful and admiring. Phrases like "sweetest in the gale" and "kept so many warm" show how much the speaker values hope.
  3. What does the last line, "It asked a crumb of me," mean? Why is it a powerful ending?
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Hope never asked for anything in return; it is a free gift; It surprises the reader and shows gratitude; the bird metaphor continues to the very end (birds eat crumbs)
    Model answer: It means hope never asked for anything in return, not even a crumb, which is what you would give a bird. It is powerful because it ends the bird metaphor and shows how freely hope gives.
  4. Complete the sentences.
    Answer: bird; extended
    Hope is a small bird from the first line to the last.

Learn 4: Imagery and Symbol: Dunbar's "Sympathy"

  1. Name the device.
    Answer: simile; symbol
    like signals a simile; a concrete thing standing for a bigger idea is a symbol.
  2. Why does Dunbar fill the first stanza with images of a beautiful spring day? How does that make the caged bird's situation feel?
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: The images show the freedom and beauty the bird can see but cannot reach; The contrast makes the cage feel more painful and unfair
    Model answer: The sunny slopes, soft wind and glassy river show everything the bird can see but cannot reach. That contrast makes being trapped feel even more painful and unfair.
  3. In stanza 3, the speaker says the bird's song "is not a carol of joy or glee." What is it instead, and how does that change the meaning of a bird's song?
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: A prayer and a plea for freedom; A song we might hear as happy is really a cry of suffering and hope; outsiders misunderstand
    Model answer: It is a prayer and a plea sent up to Heaven. Something people might hear as a happy song is really a cry for freedom, which shows that others may not understand the pain of someone who is not free.
  4. The title "Sympathy" combines the roots sym and path. What does path mean?
    Answer: feeling
    path = feeling or suffering; sym = together. Sympathy: feeling together with someone.
  5. How does the tone of "Sympathy" differ from the tone of Dickinson's poem, even though both use a bird? Use one quotation from each.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Dickinson: hopeful, grateful (e.g. "sweetest in the gale"); Dunbar: painful, sorrowful, pleading, angry at injustice (e.g. "cruel bars", "a plea"); Explains how word choice creates each tone
    Model answer: Dickinson's tone is hopeful and grateful: her bird sings "sweetest in the Gale." Dunbar's tone is sorrowful and pleading: his bird beats against "the cruel bars" and sings "a plea." Dickinson's bird is free inside the soul, but Dunbar's bird is trapped, so his words are full of pain.

Hands-On

  1. Write your final poem here, with a title.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: 8 to 14 lines with a title; Extended personification of a weather event; At least one simile, one image and one hyperbole; A consistent tone created by word choice
    Model answer: The Afternoon Storm At three o'clock the storm wakes up, stretching gray arms across the sky. He clears his throat in a thunder cough that rattles every window in Arcadia. He stomps through the orange groves like a giant late for school, spilling a million buckets on the road, and the hot asphalt hisses back at him. By five he is tired. He shuffles east toward the coast, dragging his wet coat behind him, and the sun peeks out to see if he's gone.
  2. Label the figurative language in your poem (quote each example and name it). What tone did your family hear? Is it the tone you intended?
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Each required device quoted and named correctly; Reports the family's tone word and compares it with the intended tone
    Model answer: Personification: "the storm wakes up," "he stomps." Simile: "like a giant late for school." Hyperbole: "a million buckets." Imagery: "the hot asphalt hisses." My family said the tone was playful, which is what I wanted, even though storms can be scary.

Think Deeper

  1. Choose one of today's four poems. Write a paragraph explaining how its figurative language creates its tone and meaning. Include at least two quotations and explain each one.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Names the poem and a clear claim about tone and meaning; Two or more accurate quotations; Explains how each quotation's figurative language creates the tone or meaning; Organized paragraph with a concluding sentence
    Model answer: In "Sympathy," Paul Laurence Dunbar uses the symbol of a caged bird to create a tone of sorrow and longing for freedom. In the first stanza, imagery like "the river flows like a stream of glass" shows the beautiful, free world the bird can see but cannot reach. In the second stanza, the image of blood "red on the cruel bars" turns the tone painful, because the bird keeps fighting a cage it cannot escape. Finally, Dunbar reveals that the bird's song is "a prayer" and "a plea," not joy, which shows that people denied freedom still hope. Through these images, Dunbar helps readers feel what it means to be trapped by injustice.
  2. Dickinson's bird is free inside the soul, while Dunbar's bird is trapped in a cage. Write a short paragraph comparing what each bird represents, and explain which poem speaks more to you and why. End with one sentence stating a theme of either poem.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Dickinson: hope that lives inside a person and never gives up; Dunbar: a person denied freedom, pleading to be free; A personal response supported by the text; A theme statement (a full sentence, not a topic)
    Model answer: Dickinson's bird stands for hope that lives inside every person and keeps singing even in the worst storms. Dunbar's bird stands for a person who has lost their freedom and sings only to beg for it. Dunbar's poem speaks to me more, because it reminds me that some people's hope comes from deep suffering, and it makes me think about fairness. One theme of "Sympathy" is that people denied freedom still hold on to hope.

Exit Check

  1. Name the figurative language.
    Answer: personification; simile
    Waves cannot whisper (personification); as bright as the sun uses as (simile).
  2. What is tone, and how does figurative language help create it?
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Tone: the speaker's attitude toward the subject; Word choice and images (e.g. grumbled versus roared) shape the attitude the reader hears
    Model answer: Tone is the speaker's attitude toward the subject. Figurative language creates it through word choice, like a storm that grumbles sounding annoyed but one that roars sounding frightening.
  3. Which root means "over" or "beyond," as in the word for exaggeration on purpose?
    Answer: hyper
    hyper = over, beyond: hyperbole goes over the top.
  4. In "Sympathy," what does the caged bird symbolize?
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: A person whose freedom is taken away; many readers connect it to Black Americans under racism
    Model answer: It symbolizes a person whose freedom has been taken away, and many readers connect it to Black Americans facing racism in Dunbar's time.

    Answer: Details like little cat feet, sitting and looking, silent haunches, moving on (implied metaphor).
  3. In Dickinson's poem, what is hope compared to, and why?
    Answer: A small bird that sings in the soul; hope is quiet but strong and asks nothing in return.
  4. What does the caged bird symbolize in "Sympathy"?
    Answer: A person whose freedom is taken away; often read as Black Americans under racism.
  5. Explain tone and give the tone of one poem from today.
    Answer: Tone is the speaker's attitude; e.g. Dunbar: sorrowful and pleading.
  6. Use the roots: what do sympathy and empathy have in common?
    Answer: Both contain path (feeling); sympathy = feeling together with someone.
