Unit 3: The Diversity of Life · Week 11 · Life Science · Teacher Plan

Characteristics of Living Things

Lesson length: 60 minutes live, plus the after-class interactive lesson

Objectives

  • Name and explain seven characteristics shared by all living things.
  • Describe the levels of organization from cell to organism.
  • Explain homeostasis with examples from the human body.
  • Use the characteristics to classify things as living, once living or never living, and argue about hard cases.
  • Run a controlled yeast investigation and use the results as evidence that yeast is alive.

Standards

  • SC.6.L.14.1: Describe and identify patterns in the hierarchical organization of organisms from atoms to molecules to cells to tissues to organs to organ systems to organisms.
  • SC.6.L.14.3: Recognize and explore how cells of all organisms undergo similar processes to maintain homeostasis, including extracting energy from food, getting rid of waste, and reproducing.
  • SC.6.N.1.1: Define a problem from the sixth grade curriculum, use appropriate reference materials to support scientific understanding, plan and carry out scientific investigation of various types, such as systematic observations or experiments, identify variables, collect and organize data, interpret data in charts, tables, and graphics, analyze information, make predictions, and defend conclusions.
  • SC.6.N.2.3: Recognize that scientists who make contributions to scientific knowledge come from all kinds of backgrounds and possess varied talents, interests, and goals.
  • SC.6.N.1.1 (data): see above; levels of organization are previewed here and taught in depth in Unit 4.

Materials

  • The interactive lesson
  • Active dry yeast, sugar, warm and cold water, 3 plastic bottles, 3 balloons, a funnel, measuring tools, a tape measure
  • Optional: a bean seed soaked overnight, a houseplant near a window
  • Printable practice page

Vocabulary

  • organism: Any individual living thing, from a bacterium to a blue whale.
  • stimulus: A change in the environment that causes a living thing to react (plural: stimuli).
  • response: A reaction to a stimulus.
  • homeostasis: Keeping conditions inside an organism stable, such as body temperature or water level.
  • development: The changes an organism goes through as it grows, such as a tadpole becoming a frog.
  • tissue: A group of similar cells that do the same job.
  • organ: A structure made of different tissues working together, such as the heart or a leaf.
  • unicellular / multicellular: Made of one cell / made of many cells.

Key questions to ask

  • Does it show ALL the characteristics, or only some?
  • What is the stimulus, and what is the response?
  • How does this level depend on the one below it?
  • What would change your mind?

Common mistakes to watch for

  • Anything that moves is alive (and anything that does not move is not).
  • Plants are not alive because they do not move.
  • A single organism must reproduce to be alive.
  • Viruses are tiny bacteria.
  • Seeds are dead until planted.

Support (if the student is struggling)

  • Give a checklist card with the seven characteristics to test each case.
  • Have the student invent their own memory sentence for the seven characteristics.
  • Pre-label the yeast bottles and a data table.

Challenge (if the student is ready for more)

  • Research tardigrades' survival records (heat, cold, radiation, space) and evaluate which claims come from peer-reviewed studies.
  • Repeat the yeast test with honey, salt or flour instead of sugar and explain the results.

Background for the teacher

Lists of life's characteristics vary by textbook (six to eight items); this lesson uses seven plus adaptation at the population level. Tardigrades: in 2016, Tsujimoto and colleagues (Cryobiology) reported reviving tardigrades from Antarctic moss collected in 1983 and stored frozen for 30.5 years; one laid eggs that hatched. Yeast (Saccharomyces cerevisiae) ferments sugar and releases carbon dioxide; warm water near 105 to 110°F works well, and water above about 130°F kills yeast. Jagadish Chandra Bose (1858 to 1937), physicist and plant physiologist in Kolkata, invented the crescograph (about 1919) to magnify plant growth and studied electrical responses in plants (Response in the Living and Non-Living, 1902). Charles Henry Turner was taught in Week 1 and is not repeated here. Normal body temperature averages about 98.6°F (37°C), with modern studies finding slightly lower averages. Viruses are generally not considered living; some biologists argue otherwise.

Answer key: interactive lesson

Reconnect

  1. In your own words, why does cell theory matter for deciding whether something is alive?
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Cell theory says all living things are made of cells, so being made of cells is a test for life
    Model answer: Cell theory says all living things are made of cells, so if something is not made of cells, that is a strong sign it is not alive.
  2. Which organelle releases energy from glucose for a cell?
    Answer: mitochondria
    Mitochondria. Using energy is one of today's characteristics of life.
  3. Week 10: Lynn Margulis argued that mitochondria were once free-living bacteria. Name one piece of evidence (one or two words is fine).
    Answer: their own DNA (or: they divide like bacteria; two membranes; bacteria-like ribosomes)
    Own DNA, dividing like bacteria, bacteria-like ribosomes and two membranes all point to a bacterial origin.
  4. Your cell model showed one process. Which process did you show, and what does it tell you about what cells do to stay alive?
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Names a process (photosynthesis, active transport, osmosis); Connects it to a life need: making or using energy, taking in materials
    Model answer: I showed active transport. It shows that cells spend energy to bring in the materials they need to stay alive.

Learn 1: Seven Characteristics of Life

  1. A dry bean seed sits in a jar for a year without changing. Is it alive? Use at least two characteristics to explain.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Alive (dormant); Made of cells; when given water it will use stored energy, grow and develop into a plant (and later reproduce)
    Model answer: Yes, it is alive but dormant. It is made of cells, and when it gets water it will use its stored energy to grow and develop into a bean plant that can reproduce.
  2. Mules almost never have babies. Does that mean a mule is not alive? Explain.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: A mule is alive; It shows the other characteristics (cells, energy, growth, response, homeostasis); reproduction applies to living things as a kind, and a single organism does not have to reproduce
    Model answer: A mule is alive. It is made of cells, uses energy, grows, responds and keeps homeostasis. Not every single organism has to reproduce for it to be alive.
  3. What is the word for a change in the environment, such as light or heat, that causes a living thing to react?
    Answer: stimulus
    A stimulus causes a response.

Learn 2: Levels of Organization

  1. Put the levels in order from smallest to largest.
    Answer: cell → tissue → organ → organ system → organism
    Cells form tissues, tissues form organs, organs form organ systems, and systems form the organism.
  2. A leaf is made of several kinds of tissue working together. Which level of organization is a leaf?
    Answer: an organ
    A leaf is an organ: different tissues working together to do photosynthesis.
  3. A bacterium is a single cell. Which levels of organization does it have, and which does it not have? Is it still organized?
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: It is one cell, which is also the whole organism; no tissues, organs or organ systems; Still organized: its cell parts work together
    Model answer: A bacterium is both a cell and an organism, but it has no tissues, organs or organ systems. It is still organized, because the parts inside its single cell work together.

Learn 3: Homeostasis and Responding to the World

  1. Explain how sweating on a hot day is an example of both responding to a stimulus and maintaining homeostasis.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Stimulus: body temperature rising; response: sweating; Evaporating sweat cools the body, keeping temperature stable (homeostasis)
    Model answer: Rising body temperature is the stimulus, and sweating is the response. When the sweat evaporates, it cools the skin, which keeps the body's temperature steady. That is homeostasis.
  2. Bose's crescograph recorded a plant getting bigger, and its growth slowing when the plant was touched or heated.
    Answer: grow; respond
    Growth and response are two characteristics of life.
  3. What is the word for keeping conditions inside an organism stable?
    Answer: homeostasis
    Homeostasis: homeo (same) + stasis (standing still).

Learn 4: Hard Cases: Viruses, Fire and Water Bears

  1. A classmate says, "Fire is alive because it grows, eats fuel and has babies when sparks start new fires." Respond using at least two characteristics of life.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Fire is not alive; Not made of cells; no organization; cannot maintain homeostasis; its "growth" and "spreading" are a chemical reaction, not reproduction
    Model answer: Fire looks alive, but it is not. It is not made of cells, it has no organization, and it cannot keep a stable inside. Its "growth" and "babies" are just a chemical reaction spreading to new fuel, not reproduction.
  2. Give one reason a biologist might say viruses are not alive and one reason another biologist might say they are on the edge of life.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Not alive: no cells; cannot use energy or reproduce on their own; Edge of life: they carry genetic material, reproduce (inside cells) and change over time
    Model answer: Viruses are not made of cells and cannot use energy or reproduce on their own. But they carry genetic material, make copies of themselves inside cells and change over time, so some biologists say they are on the edge of life.
  3. Sort each into living, once living or never living.
    Answer: once living; living; never living; living; once living; never living
    The chair and T-shirt came from plants that were alive; the mushroom and houseplant are alive; the rock and robot were never alive.
  4. Explain one choice from the sort that others might get wrong.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Explains a tricky case (robot uses energy and responds but has no cells; cotton came from a plant)
    Model answer: Someone might call the robot vacuum alive because it uses energy and responds to walls, but it has no cells and cannot grow or reproduce.

Hands-On

  1. Record a data table: time, and balloon size for A, B and C. Describe the pattern.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Complete table for three bottles at four times; Clear description: A grew most; B little; C slowly or little
    Model answer: At 40 minutes: A 32 cm around, B 12 cm, C 15 cm. Balloon A inflated the most, C grew slowly, and B barely changed.
  2. Write a CER: Is yeast alive? Use your data, and name the independent variables you tested and one variable you kept the same.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Claim: yeast is alive; Evidence: specific measurements showing gas made with sugar and warmth; Reasoning: yeast used sugar for energy and gave off carbon dioxide as waste; it responded to temperature; uses at least one characteristic of life; Variables: sugar (A versus B) and temperature (A versus C); controlled: amount of yeast, water, bottle size
    Model answer: Claim: Yeast is alive. Evidence: The balloon on bottle A, with sugar and warm water, grew to 32 cm around in 40 minutes, while bottle B (no sugar) stayed at 12 cm and bottle C (cold water) reached only 15 cm. Reasoning: The yeast used the sugar as food for energy and gave off carbon dioxide gas as waste, which filled the balloon. It also responded to temperature, working faster when warm. Using energy and responding are characteristics of life. I tested sugar (A versus B) and temperature (A versus C) and kept the yeast, the amount of water and the bottle size the same.

Think Deeper

  1. Return to the big question. Was the frozen tardigrade alive during its 30 years in the freezer? Write a paragraph that takes a position and supports it with at least three characteristics of life, and address one argument on the other side.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Clear position; Uses at least three characteristics accurately (cells, organization, growth, reproduction after revival, response, energy use slowed, homeostasis); Addresses a counterargument; Organized paragraph
    Model answer: I think the frozen tardigrade was alive the whole time, in a state like a dormant seed. It was still made of cells and its body was still organized, which is why it could start again. After it thawed, it moved, responded to its surroundings and even laid eggs, so it could grow and reproduce. Someone could argue it was not alive because it was not using energy or responding while frozen. But its life processes had only slowed almost to a stop, not ended, so it was alive in suspended animation, not dead.
  2. Engineers are building robots that can repair themselves and learn. Imagine a future robot that uses energy, responds to its surroundings and builds copies of itself. Would it be alive? Use the characteristics of life to argue your answer.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Takes a position; Uses characteristics accurately (cells, growth and development, homeostasis); Thoughtful reasoning about what makes life different
    Model answer: Even if it used energy, responded and built copies of itself, I would say the robot is not alive, because it is not made of cells and does not grow and develop from within or keep homeostasis the way organisms do. It would be a very advanced machine. But if scientists ever built a machine that met every characteristic, we might need to rethink our definition of life.

Exit Check

  1. List the seven characteristics of living things.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Made of cells; organized; use energy; grow and develop; respond to stimuli; reproduce; maintain homeostasis
    Model answer: Made of cells, organized, use energy, grow and develop, respond to stimuli, reproduce, and maintain homeostasis.
  2. A group of similar cells that do the same job is called a what?
    Answer: a tissue
    A tissue, such as muscle tissue.
  3. When you shiver, your muscles twitch quickly to make what, keeping your body temperature stable?
    Answer: heat
    Shivering makes heat.
  4. Why do most biologists say a virus is not alive?
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Not made of cells; cannot use energy or reproduce on its own
    Model answer: It is not made of cells and cannot use energy or reproduce without a living cell.

Answer key: printable page

  1. List the seven characteristics of living things.
    Answer: Cells; organized; use energy; grow and develop; respond to stimuli; reproduce; maintain homeostasis.
  2. Put in order: organ, cell, organism, tissue, organ system.
    Answer: cell, tissue, organ, organ system, organism
  3. Give an example of a stimulus and a response in a plant.
    Answer: Light from a window (stimulus); the plant bends toward it (response).
  4. Explain homeostasis using sweating or shivering.
    Answer: Sweating cools the body when hot; shivering warms it when cold; both keep temperature stable.
  5. Is fire alive? Explain.
    Answer: No; it is not made of cells, has no organization and cannot maintain homeostasis.
  6. In the yeast test, why did the balloon with sugar and warm water grow most?
    Answer: The yeast used the sugar for energy and gave off carbon dioxide gas; warmth made it work faster.
