Unit 3: Ancient Asia · Week 11 · World and American History · Teacher Plan

Mesopotamia and the First Writing

Lesson length: 60 minutes live, plus the after-class interactive lesson

Objectives

  • Locate Mesopotamia, the Tigris and Euphrates rivers and the cities of Sumer on a map.
  • Compare how Mesopotamians and Egyptians lived with their rivers.
  • Explain how writing developed in Mesopotamia from record keeping to literature.
  • Describe the contributions of Enheduanna, Hammurabi and the Epic of Gilgamesh.
  • Write numbers in cuneiform and explain Mesopotamia's base-60 legacy.

Standards

  • SS.6.W.2.4: Compare the economic, political, social, and religious institutions of ancient river civilizations.
  • SS.6.W.2.7: Summarize the important achievements of Mesopotamian civilization.
  • SS.6.W.2.8: Determine the impact of key figures from ancient Mesopotamian civilizations.
  • SS.6.G.2.1: Explain how major physical characteristics, natural resources, climate, and absolute and relative locations have influenced settlement, interactions, and the economies of ancient civilizations of the world.
  • SS.6.W.1.3: Interpret primary and secondary sources.

Materials

  • The interactive lesson
  • A world map or globe
  • Air-dry clay or play dough, a chopstick or popsicle stick with a triangular end, a rolling pin or glass
  • Printable practice page

Vocabulary

  • Mesopotamia: Greek for "land between the rivers"; the region of the Tigris and Euphrates, mostly in today's Iraq.
  • Sumer: The southern part of Mesopotamia, home to some of the world's earliest cities.
  • city-state: A city and the farmland around it with its own ruler and government.
  • cuneiform: Writing made of wedge-shaped marks pressed into clay; from the Latin cuneus, "wedge."
  • scribe: A person trained to read and write, who kept records.
  • ziggurat: A stepped temple tower built of mud brick at the center of a Mesopotamian city.
  • code of law: A written set of laws that applies to everyone in a society.
  • base 60: A number system that counts in groups of 60.

Key questions to ask

  • Why did people need writing?
  • How did the rivers shape the way people lived?
  • Were Hammurabi's laws fair? Fair to whom?
  • Where do you see Mesopotamia in your own life?

Common mistakes to watch for

  • Writing was invented to tell stories (it began with record keeping).
  • Hammurabi's code was the first law code (Ur-Nammu's is older).
  • Mesopotamia and Egypt were the same kind of civilization.
  • Mesopotamia is a modern country (it is a region, mostly in Iraq).

Support (if the student is struggling)

  • Provide a labeled map to keep.
  • Give a cuneiform number chart for 1 to 59.
  • Use a T-chart to compare the Nile and the Tigris and Euphrates.

Challenge (if the student is ready for more)

  • Read a short translated passage from the Epic of Gilgamesh (the flood story) and compare it with another flood story you know.
  • Write 1 minute 25 seconds as seconds, then convert 85 seconds to minutes and seconds, and explain how base 60 helps.

Background for the teacher

Proto-cuneiform tablets appear at Uruk (Uruk IV and III levels) about 3300 to 3000 BCE, chiefly administrative. Schmandt-Besserat's token theory (Before Writing, 1992) is influential but debated. Egyptian writing at Abydos tomb U-j dates to about 3250 BCE; independent invention is the mainstream view. Cuneiform was used until about the 1st century CE. Enheduanna (about 2285 to 2250 BCE), daughter of Sargon of Akkad, was en-priestess of Nanna at Ur. Hammurabi's stele (Louvre Sb 8, about 1754 BCE) has 282 laws by the conventional count; penalties vary by class (awilum, mushkenum, wardum). Ur-Nammu's code dates to about 2100 to 2050 BCE. The Gilgamesh texts include Old Babylonian versions (about 1800 BCE) and the Standard Babylonian version found at Nineveh. Babylonian numerals: vertical wedge 1, Winkelhaken 10, place value base 60.

Answer key: interactive lesson

Reconnect

  1. Which Egyptian hieroglyph became the letter A: an ox head, a reed or an eye?
    Answer: an ox head
    The ox-head sign became Proto-Sinaitic 'alp, Phoenician aleph, Greek alpha and Latin A.
  2. Kemet's civil calendar had 12 months of 30 days. How many days were in those 12 months, before the 5 extra days?
    Answer: 360 days
    12 × 30 = 360. Today you will meet another people who loved the numbers 60 and 360.
  3. In Week 6, you learned that the Nile flooded at about the same time every year. Why was that regular, predictable flood helpful to Kemet's farmers?
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Farmers could plan planting and harvest; the flood brought water and silt on schedule
    Model answer: Farmers knew when the water and fresh silt would come, so they could plan when to plant and harvest.
  4. In your own words, what is cultural diffusion? Give an example from Unit 2.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Ideas spreading between cultures and being adapted; An accurate example (the alphabet; Kush borrowing Egyptian gods)
    Model answer: Cultural diffusion is when ideas spread from one culture to another and change along the way, like the alphabet moving from Egypt to the Phoenicians and Greeks.

Learn 1: Two Rivers, Two Ways of Life

  1. Compare the floods of the Nile with the floods of the Tigris and Euphrates. How did each shape the way people farmed and lived?
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Nile: regular, predictable yearly flood that farmers could plan around; Tigris and Euphrates: unpredictable, often violent spring floods; Mesopotamians built and repaired canals and levees, which required cooperation and helped create cities
    Model answer: The Nile flooded gently at about the same time every year, so Egyptian farmers could plan around it. The Tigris and Euphrates flooded unpredictably and sometimes violently, so Mesopotamian farmers had to build and constantly repair canals and levees. That shared work brought people together and helped create cities.
  2. Mesopotamia had plenty of mud and reeds but little stone, timber or metal. How did that shortage lead to trade, and what did Mesopotamians build with instead?
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Traded grain and cloth for copper, tin, timber and stone from distant lands; Built with mud brick and reeds (ziggurats, houses); wrote on clay
    Model answer: Because they lacked stone, timber and metal, they traded their extra grain and cloth for those goods from faraway places. At home they built with mud brick and reeds, even their giant ziggurats, and they wrote on clay.
  3. What does the Greek name Mesopotamia mean?
    Answer: land between the rivers
    meso (middle, between) + potamos (river).

Learn 2: How Writing Began

  1. Why did writing begin in Mesopotamia? What does that tell you about what early cities needed?
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: To keep records of goods (grain, beer, sheep, cloth) collected and handed out by temples; Cities had large economies and many people to organize, so they needed records
    Model answer: Writing began as a way to keep records of grain, beer, sheep and cloth that temples collected and gave out. That shows early cities had so many people and goods that no one could remember everything, so they needed a reliable way to keep track.
  2. Who invented writing first, Mesopotamia or Egypt? Explain why historians are careful with this question.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Both developed writing at about the same time (about 3300 to 3200 BCE); the evidence is close; Dating is uncertain and new finds can change the picture; most think they were invented independently
    Model answer: Historians are not sure, because the earliest writing from both places dates to about the same time, and dates for clay tablets and labels are uncertain. A new discovery could change the answer. Most scholars think each invented writing on its own.
  3. From which Latin word, meaning "wedge," does cuneiform get its name?
    Answer: cuneus
    Cuneus means wedge: cuneiform is "wedge-shaped" writing.

Learn 3: Voices in Clay: Enheduanna, Gilgamesh and Hammurabi

  1. Why is Enheduanna important in world history? What does her story tell us about women in early Mesopotamia?
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Earliest known named author; wrote hymns to Inanna; high priestess at Ur; At least some elite women held religious power and were educated writers
    Model answer: Enheduanna is the earliest author whose name we know. As high priestess at Ur, she wrote hymns to Inanna, which shows that at least some royal women in Mesopotamia were educated and held real religious power.
  2. Read Laws 196, 198 and 199. What happens to the attacker in each case, and what do the three laws show about how Babylon treated people of different classes?
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: 196: eye for an eye (equal noble); 198: pays one gold mina (commoner victim); 199: pays half the slave's value (to the owner); The punishment depended on the victim's class; enslaved people were treated as property
    Model answer: If the victim was a noble, the attacker lost his own eye. If the victim was a commoner, the attacker paid one gold mina. If the victim was enslaved, the attacker paid half the slave's value, as if the person were property. The laws show that a person's class decided how much the law protected them.
  3. Writing laws down and displaying them publicly was a big change. Give one way this made life fairer and one way Hammurabi's laws were still unequal.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Fairer: people could know the laws; judges could not simply make up rules; same law for everyone in a class; Unequal: punishments depended on social class (noble, commoner, enslaved)
    Model answer: Because the laws were written and displayed, people could know the rules ahead of time, and judges could not just invent them. But the laws were still unequal: the punishment for the same crime depended on whether the victim was a noble, a commoner or an enslaved person.
  4. Gilgamesh searches for a way to live forever and fails, but comes home wiser. What theme might this ancient story share with stories today?
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: A theme statement (not a topic): e.g. accepting that life ends and making it meaningful; friendship and loss; wisdom comes from struggle
    Model answer: The theme might be that no one can escape death, so what matters is how we live and the good we leave behind. Many modern stories share that idea.

Learn 4: Counting by 60

  1. How many corner wedges (tens) and vertical wedges (ones) does it take to write 47? Type the total number of wedges.
    Answer: 11 wedges
    47 = 4 tens + 7 ones: 4 corner wedges and 7 vertical wedges, 11 wedges in all.
  2. A scribe writes one wedge in the 60s place and 2 tens and 5 ones in the ones place. What number is it?
    Answer: 85
    1 × 60 + 25 = 85.
  3. Explain why 60 is a useful number for dividing things fairly, using an example of sharing 60 loaves of bread.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: 60 has many factors (2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 10, 12, 15, 20, 30); Example: 60 loaves can be shared evenly among 2, 3, 4, 5 or 6 people
    Model answer: 60 has many factors, so 60 loaves can be shared evenly among 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 10 or 12 families without breaking a loaf. With 10 loaves, only 2, 5 or 10 families could share evenly.

Hands-On

  1. What three goods and amounts did you record? Draw or describe how you wrote each number in wedges.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Three goods with amounts; Correct tens and ones wedges for each number
    Model answer: Eggs, 24: two corner wedges and four vertical wedges. Apples, 13: one corner wedge and three vertical wedges. Bread, 6: six vertical wedges.
  2. What was hard about writing in clay? Why do you think scribes trained for years, and why might writing have given scribes power?
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: A real difficulty (clay dries, wedges smudge, small space, signs must be precise); Writing took years to learn; few people could read, so scribes controlled records, laws and messages
    Model answer: The wedges smudged when I touched the clay, and it was hard to fit everything. Scribes needed years of school to learn hundreds of signs. Since very few people could read, scribes controlled the records, letters and laws, which gave them power and respect.

Think Deeper

  1. Write a paragraph explaining how writing changed Mesopotamian society. Include at least three different uses of writing from the lesson and explain why each mattered.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Three accurate uses (record keeping, laws, literature, religion, letters, science); Explains why each mattered; Organized paragraph with a topic sentence and transitions
    Model answer: Writing changed Mesopotamia from a world that depended on memory into one that could keep records forever. First, scribes kept track of grain, beer and sheep, which helped temples and cities organize thousands of people. Next, rulers like Hammurabi wrote laws on public pillars, so people could know the rules and judges had a standard to follow. Writing also preserved literature and religion, from Enheduanna's hymns to the Epic of Gilgamesh, so ideas could pass from one generation to the next. Because of writing, knowledge no longer disappeared when a person died.
  2. Compare Mesopotamia and Kemet. Give two similarities and two differences, using evidence from Unit 2 and this lesson.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Two accurate similarities (river valleys, writing around the same time, temples and kings, farming with irrigation); Two accurate differences (flood patterns, building materials, many city-states versus a unified kingdom, natural resources); Specific evidence
    Model answer: Both civilizations grew along rivers in dry lands, and both developed writing around 3200 BCE. However, the Nile flooded predictably, while the Tigris and Euphrates flooded unpredictably and needed constant canal work. Kemet was united under one king around 3100 BCE, while Sumer was made up of rival city-states. Kemet also had good stone for pyramids, while Mesopotamia built with mud brick.

Exit Check

  1. Name the two rivers of Mesopotamia.
    Answer: the Tigris and the Euphrates
    The Tigris and the Euphrates.
  2. What was the first purpose of writing in Mesopotamia?
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: Keeping records of goods (grain, beer, sheep, cloth)
    Model answer: To keep records of goods like grain, beer and sheep.
  3. Who is often called the earliest author in history known by name?
    Answer: Enheduanna
    Enheduanna, high priestess at Ur, about 2300 BCE.
  4. Name one way you still use the Mesopotamian base-60 system today.
    Answer: Written response: score with the rubric.
    Rubric: 60 seconds in a minute, 60 minutes in an hour, or 360 degrees in a circle
    Model answer: There are 60 minutes in an hour.

Answer key: printable page

  1. Sketch Mesopotamia: draw the Tigris and Euphrates and the Persian Gulf, and label Ur, Uruk and Babylon.
    Answer: Two rivers flowing southeast into the Persian Gulf; Ur and Uruk in the south (Sumer); Babylon farther north on the Euphrates.
  2. How were the floods of the Tigris and Euphrates different from the Nile's?
    Answer: Unpredictable and often violent, so farmers built and repaired canals and levees.
  3. Explain how writing developed in Mesopotamia.
    Answer: From counting tokens to pictures on clay tablets (about 3300 to 3200 BCE) to wedge-shaped cuneiform.
  4. Who was Enheduanna?
    Answer: High priestess at Ur, daughter of Sargon, the earliest known named author.
  5. Give one fair and one unfair feature of Hammurabi's code.
    Answer: Fair: written and public. Unfair: punishments depended on social class.
  6. Write 34 in cuneiform (describe the wedges).
    Answer: 3 corner wedges (30) and 4 vertical wedges (4).
