🛶 Long Ago: Trails and Canoes

Long before cars, people walked and paddled. Indigenous Peoples made trails across the land and knew the rivers well. In Week 4, you learned that the Chickasaw and Choctaw used a path now called the Natchez Trace. Many roads today follow old Indigenous trails.

👁 Watch Me Think

How do you carry a boat? In the Great Lakes area, Ojibwe people built canoes from birch bark, tree roots and tree resin. These canoes were light. People could carry them around rocks and rapids. That is called a portage. Canoes carried people and goods, and people used them to gather wild rice.

🤝 Let's Try Together: Then and Now

Then: paddlers carried people and goods across lakes and rivers. Now: trucks carry goods on highways, and some highways follow old trails. What stayed the same? People still need to move people and goods!

⛵ The Erie Canal, 1825

In 1817, workers began digging the Erie Canal in New York State. It opened in 1825. It ran 363 miles, from Albany on the Hudson River to Buffalo on Lake Erie. Now boats could carry goods by water between the Great Lakes and New York City.

👁 Watch Me Think

Who did the hard work? Thousands of workers dug with picks and shovels. At first, most were local farmers. Later, many were new immigrants from Ireland. The work was dangerous, and many workers got sick. Horses and mules walked on a path beside the canal, called the towpath, and pulled the boats.

🤝 Let's Try Together: Cause and Effect

Cause: The canal made shipping goods easier. Effect: Towns along the canal grew fast. In 1815, Rochester had about 300 people. By 1830, it had almost 10,000!

🚂 The Transcontinental Railroad, 1869

A transcontinental railroad goes all the way across a continent. Two companies built it. The Central Pacific built east from Sacramento, California. The Union Pacific built west from Omaha, Nebraska. On May 10, 1869, the tracks met at Promontory Summit, Utah. A trip from New York to San Francisco went from months to about one week!

👁 Watch Me Think

Whose work built it? Chinese immigrants did most of the Central Pacific's work, about 12,000 workers at the busiest time. They cut tunnels through the tall Sierra Nevada mountains. They were paid less than white workers. In June 1867, Chinese workers went on strike for fair pay and shorter workdays. Irish immigrants, men who had been Civil War soldiers, and Mormon workers from Utah built much of the Union Pacific.

🤝 Let's Try Together: Who Paid the Cost?

The railroad crossed the homelands of many Indigenous nations, like the Lakota, Cheyenne and Shoshone. Sometimes the railroad broke treaties, or promises, that the United States had made with them. The railroad also brought hunters, who killed huge numbers of bison. Many nations needed bison for food and shelter. A historian asks: who gained, and who lost?

🕵️ Source Detective: A Photo and a Message from 1869

Use your Week 16 skills. Here are two primary sources from the same day, May 10, 1869.

Primary Sources: A Photograph and a Telegraph Message

Source 1: A photograph

Andrew J. Russell took this famous photo at Promontory Summit. Two trains stand nose to nose. In the middle, two railroad leaders shake hands. Crowds of men stand around them and climb on the engines. The National Park Service gives its title as:

"East and West Shaking Hands at Laying of Last Rail"

Source 2: A telegraph message

That same day, railroad leaders sent this message by telegraph to newspapers across the country:

"The last rail is laid. The last spike is driven. The Pacific railroad is completed."

Word help: telegraph = a way to send messages fast over wires, using electricity. laid = put in place. spike = a big nail that holds a rail down.

Andrew J. Russell, "East and West Shaking Hands at Laying of Last Rail," photograph, Promontory Summit, Utah, May 10, 1869 (title as given by the National Park Service). Telegraph message to the Associated Press from Promontory Summit, signed by Leland Stanford and Union Pacific officials, May 10, 1869.

👁 Think Like a Historian

Both sources were made that day, so both are primary. The photo shows who was there. The message tells what happened. But I still ask: whose story is this?

🕵️ Source Detective, Part 2: Who Is Missing?

Now look again, like a historian.

More Evidence About May 10, 1869

The telegraph message does not name any workers. In the famous photo, it is very hard to find any Chinese workers. Researchers have found only two. But that same day, a team of Chinese workers brought up the last rail.

For many years, their part in the story was left out. In 2014, the United States Department of Labor added the Chinese railroad workers to its Hall of Honor.

National Park Service, Golden Spike National Historical Park; Stanford University, Chinese Railroad Workers in North America Project; United States Department of Labor Hall of Honor (2014).

👁 Think Like a Historian

Whose story does the photo tell? The leaders' story. Who is almost missing? The Chinese workers who did so much of the work. One source is never the whole story, so historians look for more evidence.

✈️ Taking to the Sky, and Building the Rails

Meet people who changed how Americans get around. As you read, ask: What problem did they face, and what did they do?

Wilbur and Orville Wright

Wilbur 1867–1912 • Orville 1871–1948

The brothers ran a bicycle shop in Dayton, Ohio. They tested kites and gliders. On December 17, 1903, near Kitty Hawk, North Carolina, Orville flew their airplane for 12 seconds and 120 feet. They made four flights that day. Wilbur's last one went 852 feet in 59 seconds. John Daniels, a man from a nearby lifesaving station, took the famous photo.

Bessie Coleman

1892–1926

She was born in Atlanta, Texas. She was African American, and her father may have had Cherokee or Choctaw grandparents. She worked as a manicurist in Chicago. No one in the United States would teach a Black woman to fly. So she went to France. On June 15, 1921, she earned her pilot's license, the first African American woman to do so. Back home, she flew in air shows. She dreamed of a flying school for Black students. She died in a plane accident in 1926.

The traqueros

about 1870–1930

Traqueros were Mexican and Mexican American track workers. They built and fixed thousands of miles of railroad tracks in the Southwest and Midwest. Around Los Angeles, they built and fixed many streetcar lines.
