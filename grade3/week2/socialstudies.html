🏛️ Three Levels of Leaders

A government makes laws and runs services like schools, parks and roads. Your town has a mayor and a council. Your state has a governor. The whole country has a president and Congress.

👁 Watch Me Think

I picture boxes inside boxes. The smallest box is my town. The mayor and council fix streets and run parks. The middle box is my state. The governor and state lawmakers take care of state roads and state parks. The biggest box is the whole country. The president and Congress work in Washington, D.C. Each box has leaders for its own size of jobs.

🗳️ How Voting Works

In the United States, people choose many leaders by voting. Each voter marks a ballot. Voting is secret, so no one has to show their choice. Usually the person with the most votes wins.

👁 Watch Me Think

Let me read the results for Pretend Town. Ms. Lopez got 540 votes. Mr. Brooks got 610 votes. Dr. Kim got 120 votes. I compare: 610 is the biggest number. So Mr. Brooks wins. Then I ask one more question: did everyone who wanted to vote get to vote? A fair election counts every vote AND lets every voter take part.

📜 Who Could Vote? Then and Now

Long ago, voting was NOT fair to everyone. At first, mostly white men who owned land could vote. Over many years, people worked hard to change unfair laws. Today, most citizens age 18 and older can vote.

👁 Watch Me Think

I read a timeline like a detective. I ask: who could vote, and who was left out? In 1870, a new law said Black men could vote. But many were blocked by unfair tests, taxes and threats. So a law on paper is not the whole story. I always check what really happened.

🧩 Picture It: Voting Timeline

  • At first (1700s): Mostly white men who owned land could vote.
  • 1870: The 15th Amendment says Black men can vote. Many are still blocked by unfair tests and threats.
  • 1920: The 19th Amendment: women win the right to vote. Many Black women are still blocked.
  • 1924: A law makes Indigenous Peoples born in the U.S. citizens. Some states still block them from voting for years.
  • 1943: A law finally lets Chinese immigrants become citizens. Then they can vote.
  • 1965: The Voting Rights Act helps protect the vote for Black Americans and others.
  • 1971: The 26th Amendment: the voting age drops from 21 to 18.

An amendment is a change to the U.S. Constitution, our country's highest set of laws.

🤝 Let's Try Together: Who Was Left Out?

Look at 1870. Black men were promised the vote. But some state leaders made unfair tests and taxes to stop them. It took until 1965 for a strong law to help. That is 95 years! Brave people, like Fannie Lou Hamer, worked hard to change it.

🌟 Leaders Who Opened Doors

These people led, voted, or fought for the right to vote. Some were the first to do something big.

Shirley Chisholm

1924–2005

In 1968, Shirley Chisholm became the first Black woman elected to Congress. She served New York for 14 years. In 1972 she ran for president. She was the first Black woman to try for a major party's nomination. Her motto was "Unbought and Unbossed."

Carl Stokes

1927–1996

In 1967, Carl Stokes was elected mayor of Cleveland, Ohio. He was one of the first Black mayors of a big U.S. city. He worked on housing and jobs for the people of his city.

Patsy Mink

1927–2002

Patsy Mink was a Japanese American leader from Hawaii. In 1964 she became the first woman of color elected to Congress. She helped write a law that gave girls a fair chance in school sports and classes.

Fannie Lou Hamer

1917–1977

Fannie Lou Hamer was a Black farm worker in Mississippi. In 1962 she tried to register to vote and lost her job for it. She kept working so Black citizens could vote. Her work helped lead to the Voting Rights Act of 1965.

Susan B. Anthony

1820–1906

Susan B. Anthony worked for women's right to vote. In 1872 she voted anyway and was arrested. She died before women won the vote in 1920.
