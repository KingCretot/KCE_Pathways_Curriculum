🇺🇸 Our Flag Is a Symbol

A symbol is a thing that stands for an idea. The United States flag is a symbol of our country. It has 13 stripes and 50 stars. Each part stands for something.

👁 Watch Me Think

I ask: what does each part stand for? There are 13 stripes. The first 13 states began as 13 colonies. So the stripes stand for them. There are 50 stars. Today there are 50 states. So each star stands for one state. When a new state joined, a star was added.

🧩 Picture It: Reading the Flag

🤝 Let's Try Together: How Do We Know?

Many people say Betsy Ross sewed the first flag. Is it true? A historian looks for proof. Betsy Ross was a real flag maker in Philadelphia. But her grandson, William Canby, first told this story in 1870, almost 100 years later. No papers from 1776 show it. So we say: it is a famous story, but we are not sure it happened.

🔔 Symbols of Liberty

Liberty means freedom. The Liberty Bell is in Philadelphia, Pennsylvania. The Statue of Liberty stands in New York Harbor. Both are symbols of freedom. But people have asked: freedom for whom?

👁 Watch Me Think

Let me read the words on the bell: "Proclaim LIBERTY throughout all the Land unto all the Inhabitants thereof." That means: tell everyone in the land they are free. But in the 1830s, millions of people were still enslaved. People working to end slavery saw this. They named it the Liberty Bell. They made it a symbol of ending slavery.

🎉 Holidays and Whose Stories They Tell

A holiday is a special day to remember or celebrate. Every holiday tells a story. A historian asks: Whose story is it? Who is missing? The answers help us understand our country better.

👁 Watch Me Think

Take July 4th. In 1776, leaders approved the Declaration of Independence. It said "all men are created equal." But many people were not free then. In 1852, Frederick Douglass gave a speech. He asked what July 4th meant to people who were still enslaved. One holiday can mean different things to different people.

🧩 Picture It: Three Holidays, Three Stories

HolidayWhenWhose story it tells
Independence DayJuly 4the colonies' freedom from Britain in 1776
JuneteenthJune 19the day in 1865 when enslaved people in Texas learned they were free
Indigenous Peoples' Daysecond Monday in Octoberthe first peoples of the Americas, long ago and today

🤝 Let's Try Together: One Day, Two Names

The second Monday in October has two names. Many places call it Columbus Day. Many Italian Americans celebrate it with pride. Columbus arrived in the Americas in 1492. Then Columbus and his men forced Taíno people to work, and many died. So many places also celebrate Indigenous Peoples' Day. It honors the nations who were already here, and still are.

📜 Source Detective: The Juneteenth Order

On June 19, 1865, a U.S. Army general gave an order in Galveston, Texas. It is called General Order No. 3. Read part of it and the timeline. Then answer.

Primary Source: General Order No. 3 (Galveston, Texas, 1865)

Part of the order:

"The people of Texas are informed that, in accordance with a proclamation from the Executive of the United States, all [enslaved people] are free."

Note: We changed the words in [brackets]. The order used an older word for enslaved people.

Word help: informed = told. Executive = the President.

Timeline

DateWhat happened
January 1, 1863President Lincoln's Emancipation Proclamation says enslaved people in the states fighting the Union are free.
April 1865The Civil War is ending. Few Union soldiers are in Texas.
June 19, 1865General Gordon Granger gives General Order No. 3 in Galveston, Texas.
December 1865The 13th Amendment ends slavery in the whole country.
June 17, 2021Juneteenth becomes a national holiday.
General Order No. 3, issued by Major General Gordon Granger, Galveston, Texas, June 19, 1865 (National Archives). Edited by KCE: the words in brackets replace the order's older word.

👁 Think Like a Historian

A historian asks: Who made this? When? Why? General Gordon Granger gave this order in Galveston, Texas, on June 19, 1865. The words in [brackets] were changed by us. They are not the exact words of the order. Next I check the timeline. The Emancipation Proclamation came in 1863. So the news came to Texas about two and a half years later.

🗣️ People Behind Our Symbols and Holidays

Meet people who gave our symbols and holidays new meaning. As you read, ask: Whose story did each person help tell?

Opal Lee

born 1926

Opal Lee grew up in Texas. When she was 12, an angry crowd forced her family out of their new home in Fort Worth. It happened on June 19, 1939. She became a teacher. At age 89, she began walking to ask leaders to make Juneteenth a national holiday. At each stop she walked 2.5 miles. The 2.5 miles stand for the 2.5 years Texas waited for the news. In 2021, she stood beside the President when Juneteenth became a holiday. She is called the Grandmother of Juneteenth. She turned 100 in 2026.

Frederick Douglass

about 1818–1895

Frederick Douglass was born enslaved in Maryland. In 1838, he escaped to freedom. He became a great speaker and writer against slavery. On July 5, 1852, he gave a famous speech in Rochester, New York. He asked what July 4th meant to people who were still enslaved.

Emma Lazarus

1849–1887

Emma Lazarus was a Jewish American poet from New York City. She helped Jewish immigrants who came from Russia. In 1883, she wrote a poem about the Statue of Liberty. It calls the statue the Mother of Exiles. In 1903, her poem was put on a plaque inside the statue's base.

Indigenous Peoples' Day organizers

1990–1992

In 1990, Indigenous leaders from across the Americas met in Quito, Ecuador. Soon it would be 500 years since 1492. They asked: why tell only Columbus's story? In 1992, the city of Berkeley, California, became one of the first places to celebrate Indigenous Peoples' Day. Today many states and cities celebrate it.
