🧊 Three States of Matter

Matter is anything that takes up space. Matter comes in three main states. A solid keeps its own shape. A liquid flows and takes the shape of its cup. A gas spreads out to fill all the space it is in.

👁 Watch Me Think

I want to sort a block, juice, and air. First I ask: does it keep its shape? The block does, so it is a solid. Does it flow and take the shape of its cup? Juice does, so it is a liquid. Does it spread out everywhere? Air does, so it is a gas.

🧩 Picture It: How the Three States Act

StateShapeExamples
Solidkeeps its own shaperock, ice cube, pencil
Liquidtakes the shape of its containerwater, milk, juice
Gasspreads out to fill all the spaceair, steam, the air in a balloon

All matter is made of tiny bits, too small to see. In a solid, the bits are packed tight. In a liquid, they slide past each other. In a gas, they zoom far apart.

🤝 Let's Try Together: Let's Try One Together

Pour milk from a tall glass into a flat bowl. Did the milk change shape? Yes. It took the shape of the bowl. Did the amount of milk change? No. So milk is a liquid. Now put a toy block in the bowl. It stays a cube. The block is a solid.

🔥 Heating Changes Matter

Heating can change matter from one state to another. When ice warms up, it melts into liquid water. Water melts and freezes at 32°F. When liquid water gets hot, it turns into a gas called water vapor. At 212°F, water boils and turns to vapor fast.

👁 Watch Me Think

I leave a chocolate bar in a hot car. Later it is soft and runny. Why? The heat made the solid chocolate melt. It turned into a liquid. The chocolate is still chocolate. Only its state changed.

🧩 Picture It: Water's Temperature Ladder

TemperatureWhat happens to water
212°FWater boils. Bubbles of water vapor rise fast.
Between 33°F and 211°FWater is a liquid. Some slowly evaporates into the air.
32°FIce melts if it warms. Water freezes if it cools.
Below 32°FWater stays a solid: ice.

Safety: Boiling water and steam can burn you. Only a grown-up should handle them.

🤝 Let's Try Together: Let's Try One Together

A pot of water sits on a hot stove. Small bubbles appear. Then big bubbles roll up fast. What is in the big bubbles? Water vapor, a gas. The heat turned liquid water into a gas. The water is boiling. If we waited long enough, the pot would boil dry.

❄️ Cooling Changes Matter

Cooling changes matter too. When liquid water gets colder than 32°F, it freezes into ice. When water vapor cools, it turns back into liquid drops. This is condensation. Many changes can go back and forth. Some changes cannot be undone. A cooked egg cannot turn raw again!

👁 Watch Me Think

On a hot day, I hold a glass of ice water. The outside of the glass gets wet. Did water leak through the glass? No, the glass has no holes. The air has water vapor in it. The cold glass cooled the vapor. It turned into tiny liquid drops. That is condensation.

🧩 Picture It: Back and Forth with Water

Ice (solid) → heat it: melting → liquid water → heat it more: evaporation or boiling → water vapor (gas)

Water vapor (gas) → cool it: condensation → liquid water → cool it more: freezing → ice (solid)

Can go backCannot go back
ice melting, then freezing againcooking an egg
chocolate melting, then getting hardburning toast

🤝 Let's Try Together: Let's Try One Together

Pour juice into an ice pop mold. Put it in the freezer. The next day it is hard. The juice froze. It went from liquid to solid. Now leave the pop in the sun. It melts back into juice. This change can go back, so it is not like cooking an egg.

🧪 Scientists Who Worked with Matter

These scientists changed matter, kept it cold, or studied how it freezes. Their work helped sick people, kept food safe, and explained snow.

Alice Ball

1892–1916

A Black American chemist from Seattle, Washington. A chemist studies what things are made of and how they change. Oil from the seeds of the chaulmoogra tree could help people with Hansen's disease, a sickness of the skin and nerves. But the oil was thick and sticky. In Hawaiʻi, Alice Ball found a way to change the oil so doctors could give it as a shot. It helped many people. She died young, at 24. After she died, a man at her college took credit for her work. Today people know it was hers.

Frederick McKinley Jones

1893–1961

A Black American inventor from Cincinnati, Ohio. He was mostly self-taught. In the late 1930s, he designed a cooling machine for trucks. Now trucks could carry food and medicine far away without it spoiling. Cold food stayed cold. Frozen food stayed frozen!

Mary Engle Pennington

1872–1952

A chemist who studied how to keep food safe. She led a U.S. government food lab. Her work showed how keeping food cold helps it stay fresh and safe. She also studied railroad cars that kept food cold on long trips.

Ukichiro Nakaya

1900–1962

A Japanese scientist who studied snow. In 1936, he was the first person to grow a snow crystal in a lab. In a very cold room, water vapor froze onto a single rabbit hair. He found that a snowflake's shape depends on how cold and how wet the air is.
