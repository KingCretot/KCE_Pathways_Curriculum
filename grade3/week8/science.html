🪨 Sorting Rocks by Their Properties

Scientists who study rocks are geologists. They sort rocks by properties. A property is something you can observe. Rocks have different colors and textures. They also have different hardness. Do a scratch test. A harder thing scratches a softer thing.

👁 Watch Me Think

I have a white rock and a black rock. First, I look at color. Next, I feel texture. The white rock is powdery. The black rock is smooth like glass. Last, I try my fingernail. It scratches the white rock but not the black one. So the white rock is softer.

💎 Minerals Make Up Rocks

Rocks are made of minerals. Minerals were never alive. Think of a cookie with chocolate chips. The rock is the cookie. The minerals are the chips. Over a long time, water, ice, wind, and roots break rocks into tiny bits. This is called weathering.

👁 Watch Me Think

Granite has pink, white, and black specks. Each kind of speck is a different mineral. So I know granite is made of more than one mineral. If I see three kinds of specks, I know there are at least three minerals.

🧩 Picture It: Look Inside Granite

Mineral in graniteWhat it looks like
Quartzclear or milky white, glassy
Feldsparpink or white blocks
Micablack or silver, peels in thin, shiny sheets

Hardness: Scientists rank minerals from 1 (talc, the softest) to 10 (diamond, the hardest). Quartz is a 7. It can scratch glass.

🤝 Let's Try Together: Let's Try One Together

Water gets into a crack in a rock. In winter, the water freezes into ice. Ice takes up more space than water, so it pushes the crack wider. This happens again and again. Little by little, pieces break off. That is weathering. Those tiny bits of rock become part of soil.

🌱 What Is in Soil, and Why Farmers Care

Soil is a mix. It has tiny bits of rock. It has humus, made from dead plants and animals. It also has water and air. Plants get water and nutrients from soil. If farmers grow the same crop again and again, soil can wear out.

👁 Watch Me Think

I want to know if soil is good for plants. First, I look for dark humus. Humus gives nutrients. Next, I check for water and air spaces. Roots need both. Dark, crumbly soil with water and air is good for plants.

🧩 Picture It: A Healthy Soil Recipe

In a scoop of healthy soil, there is about:

  • Bits of rock (sand, silt, clay): almost half
  • Water: about one quarter
  • Air: about one quarter
  • Humus (broken-down plants and animals): a small part

Worms, bugs, and tiny decomposers live in soil, too.

🤝 Let's Try Together: Let's Try One Together

👁 Think Like a Soil Scientist

A soil scientist reads the layers from the bottom up. Sand sinks first, so it is on the bottom. Silt is next. Clay is the finest, so it settles last, on top. Bits of humus float. Then I compare the two jars, one row at a time.

🥜 Scientists of Rocks and Soil

These scientists looked closely at the ground under our feet. One helped farmers save their land. One helped map the rocks of the United States.

George Washington Carver

about 1864–1943

Born enslaved in Missouri. He became a plant and soil scientist. He taught at Tuskegee Institute in Alabama. Southern farmers grew cotton year after year, and the soil wore out. Carver taught crop rotation. Plant peanuts and cowpeas to help put nutrients back. He also taught farmers to grow sweet potatoes. In 1906, his Jesup Wagon, a school on wheels, brought lessons to farms. He found hundreds of uses for peanuts, so farmers could sell them.

Florence Bascom

1862–1945

A geologist. In 1896, she became the first woman hired by the U.S. Geological Survey. She studied the rocks of Pennsylvania and Maryland. She looked at thin slices of rock under a microscope to find their minerals. She trained many young women to become geologists.
