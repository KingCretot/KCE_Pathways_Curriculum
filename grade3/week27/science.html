🌡️ Two Temperature Scales

Temperature tells how hot or cold something is. We measure it with a thermometer, in units called degrees. There are two common scales. In the United States, weather reports use degrees Fahrenheit, written °F. Scientists use degrees Celsius, written °C, and so do people in many other countries. On the Fahrenheit scale, water freezes at 32 degrees and boils at 212 degrees. On the Celsius scale, water freezes at 0 degrees and boils at 100 degrees. (Water boils at these temperatures near sea level.)

👁 Watch Me Think

A thermometer says 20. Is that warm or cold? It depends on the scale! 20°F is below freezing, so I need a heavy coat. But 20°C is a nice day. So I always check the letter after the number. Anders Celsius made his scale in 1742, using the freezing point and the boiling point of water.

🔥 Heat Moves from Warmer to Cooler

Heat is energy that moves. Heat always moves from a warmer thing to a cooler thing. It keeps moving until both things are the same temperature. A mug of hot cocoa on a table gives off heat to the cooler air around it. The cocoa cools until it reaches room temperature, the temperature of the air in the room. A cold glass of juice warms up, because heat moves from the warmer air into the cooler juice.

👁 Watch Me Think

When I hold an ice cube, my hand feels cold. Is cold moving into my hand? No! Heat is moving out of my warm hand and into the cold ice. That is why the ice melts and my hand feels cold. Cold does not move. Heat does.

🥄 Conductors and Insulators

Some materials let heat move through them easily. They are conductors. Metals, like copper, aluminum, and steel, are good conductors. Other materials slow heat down. They are insulators. Wood, plastic, wool, and foam are insulators. That is why a cooking pot is made of metal, so heat can reach the food, but its handle is often plastic or wood, so it does not get as hot.

👁 Watch Me Think

A metal spoon and a wooden spoon sit in the same drawer, so they are the same temperature. Why does the metal one feel colder? Metal is a better conductor. It carries heat away from my warm hand faster than wood does, so my hand cools faster. The spoons really are the same temperature!

🏠 Try It at Home: Ice Cube Rescue

Which wrap keeps an ice cube from melting the longest? You need 4 ice cubes of the same size, 4 small plates, aluminum foil, a wool sock, a paper towel, and a timer. Wrap one ice cube in foil, one in the sock, and one in the paper towel. Leave one ice cube with no wrap. That one is your control, the one you compare with.

👁 Watch Me Think

To make it a fair test, I change only one thing: the wrap. Every cube is the same size, sits on the same kind of plate, and stays in the same room. Every 15 minutes, a grown-up helps me peek at each cube and write down how much ice is left. I predict the wool sock will win, because wool is an insulator.

☀️ Mária Telkes, the Sun Queen

She found ways to catch the Sun's heat and save it for later.

Mária Telkes

1900–1995

Mária Telkes was born in Hungary in 1900. She became interested in solar energy while studying at the University of Budapest, and later worked as a scientist and inventor in the United States. In 1948, she worked with architect Eleanor Raymond and with Amelia Peabody, who paid for the project, on the Dover Sun House in Dover, Massachusetts. It was the first house heated completely by the Sun. Sunlight melted a special salt. When the salt cooled and hardened again, it gave off its stored heat to warm the house. The salt system stopped working well after two winters, but Telkes kept inventing, including a solar still that turns salty seawater into drinking water. Reporters called her the Sun Queen, and she earned more than 20 patents.

🔎 More People Who Worked with Heat

These people asked, "How can we measure heat, keep it in, or move it where we need it?"

Anders Celsius

1701–1744

A Swedish astronomer. In 1742, he made a temperature scale based on two points, the freezing point and the boiling point of water, 100 degrees apart. Surprise! On his first scale, 0 was the boiling point and 100 was the freezing point. Not long after he died, thermometers flipped the scale to the way we use it today.

James Dewar

1842–1923

A Scottish scientist at the Royal Institution in London. In 1892, he invented the vacuum flask: one glass flask inside another, with almost no air between them, so the inside stayed very cold. He never patented it. In 1904, a company turned it into the Thermos, which keeps drinks hot or cold.

Alice H. Parker

born 1895

A Black inventor from Morristown, New Jersey, who studied at the Howard University Academy in Washington, D.C. In 1919, she received a patent for a heating furnace that burned natural gas. Her design sent warm air through pipes called ducts to different rooms, with burners that could each be controlled. It helped lead the way to the central heating many homes use today.
