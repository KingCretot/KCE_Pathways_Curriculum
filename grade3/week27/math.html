🏠 Picture It: Designing a Playground in Chattanooga

It's a bright morning on a grassy lot near the Tennessee River in Chattanooga. Orange string and wooden stakes mark where a new community playground will go. Designer Mr. Conrad unrolls his plans on a picnic table. His young planners, Chloe, Chidi, Colton, and Cora, grab their measuring tapes.

Every part of the playground needs two numbers. A fence or border goes around the edge, so the planners need the perimeter. Rubber mats, sand, and tiles cover the inside, so they need the area.

🌉 Chattanooga Facts

  • Chattanooga is a city in southeast Tennessee. The Tennessee River curves right through it, and Lookout Mountain rises above the city.
  • The Walnut Street Bridge crosses the Tennessee River. It was built in 1890 and is 2,376 feet long, which is almost half a mile.
  • Cars stopped using the Walnut Street Bridge in 1978. After repairs, it reopened in 1993 as a bridge for people on foot. It was one of the longest walking bridges in the world when it reopened.
  • The bridge connects downtown Chattanooga to the North Shore, where Coolidge Park sits beside the river.
  • Coolidge Park has a restored carousel built in 1894, with 52 hand-carved wooden animals, and a fountain kids can play in from May through September.

🌟 Word Wrangler

Flip each card to learn this week's vocabulary! Each card shows a sentence from the lesson. Tap the speaker to hear the word or the meaning.

perimeter
The distance around a shape. Add the lengths of all its sides. It is measured in feet.
“A fence or border goes around the edge, so the planners need the perimeter.”
area
The number of square units that cover a shape. It is measured in square feet.
“Rubber mats, sand, and tiles cover the inside, so they need the area.”
square foot
A square that is 1 foot long on each side. It is a unit for measuring area.
“Their areas are 11, 20, 27, 32, 35, and 36 square feet.”
dimensions
The length and the width of a rectangle.
“The rectangle with dimensions 6 feet by 6 feet holds the most sand.”
rectilinear figure
A shape whose sides are all straight and meet at right angles, like an L shape made of rectangles.
“The climbing area is a rectilinear figure shaped like the letter L.”
distributive property
You can split a rectangle into two smaller rectangles, find each area, and add them.
“By the distributive property, 7 × 13 = 7 × 8 + 7 × 5.”

Here is a memory trick: the word perimeter has rim in it, and a rim goes around the edge. Area is the space you would cover with a rug, so it is counted in square units.

🔄 Same Perimeter, Different Areas

Mr. Conrad has 24 feet of rubber border for a sandbox. Chloe lists every rectangle with whole-number sides that uses all 24 feet. The length plus the width must be half of 24, which is 12: 1 by 11, 2 by 10, 3 by 9, 4 by 8, 5 by 7, and 6 by 6. Their areas are 11, 20, 27, 32, 35, and 36 square feet.

👁 Watch Me Think

Same border, very different sandboxes! I list the rectangles in order so I do not skip one. As the rectangle gets closer to a square, the area grows. The rectangle with dimensions 6 feet by 6 feet holds the most sand.

🧩 Picture It

Perimeter 24 feet: 12 = length + width
2 × 10 = 20 square feet
4 × 8 = 32 square feet
6 × 6 = 36 square feet

📏 Same Area, Different Perimeters

Chidi has 24 square tiles of rubber safety mat. Each tile is 1 foot on each side, so any mat he makes covers 24 square feet. He finds every rectangle: 1 by 24, 2 by 12, 3 by 8, and 4 by 6. Their perimeters are 50, 28, 22, and 20 feet.

👁 Watch Me Think

To find every rectangle with an area of 24, I look for factor pairs: two numbers that multiply to 24. Same area, but the border changes a lot! A long, skinny mat needs the most border. The mat closest to a square needs the least.

🧩 Picture It

Area 24 square feet: factor pairs of 24
2 by 12: perimeter 28 feet
3 by 8: perimeter 22 feet
4 by 6: perimeter 20 feet

🤝 Let's Try Together

Colton needs a mat that covers 10 square feet. 1 × 10 = 10 and 2 × 5 = 10. The perimeters are 1 + 10 + 1 + 10 = 22 feet and 2 + 5 + 2 + 5 = 14 feet, so the 2 by 5 mat needs less border.

✂️ Combined Rectangles and the Distributive Property

The climbing area is 7 feet wide. Part of it is 8 feet long for the rope net, and the rest is 5 feet long for the climbing wall. Cora finds the area of each part: 7 × 8 = 56 and 7 × 5 = 35. 56 + 35 = 91 square feet. The whole rectangle is 7 by 13, and 7 × 13 = 91 too!

👁 Watch Me Think

Splitting a rectangle into two smaller rectangles is the distributive property in a picture. By the distributive property, 7 × 13 = 7 × 8 + 7 × 5. For a shape like an L, I split it into rectangles and add, or I find a big rectangle and subtract the missing corner.

🧩 Picture It

7 × 13 = 7 × (8 + 5)
= 7 × 8 + 7 × 5
= 56 + 35 = 91 square feet

🤝 Let's Try Together

Chloe needs the area of a 6 by 12 picnic patio. She splits 12 into 10 + 2: 6 × 10 = 60 and 6 × 2 = 12. 60 + 12 = 72 square feet.

🏗️ Design Challenge: L-Shaped Figures

The climbing area is a rectilinear figure shaped like the letter L. It fits inside a 10 by 8 rectangle with a 4 by 3 corner missing. Colton walks around it: 6 + 3 + 4 + 5 + 10 + 8 = 36 feet of border. For the area, he takes the big rectangle and subtracts the corner: 80 − 12 = 68 square feet.

👁 Watch Me Think

To find a missing side, I compare opposite directions. The top pieces must add up to the bottom: 6 + 4 = 10. The right-side pieces must add up to the left side: 3 + 5 = 8. Then I can find the perimeter by adding all six sides, and the area by splitting or subtracting.

🧩 Picture It

Perimeter: 6 + 3 + 4 + 5 + 10 + 8 = 36 feet
Area: 10 × 8 − 4 × 3
= 80 − 12 = 68 square feet

🤝 Let's Try Together

Split it another way: a 6 by 8 rectangle on the left, 6 × 8 = 48, and a 4 by 5 rectangle on the right, 4 × 5 = 20. 48 + 20 = 68 square feet. Same area!
