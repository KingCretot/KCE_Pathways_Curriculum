🧳 Why People Come

An immigrant moves to a new country to live. People come to the United States to work, to study, to join family, or to be safe. A refugee is a person who must flee home because it is not safe there. Chien-Shiung Wu and Dalip Singh Saund came to study at a university in California. After 1959, many Cuban families fled to Miami. In 1975, many families fled Vietnam as refugees.

👁 Watch Me Think

Is every person who moves here an immigrant? Let me think. Jeong's grandma moved here to join her brother. She chose to come, so she is an immigrant. A refugee did not choose danger at home; leaving was the only safe choice. And I remember Week 14: Native nations were here first, and many Africans were brought here by force, not by choice. Every family story begins in its own way.

🤝 Let's Try Together: Becoming a Citizen

People born in the United States are citizens. An immigrant can become a citizen through naturalization. The United States Citizenship and Immigration Services says an adult usually must be at least 18, live here as a permanent resident for at least 5 years, speak, read and write basic English, pass a test about United States history and government, and take the Oath of Allegiance.

🏝️ A Day at Ellis Island

Ellis Island, in New York Harbor, opened on January 1, 1892. The first person checked was Annie Moore, a 17-year-old from County Cork, Ireland, who came with her two younger brothers. Newcomers took a ferry from their ship to the island. They climbed the stairs to the big Registry Room while doctors watched for signs of sickness. Doctors sometimes had only six seconds for each person!

👁 Watch Me Think

How many people were turned away? Let me check the National Park Service. About 1 in 10 people was held back for another medical exam. But almost everyone was let in at the end. The busiest day was April 17, 1907, when 11,747 immigrants came through. So most stories at Ellis Island end with a ferry or a train to a new home.

🤝 Let's Try Together: Myth Buster: Were Names Changed?

Many families say their name was changed at Ellis Island. Historians say no! Ship workers wrote each passenger's name on a list, called a manifest, before the trip began. At Ellis Island, inspectors checked people against that list, with interpreters who spoke many languages. Some immigrants did change their own names later, by choice.

🌉 Angel Island: The Western Doorway

Angel Island, in San Francisco Bay, opened on January 21, 1910. It was called the "Ellis Island of the West." People came from more than 80 countries, including China, Japan, India, Korea, the Philippines, Russia and Mexico. About 100,000 Chinese and 85,000 Japanese newcomers were held there for a time.

👁 Watch Me Think

Why was Angel Island so different from Ellis Island? Most people at Ellis Island were let in within hours. But at Angel Island, a law from 1882 tried to keep most Chinese workers out. So Chinese newcomers had to answer many questions to prove who their families were. Many waited three weeks to three months. One man waited 756 days. Then, in 1940, a fire destroyed the main building, and the station closed.

🏘️ Newcomers Build Communities

Newcomers need homes, jobs, schools and friends who speak their language. So they help each other and build communities. In 1889, Jane Addams and Ellen Gates Starr opened Hull House in Chicago, one of the first settlement houses in the United States. Many neighbors were immigrants. Hull House had a kindergarten, classrooms, libraries, child care, legal help and an office to help people find jobs.

👁 Watch Me Think

Let me look at two neighborhoods. After 1959, so many Cuban families settled in one part of Miami, Florida, that it became known as Little Havana, named for Havana, the capital of Cuba. After 1975, Vietnamese refugee families settled in Orange County, California. Today Little Saigon there is the biggest Vietnamese community outside Vietnam. When newcomers live near each other, they can share language, food and help.

🕵️ Source Detective: A Law from 1882

On May 6, 1882, Congress passed a law that stopped Chinese workers, called laborers, from coming to the United States. It is called the Chinese Exclusion Act. It was the first big United States law to keep out one group of workers because of where they came from. In Week 18, you met Mary Tape, who spoke up for her daughter, Mamie. Her family lived with this law. Read its words like a historian.

Primary Source: The Chinese Exclusion Act (1882; parts)

"Whereas, in the opinion of the Government of the United States the coming of Chinese laborers to this country endangers the good order of certain localities within the territory thereof: Therefore, ..."

"... until the expiration of ten years next after the passage of this act, the coming of Chinese laborers to the United States be, and the same is hereby, suspended ..."

Word help: whereas = because. in the opinion of = what someone thinks, which is not the same as a proven fact. laborers = workers. endangers = puts in danger. good order = peace and safety. certain localities = some places. expiration = end. passage of this act = the day the law passed. suspended = stopped for a time.

An Act to execute certain treaty stipulations relating to Chinese (the Chinese Exclusion Act), approved May 6, 1882. Text from the National Archives, "Chinese Exclusion Act (1882)," Milestone Documents.

👁 Think Like a Historian

Let me read like a historian. The law begins with "in the opinion of." An opinion is what someone thinks, not a proven fact. Whose opinion? The lawmakers. Whose voice is missing? The Chinese workers it kept out, who had helped build railroads, farms and towns. How do we know how they felt? From their own words, like the poems carved on the walls at Angel Island and Mary Tape's letter. The law said ten years, but Congress kept it going until 1943. Today we can see that it was unfair.

🌟 Newcomers Who Helped Build America

Meet four immigrants from four different countries. As you read, ask: Why did each person come, and what did they build here?

Annie Moore

about 1874–1924

She came from County Cork, Ireland, on a steamship with her brothers, Anthony and Philip. On January 1, 1892, she was the first person checked at Ellis Island, and she was given a ten-dollar gold piece. She lived the rest of her life on the Lower East Side of New York City. Statues of her stand at Ellis Island and in Cobh, Ireland.

Dalip Singh Saund

1899–1973

He was born near Amritsar, India, and came in 1920 to study at the University of California, Berkeley. Until a 1946 law, people from India were not allowed to become citizens. He became a citizen in 1949. In 1956, he was elected to Congress from California: the first Asian American, first Indian American and first Sikh in Congress.

Chien-Shiung Wu

1912–1997

She was born near Shanghai, China. In 1936, she sailed to San Francisco and began studying physics at the University of California, Berkeley. She later taught at Columbia University and became a citizen in 1954. In 1975, she became the first woman president of the American Physical Society.

Jaime Escalante

1930–2010

He was born in La Paz, Bolivia, and came to the United States in the 1960s. He learned English and became a math teacher at Garfield High School in East Los Angeles in 1974. In 1982, 18 of his students passed a hard Advanced Placement calculus test. The 1988 movie Stand and Deliver tells his story.
