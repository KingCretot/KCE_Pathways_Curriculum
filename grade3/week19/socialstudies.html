child labor
Hard work done by children, often for long hours, instead of going to school. Example: Lewis Hine took photos to show child labor.
mill
A factory that makes things like cloth or thread. Example: Sadie Pfeifer worked in a cotton mill.
newsie

strike
When workers stop working together until things are made fairer. Example: The newsies' strike lasted about two weeks.
evidence
Facts or clues that show something is true. Example: Hine's photos were evidence that young children worked.
reformer
A person who works to fix unfair things. Example: Mother Jones was a reformer who spoke up for working children.
chore
A small, safe job at home. Example: Feeding the family pet is a chore, not child labor.

🧺 Kids at Work About 1900

About the year 1900, many children worked instead of going to school. They worked in mills that made cloth, in coal mines, on farms, and on city streets. In 1900, more than 1.7 million children ages 10 to 15 had jobs. That is almost 1 out of every 5 children that age. Some even younger children worked too.

👁 Watch Me Think

Why did so many children work? Many families were poor. They needed every bit of money to pay for food and a home. Also, there were few laws to stop it. So the causes were money troubles and missing laws. The effect was that many children missed school.

🤝 Let's Try Together: Chore or Child Labor?

Is helping at home the same as child labor? Let's compare. A chore, like setting the table, is short and safe, and it helps your family. Child labor was long, hard work, often all day, that kept kids out of school. So a chore is NOT child labor.

📸 Lewis Hine's Camera

Lewis Hine was a teacher who became a photographer. Starting in 1908, he worked for the National Child Labor Committee, a group that wanted to end child labor. He traveled all over the country and took thousands of photos of working children.

👁 Watch Me Think

Why did photos matter? People far away could not see inside the mills. A photo let them see for themselves. A photo is evidence. Hine also wrote a note, called a caption, for each photo. Photos plus notes made the truth hard to ignore.

🤝 Let's Try Together: Is a Photo the Whole Story?

Hine chose where to point his camera. He wanted people to see a problem, so his photos show one side. Historians also look for what the children, their families and the mill owners said. Asking "Whose voice is missing?" helps us see more.

📣 Kids Who Spoke Up

Children did not just wait for help. In July 1899, newspaper sellers called newsies went on strike in New York City. Two big newspapers had raised the price newsies paid, from 50 cents to 60 cents for 100 papers. The newsies stopped selling those papers for about two weeks.

👁 Watch Me Think

Did the strike work? Partly. The newspapers kept the price at 60 cents. But they agreed to buy back the papers the newsies could not sell. So the newsies lost less money. A strike can win part of what you want.

President Roosevelt did not meet the marchers. Did the march fail? Let's look. Newspapers wrote about the mill children, so more people learned about child labor. In 1905, Pennsylvania made its child labor law stronger. Speaking up can work slowly.

⚖️ New Rules, and Kids' Work Today

In 1938, President Franklin D. Roosevelt signed the Fair Labor Standards Act. This law said children under 16 could not work in most jobs, like factories and mills. No one under 18 could do dangerous jobs. Frances Perkins, the Secretary of Labor, worked hard for this law.

👁 Watch Me Think

Did the law protect every child? Not all. The law had weaker rules for farm work. So many children from farmworker families still worked in the fields. One of them was César Chávez. You will meet him soon.

🤝 Let's Try Together: Is It Fair Work?

A 10-year-old feeds the family dog each morning. Is that child labor? No. It is a short, safe chore that helps the family. A 10-year-old working all day in a mill instead of going to school? That was child labor, and today the law does not allow it.

🕵️ Source Detective: Two Photo Captions

Lewis Hine wrote a caption, or note, for each photo he took. Read two of his real captions. Both are kept at the Library of Congress.

Primary Sources: Two Captions by Lewis Hine (1908 and 1913)

Source 1: A cotton mill in South Carolina, 1908 (part of the caption)

"Sadie Pfeifer, 48 inches high, has worked half a year. One of the many small children at work in Lancaster Cotton Mills."

Word help: 48 inches high = 4 feet tall, about as tall as many second graders. half a year = 6 months.

Source 2: A cotton field in Texas, 1913 (part of the caption)

"Alton, seven years old, boy, picks fifty pounds a day. Ruth, nine years old, picks seventy-five pounds a day. ... Sun is hot, hours long, bags heavy."

Word help: picks = picks cotton by hand. fifty pounds = about as heavy as a 7-year-old child.

Lewis Hine, captions for photos taken on November 30, 1908, in Lancaster, South Carolina, and in September 1913, in Bells, Texas. National Child Labor Committee Collection, Library of Congress.

👁 Think Like a Historian

Let me compare. Source 1 is about a mill. Source 2 is about a cotton field. Source 1 gives Sadie's height, not her age. Source 2 gives the children's ages. Both were written by Hine, not by the children. So I ask: what would Sadie or Ruth have said in their own words?

🌟 People Who Worked for Kids

Meet four people connected to child labor. As you read, ask: What did each person do, and how did it help children?

Mother Jones

1837–1930

Her real name was Mary Harris Jones. She was born in Ireland. She was a reformer who spoke up for workers for many years. In 1903, she led mill children and parents on a march of nearly 100 miles. She wanted the President and the whole country to see child labor.

Lewis Hine

1874–1940

He was born in Oshkosh, Wisconsin. He taught at a school in New York City, where he took his students out to take photos. Then he used his camera to show child labor across the country. Today, you can see thousands of his photos at the Library of Congress.

Frances Perkins

1880–1965

In 1911, she saw a terrible factory fire in New York City. She decided to work for safer jobs. In 1933, she became the first woman to serve in a President's Cabinet, as Secretary of Labor. She helped pass the 1938 law that limited child labor.

César Chávez

1927–1993

He was born near Yuma, Arizona. When he was a boy, his family lost their farm. They moved around California to pick crops. He went to more than 30 schools! After eighth grade, he left school to work in the fields full time. Later, he led farmworkers to fight for fair pay.
