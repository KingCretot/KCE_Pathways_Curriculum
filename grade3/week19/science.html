🦴 How a Fossil Forms

A fossil is the remains or traces of a living thing from long ago, saved in rock. Most living things never become fossils. Here is how one can. An animal dies near water. Mud and sand, called sediment, quickly cover it. The soft parts rot away, but hard parts like bones, teeth, and shells stay. More layers pile on top. Over a very long time, often millions of years, the layers press into sedimentary rock. Water with minerals seeps in and fills tiny holes in the bones, so they become hard like stone.

👁 Watch Me Think

Why don't fossils form in hot lava from a volcano? Lava is melted rock, hot enough to burn up a body. Fossils usually need a gentle, quick burial in mud or sand. That is why most fossils are found in sedimentary rock. In Week 8 you sorted rocks. Now you know one kind can hold fossils!

🧩 Picture It: Steps of a Fossil

StepWhat happens
1. DiesA living thing dies, often near water.
2. BuriedMud and sand cover it quickly.
3. Soft parts rotBones, teeth, and shells stay.
4. Layers pile upThe layers press into rock.
5. Minerals seep inThe bones turn hard like stone.
6. UncoveredWind and water wear away the rock, and people find it.

🐾 Kinds of Fossils

Scientists sort fossils into two big groups. Body fossils are parts of the living thing itself: bones, teeth, shells, wood, and leaves. Trace fossils show what a living thing did: footprints, tracks, burrows, and even fossil poop! Some fossils are imprints or molds, shapes pressed into mud. If a mold fills up with minerals, it makes a cast, a copy of the shape. Some insects were trapped in sticky tree sap that hardened into amber.

👁 Watch Me Think

I find dinosaur footprints in rock. Is that a body fossil? No. No part of the dinosaur is there, only the prints it left when it walked. So it is a trace fossil. It tells me how the dinosaur moved, not what its bones looked like.

🐟 Fossils Are Clues to the Past

Fossils tell us what a place was like long ago. Fish and seashell fossils found on dry land mean water was once there. Scientists even found sea animal fossils in rock at the top of Mount Everest, the tallest mountain on Earth! That rock formed on an ancient sea floor, and over millions of years it was pushed up high. Fossil trees in icy Antarctica show it was once warmer, with forests.

👁 Watch Me Think

Florida has lots of fossil shark teeth, like the ones people find in the Peace River. Sharks live in the sea. So what does that tell me? Long ago, the sea covered Florida. That matches what scientists say: parts of Florida were underwater, on and off, for millions of years.

🏠 Try It at Home: Make an Imprint Fossil

Make your own fossils! You will make an imprint, like a leaf print in mud, and a trace fossil, like a footprint. Ask a grown-up to help you find safe things to press into play dough or modeling clay.

👁 Watch Me Think

I press a seashell into flat play dough and lift it out. The shell is gone, but its shape is left behind. That is a mold. Then I walk a toy dinosaur across the dough. Its tracks are trace fossils. They show how it moved, not what it looked like.

👧 Mary Anning, Fossil Hunter

Mary Anning found some of the most important fossils of her time, even though many scientists of her day did not give her credit.

Mary Anning

1799–1847

Mary grew up in Lyme Regis, a town by the sea in England. Her family was poor. She and her brother Joseph hunted fossils in the cliffs and sold them to help pay the family's bills. In 1811, Joseph found a strange skull. In 1812, when Mary was only 12 or 13, she dug out the rest of the skeleton. It was an ichthyosaur, an ancient sea reptile. In 1823 she found the first complete plesiosaur skeleton. She also helped scientists study fossil poop, which shows what ancient animals ate. Famous scientists used her finds, but the Geological Society of London did not let women become members until 1919, long after she died.

🔎 More Fossil Hunters

Fossil scientists come from all over the world. Each one asks, "What can this fossil tell us?"

Sue Hendrickson

born 1949

An American fossil hunter. In 1990, in South Dakota, she spotted big bones sticking out of a cliff. They belonged to one of the largest and most complete Tyrannosaurus rex skeletons ever found, about 90 percent complete. The dinosaur was named SUE after her. You can see it at the Field Museum in Chicago.

Xu Xing

born 1969

A paleontologist from China. He has named dozens of new kinds of dinosaurs, more than almost any other scientist alive today. Many of his fossils even show feathers! His feathered dinosaur fossils helped show that birds came from dinosaurs.

Lisa D. White

paleontologist today

A paleontologist who studies microfossils, fossils so tiny you need a microscope to see them. She studies fossil diatoms, tiny living things from ancient seas. She works at the University of California Museum of Paleontology. She has led programs that bring young people from many backgrounds outdoors to do science.
