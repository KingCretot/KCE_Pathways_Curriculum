🌳 Roots and Stems

A plant has parts, and each part has a job. Roots grow down into the soil. They hold the plant in place. They soak up water and minerals from the soil. The stem holds the plant up. Inside the stem are tiny tubes. The tubes carry water up from the roots to the leaves. They also carry food from the leaves to the rest of the plant.

👁 Watch Me Think

A big wind blows, but the oak tree does not fall over. Why? Its roots spread out under the ground and hold on tight. And how does water get from the ground to the top leaves? It travels up tiny tubes inside the trunk. A tree trunk is a big, woody stem!

🧩 Picture It: Try It at Home: The Colored Water Test

🍃 Leaves Make Food

Leaves are food factories! A plant makes its own food. Leaves take in sunlight. They take in a gas from the air called carbon dioxide. Water comes up from the roots. The leaf uses the light's energy to turn water and carbon dioxide into sugar. Sugar is the plant's food. The leaf gives off oxygen, a gas we breathe. Scientists call this photosynthesis.

👁 Watch Me Think

Does a plant eat dirt? Long ago, many people thought so. But a plant does not eat soil. Soil gives the roots water and tiny bits of minerals. A plant builds most of its body from water and from carbon dioxide in the air! Its food is made in the leaves.

🌸 Flowers and Pollination

Flowers have a big job: making seeds. Flowers make a yellow dust called pollen. To make seeds, pollen must move to the right part of a flower of the same kind. Moving pollen is called pollination. Many flowers have bright colors, a sweet smell and a sugary drink called nectar. These invite animals to visit.

👁 Watch Me Think

A bee lands on a flower to sip nectar. Yellow pollen sticks to its fuzzy body. It flies to the next flower. Some pollen rubs off there. The bee did not mean to help. It was just getting food! But now that flower can make seeds.

🌬️ How Seeds Travel

After pollination, a flower can make seeds. A seed holds a tiny baby plant and food to help it start growing. Seeds grow best with room, away from the parent plant. But plants cannot walk! So seeds travel in other ways. This is called seed dispersal.

👁 Watch Me Think

After a walk, I find a burr stuck to my dog's fur. It is covered in tiny hooks. That burr is a seed case hitching a ride! When it falls off far away, a new plant can grow there.

🧩 Picture It: Seed Travel Guide

How it travelsExample
windDandelion seeds have fluffy parachutes. Maple seeds have a wing and spin like helicopters.
waterCoconuts can float on the ocean to new beaches.
animals that eatBirds eat berries. The seeds pass through and land in the birds' droppings.
animals that hide foodSquirrels bury acorns and forget some. Those can sprout.
stickingBurrs hook onto fur and clothes.
poppingJewelweed pods burst open and fling their seeds.

🤝 Let's Try One Together

Why should a seed move away from its parent? If it lands right under the parent plant, it has to share sunlight, water and space. Far away, it may have more room to grow. So traveling helps the new plant.

🌺 Plant Scientists and an Inventor

These people looked closely at plants. One even turned a seed idea into an invention!

Janaki Ammal

1897–1984

A botanist, a scientist who studies plants, from Kerala, India. In 1931, she earned a top science degree, called a PhD, at the University of Michigan. Back in India, she mixed different kinds of sugarcane. She helped make sugarcane that grew well in India. People said she "sweetened" India's sugarcane. Later she spoke up to help save a forest called Silent Valley. A magnolia tree with small white flowers is named for her.

Marie Clark Taylor

1911–1990

In 1941, she became the first Black woman to earn a PhD in botany, at Fordham University in New York. She studied how the amount of light each day changes how plants grow and make flowers. She led the botany department at Howard University. She taught many science teachers to use real plants in their classes.

George de Mestral

1907–1990

An engineer from Switzerland. In 1941, he came home from a walk with his dog. Burdock burrs were stuck to his pants and his dog's fur. He looked at a burr under a microscope. It was covered in tiny hooks that grabbed loops in cloth and fur. He copied that idea and invented Velcro, a hook-and-loop fastener. He got a patent in 1955.
