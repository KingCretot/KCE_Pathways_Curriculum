🕰️ Timelines: Putting Time in Order

The past is everything that already happened. A timeline shows events in the order they happened. The oldest event goes first. Ten years make a decade. One hundred years make a century.

👁 Watch Me Think

How do I read years? A smaller year number means longer ago. So 1839 is long before 1926. To find how far apart two years are, I subtract. 1936 minus 1926 is 10. That is one decade!

🤝 Let's Try Together: How Long Is "Long Ago"?

"Long ago" can mean 100 years or 1,000 years. That is not very exact! So historians use real years. Then everyone knows exactly WHEN something happened. Seventy years ago is seven decades. Your great-grandparents may have been kids back then.

📜 Primary and Secondary Sources

Historians are like detectives. Their clues are called sources. A primary source was made at the time by someone who was there: a letter, a diary, a photo or an artifact. A secondary source was made later by someone who was not there, like a textbook or a video about history.

👁 Watch Me Think

Is a book about Harriet Tubman written in 2020 a primary source? I ask: was the writer there? No. Harriet Tubman lived in the 1800s. So it is a secondary source. Secondary sources still help! Good ones tell us which primary sources they used.

🤝 Let's Try Together: Clues You Can Hold

An artifact is an object. Archaeologists dig carefully to find artifacts like shell tools, pots and canoes. In our Florida lessons, you saw how Calusa shell tools show how people lived. An artifact cannot talk, but it is still a clue!

🎙️ Oral History: Ask an Elder

Not all history is written down. Oral history is memories people tell out loud. Someone records them so others can learn. For thousands of years, Indigenous Peoples, like the Seminole and the Cherokee, have passed down their history by telling it, from elders to children.

👁 Watch Me Think

From 1936 to 1938, writers saved more than 2,300 life stories from people who had been enslaved, many of them as children. The Library of Congress keeps these interviews. Slavery was cruel, so the stories can be hard to hear. But they let people tell their own lives, in their own words.

🤝 Let's Try Together: Be an Interviewer

One: ask permission. Two: ask open questions, like "What games did you play as a kid?" Three: listen and take notes, or record with a grown-up's help. Four: say thank you! StoryCorps, started in 2003, helps families record talks like this. Copies are saved at the Library of Congress.

🕵️ Source Detective: Reading an Old Photograph

Historians read photos like a book. Look, think, then ask questions. This famous photo is kept at the Library of Congress.

Primary Source: A Photograph from March 1936

What the photo shows: A mother sits outside a tent. She looks worried. Her hand rests near her chin. Two children lean on her shoulders, with their faces turned away. A baby lies in her lap.

Who took it: Dorothea Lange took the photo for the United States government. It was the Great Depression. Many families had little money and no work. Her photos showed people what was happening. Today it is often called Migrant Mother.

The caption kept with the photo at the Library of Congress:
"Destitute pea pickers in California. Mother of seven children. Age thirty-two. Nipomo, California"

Word help: destitute = very poor, with almost nothing. pea pickers = farm workers who pick peas. caption = words that tell about a photo.

Dorothea Lange, photograph known as "Migrant Mother," Nipomo, California, March 1936. Library of Congress, Prints and Photographs Division. Caption from the Library of Congress caption card.

👁 Think Like a Historian

I look: what do I see? I think: why was it made? I ask: what is missing? The caption tells her age and her work. But wait. It does not tell her name!

🕵️ Source Detective, Part 2: Her Side of the Story

Now read a second source. It comes from the mother herself.

Source 2: Her Own Words, 1978

For more than 40 years, most people did not know the mother's name. Then, in 1978, a reporter for a California newspaper, the Modesto Bee, found her. She told the newspaper that she was the mother in the photo.

Her name was Florence Owens Thompson. She was born in 1903 in Indian Territory, now Oklahoma. Her father was Cherokee. Later, she told a reporter she felt exploited by the photo. Her son said Lange had mixed up some details of the family's story.

Word help: exploited = used unfairly.

Florence Owens Thompson, as reported by the Modesto Bee and the Associated Press (1978), and by historians.

👁 Think Like a Historian

Two sources, two views. Source 1 tells what Lange saw and heard. Thompson's own words tell who she really was. A historian uses both and asks: Whose story is this? Who was missing?

🧭 Keepers of the Past

Meet three historians who saved stories that others left out. As you read, ask: Whose story did they save?

Carter G. Woodson

1875–1950

His parents had been enslaved in Virginia. As a young man, he worked in coal mines in West Virginia. He started high school at age 20! In 1912, he earned the highest degree from Harvard University. He saw that history books left out Black Americans. In 1926, he started Negro History Week in February, the month of Frederick Douglass's and Abraham Lincoln's birthdays. It grew into Black History Month.

Jovita González

1904–1983

She was born in Roma, Texas, by the border with Mexico. As a girl, she heard stories on her grandparents' ranch. She traveled to towns in South Texas. She listened to Mexican American families and wrote down their stories and traditions. In 1930, she became the first Mexican American president of the Texas Folklore Society.

Him Mark Lai

1925–2009

He was born in San Francisco. His parents came from China. In 1969, he and Philip Choy taught a college class on Chinese American history at San Francisco State. He helped translate the poems carved at Angel Island, from Week 14, for a 1980 book. People called him the "Dean of Chinese American History."
