💡 Ideas That Change Daily Life

An invention is a new thing or a new way to solve a problem. Inventors start with a problem. They try an idea, test it and make it better. Many inventions change how people live every day.

👁 Watch Me Think

Let me follow one invention. Problem: long ago, homes were dark at night. People used candles and oil lamps. They were smoky and could start fires. Idea: an electric light bulb. But early bulbs burned out fast. Inventors kept testing. Lewis Latimer and others found better ways to make the thin part inside that glows. Bulbs lasted longer and cost less.

They got patents for these inventions. Every inventor follows the same steps: find a problem, try an idea, test it, make it better.

⚡ Benjamin Franklin: The Whole Story

Benjamin Franklin was a printer, an inventor and a founder of the United States. He signed the Declaration of Independence and the Constitution. He also enslaved people for many years. Later, he worked to end slavery. Historians tell the whole story.

👁 Watch Me Think

A historian lists what Franklin did, good and bad. He invented the lightning rod to keep houses safe from fires. He started a library and a fire company in Philadelphia. But for many years, he enslaved people in his home. Later in life, he changed his mind. In 1787, he became the leader of a group working to end slavery. Both parts are true.

🔦 Hidden Builders: Who Gets the Credit?

Big inventions are often made by teams. But sometimes only one name gets remembered. Some builders were left out because they were Black, women or both. Historians look for the hidden people too.

👁 Watch Me Think

I ask: who else helped? Thomas Edison is famous for the light bulb. But many people worked on it. Lewis Latimer was a Black inventor. He taught himself to draw plans. He made the drawings for Alexander Graham Bell's telephone patent. He helped make light bulbs better. Later he taught English and drawing to immigrants. For many years, his name was left out of books.

🤝 Let's Try Together: Find the Hidden Figure

In 1953, Katherine Johnson started work at a research center in Virginia. It later became part of NASA. At first, Black women there had to work in a separate office. That was unfair. Still, her math was so good that astronauts trusted it. For years, few people knew her name. In 2015, she received the Presidential Medal of Freedom.

📜 Source Detective: Franklin's Own Words

In the 1740s, Franklin invented a stove that heated rooms better and used less wood. A governor offered him a patent, so only Franklin could sell it. Read what Franklin wrote about it later.

Primary Source: From Benjamin Franklin's Autobiography

"...as we enjoy great advantages from the inventions of others, we should be glad of an opportunity to serve others by any invention of ours; and this we should do freely and generously."

In plain words: We get a lot of help from other people's inventions. So we should be glad to help others with our inventions. We should share them freely.

Facts:

  • Franklin said no to the patent for his stove.
  • He did not patent the lightning rod or his other inventions either.
  • Other people were free to make and sell his stove.
Benjamin Franklin, The Autobiography of Benjamin Franklin (written 1771–1790), about the stove he invented around 1742.

👁 Think Like a Historian

A historian asks: Who wrote this? Franklin did, in the story of his own life. When? Many years later. Why? To explain his choice. Old writing can be hard, so I read the plain words too. Then I ask: who benefited from his choice?

🚀 Builders and Inventors Who Made a Difference

Meet five Americans whose ideas changed daily life. As you read, ask: What problem did each one solve? Who was left out of the story?

Benjamin Franklin

1706–1790

Franklin is also known for bifocals, glasses with two kinds of lenses in one frame. He wrote Poor Richard's Almanack, a yearly book with weather, sayings and tips. He enslaved people for many years. In 1790, near the end of his life, he asked Congress to end slavery.

Lewis Latimer

1848–1928

Lewis Latimer was born free in Massachusetts. His parents had escaped slavery in Virginia. As a teen, he served in the U.S. Navy. Then he worked for patent lawyers and taught himself to draw plans. In 1890, he wrote a book about electric lighting. He also taught English and drawing to immigrants.

Katherine Johnson

1918–2020

Katherine Johnson grew up in West Virginia. She loved to count everything. She finished high school at 14 and college at 18. At NASA, she did math by hand for space flights. In 2017, NASA named a building after her.

Mae Jemison

born 1956

Mae Jemison grew up in Chicago. She studied to be an engineer and a doctor. She worked as a doctor in Sierra Leone and Liberia in West Africa. In 1992, she flew on the Space Shuttle Endeavour. She was the first Black woman in space.

Ellen Ochoa

born 1958

Ellen Ochoa grew up in California. Her family has roots in Mexico. She is an engineer and an inventor. She has patents for ways computers use light to check objects. In 1993, she became the first Hispanic woman in space. She even played her flute there! Later, she led NASA's Johnson Space Center in Houston, Texas.
