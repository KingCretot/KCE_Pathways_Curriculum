🛒 Equal Groups & Repeated Addition

Theo lined up jars of honey on the trading post shelf: 4 jars in each row, and he made 3 rows. Instead of counting every jar one by one, he could add 4 three times: 4 + 4 + 4.

👁 Watch Me Think

When every group has the SAME number inside, I can either add the group size over and over (repeated addition), or I can multiply: the number of groups times the size of one group. 3 groups of 4 is the same as 4 + 4 + 4, which is the same as 3 × 4.

🧩 Picture It: Groups to Multiplication

3 groups of 4 jars
4 + 4 + 4 = 12
3 × 4 = 12

🤝 Let's Try Together

Mia stacked crates: 2 groups of 5. That's 5 + 5 = 10, which is the same as 2 × 5 = 10.

📑 Arrays: Rows & Columns

Grandpa asked Mia to arrange the seed packets in a neat array on the shelf: 4 rows, with 5 packets in each row. An array is any arrangement of equal rows and columns — and it's another way to see multiplication.

👁 Watch Me Think

In an array, the first number is usually the number of ROWS, and the second is the number in each row (COLUMNS). 4 rows of 5 is written 4 × 5. I can count by 5s to check: 5, 10, 15, 20.

⚡ Multiplication: The Fast Way to Add

By the end of the day, Theo and Mia had counted dozens of equal groups all over the trading post — jars, crates, seed packets, firewood. They realized multiplication was really just a shortcut for addition, and it worked no matter WHAT was being counted.

👁 Watch Me Think

Any repeated addition problem can become a multiplication fact: count the number of equal groups, then count how many are in EACH group. Multiply those two numbers, and you get the same answer as adding — just much faster.

🧩 Picture It: Same Answer, Two Ways

6 + 6 + 6 + 6 = 24
4 × 6 = 24

🤝 Let's Try Together

Grandpa had 5 shelves with 3 boxes on each shelf. 5 × 3 = 15 boxes total — much faster than adding 3+3+3+3+3!
