🐚 The Calusa: People of the Shells

The Calusa lived long ago on Florida's southwest coast, near Charlotte Harbor. They caught fish, crabs and clams with nets and traps. They did not need to farm. They piled shells into big shell mounds.

👁 Watch Me Think

The Calusa did not write books. So how do we know they ate seafood? I look at the clues. Their mounds are full of shells and fish bones. Shells are left over from seafood meals. So the mounds tell me what they ate!

🧩 Picture It: Calusa clues

  • Mound Key, in Estero Bay: an island made bigger with piles of shells
  • Canals the Calusa dug for their canoes
  • Fish bones and shell tools found in the mounds
  • Charlotte Harbor: the Peace River flows past Arcadia and ends here

🛶 The Timucua: Villages and Canoes

The Timucua lived long ago in north Florida, in many villages. They grew corn, beans and squash, and they fished and hunted. They paddled dugout canoes on rivers and lakes. After the Spanish came, many Calusa and Timucua died from new sicknesses and wars.

👁 Watch Me Think

How did people make a canoe with no metal tools? They used one big log. They burned the wood a little with fire. Then they scraped it out with shell and stone tools. That took skill and patience! I also ask: why did so many people die? The Spanish brought sicknesses that were new to Florida, and wars took more lives.

🧩 Picture It: A canoe clue

In 2000, a dry spell made the water in Newnans Lake, near Gainesville, very low. More than 100 old dugout canoes showed up in the mud! Some were almost 5,000 years old. People in north Florida made canoes for thousands of years.

🏡 Seminole and Miccosukee: Here Today

Later, Creek and other Indigenous people moved into Florida. They became the Seminole and Miccosukee nations. Today the Seminole Tribe of Florida and the Miccosukee Tribe have their own governments. Many families live on reservations, and some still build chickees and sew patchwork.

👁 Watch Me Think

I ask: why are they still here? In the 1800s, the U.S. Army fought wars to force them out of Florida. Many were forced to move far west. But a few hundred hid deep in the Everglades and never gave up. So their families still live in Florida today!

🔍 Source Detective: The Florida Evidence File

You are a history detective! Here are four clues about Florida's first peoples. Ask: Who made each one? What can it tell us?

Evidence File: Four Clues

Clue A. A shell mound (long ago)
Mound Key, in Estero Bay. An island built up with shells. Made by: Calusa people.

Clue B. A dugout canoe (long ago)
Found in Newnans Lake in 2000. Made from one big log. Made by: people in north Florida long ago.

Clue C. A picture printed in 1591
A picture of Timucua people planting seeds, printed in Europe. The printer, Theodor de Bry, never came to Florida. He copied drawings by a French artist who visited in the 1560s.

Clue D. A patchwork skirt (today)
A long skirt with rows of bright patchwork designs. Made by: a Seminole woman. The Seminole Tribe's own Ah-Tah-Thi-Ki Museum shows skirts like this.

A: Mound Key Archaeological State Park, Florida State Parks. B: Newnans Lake canoe site, studied by Florida archaeologists in 2000. C: Engraving by Theodor de Bry (1591), based on drawings by Jacques Le Moyne. D: Seminole patchwork, as shown by the Ah-Tah-Thi-Ki Museum of the Seminole Tribe of Florida.

🌟 People Who Shape Florida's Story

Meet groups of Florida's first peoples, long ago and today.

Calusa canal diggers and shell builders

long ago

They lived near Charlotte Harbor and Estero Bay. They dug canals for their canoes. They piled up shells to make Mound Key bigger.

Timucua village farmers

long ago

They lived in villages across north Florida. Each village had a chief. Families grew corn, beans and squash. They lived where St. Augustine is when the Spanish came in 1565.

Seminole patchwork makers

since about 1920

Seminole women began making patchwork about 100 years ago. They used hand-powered sewing machines. Mothers teach their daughters. Each design is sewn from tiny strips of colored cloth.

Seminole cattle ranchers

since the 1700s

Seminole families raised cattle in Florida as far back as the 1700s. Today they raise herds on the Brighton and Big Cypress Reservations. Brighton is near Lake Okeechobee.

The Ah-Tah-Thi-Ki Museum team

opened 1997

The Seminole Tribe of Florida runs this museum on the Big Cypress Reservation. Its name means "a place to learn, a place to remember." Here the Seminole tell their own story.

Miccosukee police and wildlife officers

since 1976

The Miccosukee Tribe started its own police department in 1976. Some officers patrol tribal lands in the Everglades. They help protect the wildlife there.
