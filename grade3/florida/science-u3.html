💧 Water Under Our Feet

When rain falls in Florida, some of it soaks into the ground. Under Florida is a rock called limestone. Limestone is full of tiny holes, like a sponge. The water fills the holes. A big layer of wet rock like this is an aquifer. Most Floridians drink water pumped up from aquifers.

👁 Watch Me Think

Where does my tap water come from? I live in Florida. Rain soaked into the ground. It filled holes in the limestone far below me. A well pumped it up. So the rain that falls on my yard can end up in my glass!

🧩 Picture It: The Path of a Raindrop

1. Rain falls on yards, fields, and forests → 2. Soaks in through sand and soil → 3. Fills holes in the limestone → 4. The aquifer holds the water → 5. A well pumps it up for drinking, or it flows out at a spring.

Florida's biggest aquifer is the Floridan Aquifer. It lies under all of Florida.

🤝 Let's Try Together: Let's Try One Together

A family dumps old paint on the ground. Rain falls. Could the paint reach the aquifer? Rain soaks in through the soil. It carries what is on the ground with it. Limestone has holes, so the water keeps going down. Yes, the paint could reach the aquifer. What goes on the ground can end up in our water.

🫧 Springs: Where the Aquifer Bubbles Up

In some places, water from the aquifer flows up out of the ground. That is a spring. Florida has more than 1,000 springs! Spring water is often very clear. It stays about 72°F all year. It feels cool in summer and warm in winter.

👁 Watch Me Think

Why does spring water stay the same temperature? It comes from deep underground. The air outside gets hot in summer and cold in winter. But deep rock stays about the same all year. So the water from the rock stays about the same too.

🐋 Manatees Need Warm Water

Manatees are big, gentle animals. They eat sea grass and water plants. They breathe air, so they swim up to the surface. Manatees get sick in water colder than about 68°F. In winter, many swim into warm springs. Boats and pollution can hurt them. Manatees are threatened, so Florida protects them.

👁 Watch Me Think

Why do manatees crowd into springs in January? The rivers and bays get cold in winter. Manatees get sick below about 68°F. Springs stay about 72°F. 72 is warmer than 68. So the spring is a safe, warm place to wait for spring weather.

🌿 People and Florida's Springs

People have lived by, studied, protected, and enjoyed Florida's springs for thousands of years. But not everyone was always allowed to enjoy them.

The Timucua People

lived in Florida until the 1700s

Indigenous People who lived in north and central Florida. They and the people who lived there before them made homes near springs and rivers for thousands of years. They fished, gathered snails and shellfish, and used the clear water. The shells they left made big piles called shell mounds. A huge shell mound still sits at Blue Spring. Mounds like this are evidence of how people lived.

Howard T. Odum

1924–2002

An ecologist, a scientist who studies how living things and their homes work together. In the 1950s, he studied Silver Springs. He measured how much the plants grew and how much the animals ate. It was one of the first studies of a whole ecosystem, from sunlight to fish.

Wayne Hartley

counting since about 1980

A manatee expert at Blue Spring State Park. He started there as a park ranger. For more than 40 years, he has paddled a canoe on cold winter mornings to count the manatees. He knows many of them by the scars on their backs and tails. His records show the manatees at Blue Spring growing from a few dozen to hundreds.

Paradise Park visitors

1949–1969

In the days of segregation, Black families were not allowed into Silver Springs. In 1949, the owners of Silver Springs opened Paradise Park nearby, just for Black visitors. Families came from all over to swim, picnic, and ride glass-bottom boats. In the 1960s, Silver Springs began letting Black visitors in, and Paradise Park closed in 1969. Today a Florida historical marker tells its story.
