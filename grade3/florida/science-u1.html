🌾 A River of Grass

The Everglades is a big wetland in South Florida. Water spills out of Lake Okeechobee. It moves south very, very slowly. Tall sawgrass grows in the shallow water. So people call it a river of grass.

👁 Watch Me Think

I know a river is moving water. Everglades water moves too, but very slowly. The land is almost flat, so the water creeps along. The water is shallow, and grass grows in it. So it is a river of grass!

🧩 Picture It: Follow the Water

  • Top: Lake Okeechobee, a big lake
  • Middle: the sawgrass marsh, the river of grass

🐊 Animals of the Everglades

Many animals live in the Everglades. The American alligator swims in ponds and marsh. Wading birds have long legs. The pink roseate spoonbill is a wading bird. The Florida panther hunts on drier land. Gentle manatees swim in warm water nearby and eat sea grass.

👁 Watch Me Think

Each animal has body parts that help it. The spoonbill has a flat bill shaped like a spoon. It swings its bill in the water to feel for food. The alligator has eyes and a nose on top of its head. It can see and breathe while it hides in the water.

🤝 Let's Try Together: Make a Food Chain

Sun → water plants → small fish → alligator. The water plants are producers. They make food from sunlight. The small fish and the alligator are consumers. They eat other living things.

💧 What Living Things Need, and Why We Protect It

Everglades animals need food, water, air and shelter. Long ago, people dug canals to drain water away. They built farms, roads and towns. Many animals lost their homes. Today the Florida panther is endangered. Many people now work to protect the Everglades.

👁 Watch Me Think

I ask: what happens if the water goes away? Plants dry up. Small fish have no place to live. Then birds have less food. One change can hurt the whole food chain. That is why clean water matters so much.

🧩 Picture It: Then and Now

  • Long ago: water spread over the whole river of grass.
  • 1900s: people dug canals and drained land for farms and towns.
  • 1947: Everglades National Park opened to protect part of it.
  • Today: people work to send more clean water south.

🤝 Let's Try Together: What Does an Alligator Need?

Food: fish and turtles. Water: ponds and marsh. Air: it comes up to breathe. Shelter: a gator hole, a pool it keeps open when the land dries. Now try it with the spoonbill!

👩 People Who Protect the Everglades

Many people help the Everglades. Some write. Some study. Some have lived there for a long time.

Marjory Stoneman Douglas

1890–1998

She was a writer in Miami. In 1947 she wrote a book called The Everglades: River of Grass. She said the Everglades was not a useless swamp. It was a living river! She spoke up for it her whole long life. She lived to be 108.

The Miccosukee and Seminole Peoples

Long ago to today

These Indigenous Peoples have made homes in the Everglades for a long time. They know where fish gather and how the water moves. Families lived in chickees. A chickee has open sides and a roof made of palm leaves. Today they still live there. They work to keep the water clean.

Park Rangers and Scientists

Today

Rangers at Everglades National Park teach visitors and help keep animals safe. Scientists count bird nests, measure the water and keep track of Florida panthers.
