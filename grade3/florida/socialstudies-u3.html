🏰 Fort Mose: A Free Black Town

Long ago, Spain ruled Florida as a colony. Britain ruled Carolina, to the north. Many Africans were enslaved in Carolina. Some escaped and ran south to Spanish Florida. In 1738, near St. Augustine, they built Fort Mose. It was the first legal free Black town in what is now the U.S.

👁 Watch Me Think

I ask: why did freedom seekers run SOUTH? Most escape stories we hear go north. But in the 1700s, Spanish Florida was close to Carolina. The king of Spain promised freedom to people who escaped. They had to become Catholic and help defend Florida in the militia. So for them, south meant freedom!

🧩 Picture It: Timeline: Fort Mose

YearWhat happened
1693Spain's king promises freedom to people escaping slavery in British colonies.
1738Fort Mose is built, about 2 miles north of St. Augustine.
1740British soldiers take Fort Mose. The Fort Mose militia helps win it back, but the fort is wrecked.
1752People rebuild Fort Mose.
1763Spain gives Florida to Britain. Fort Mose families sail to Cuba to stay free.
1994Fort Mose becomes a National Historic Landmark. Today it is a state park.

🤝 Let's Try Together: Who Was Francisco Menéndez?

Francisco Menéndez was born in West Africa. He was enslaved in Carolina, then escaped to Florida. At first, Spanish leaders did not set him free. So he sent written requests to Spanish leaders again and again, asking for his people's freedom. In 1738, the governor said yes. Menéndez became captain of the Fort Mose militia. He was a trailblazer who led his town.

📚 Zora Neale Hurston: Stories From Eatonville

Zora Neale Hurston grew up in Eatonville, Florida, near Orlando. It was one of the first U.S. towns started and run by Black citizens. Her father was its mayor. Zora became an author. She also collected folklore, the stories people tell and pass down.

👁 Watch Me Think

Why did Zora collect stories? She noticed something. Books in her time rarely showed how Black Southerners really talked. So she went back to Florida with a notebook. She listened to stories in towns and work camps. She wrote them down so they would not be lost. A story keeper can be a trailblazer too!

🧩 Picture It: Timeline: Zora's Path

YearWhat happened

1925She moves to New York City to study and write.

1960She dies in Fort Pierce, Florida.
1973Writer Alice Walker finds her grave and puts up a marker.

🤝 Let's Try Together: Jump at the Sun

Zora's mother told her children to "jump at de sun." Zora wrote: "We might not land on the sun, but at least we would get off the ground." What did her mother mean? Aim high! Even if you miss, you still go far. Zora aimed high her whole life.

🌟 Bethune and Tiger Jumper: New Stories

Trailblazers see a need and fill it. Mary McLeod Bethune saw that Black patients in Daytona were turned away from the hospital. So in 1911, she opened a small hospital for them. Betty Mae Tiger Jumper trained as a nurse. She cared for Seminole families for about 40 years.

👁 Watch Me Think

Let me compare their paths. Both saw people who needed care. Both learned skills to help. Tiger Jumper started learning English at 14. In 1945, she became the first Florida Seminole to finish high school. Then she went to nursing school. Bethune and Tiger Jumper both opened doors for others to follow.

🤝 Let's Try Together: Words That Last

Near the end of her life, Bethune wrote a letter to the future. She called it "My Last Will and Testament." It was printed in 1955, after she died. She wrote: "I leave you love. I leave you hope." She also wrote: "I leave you a thirst for education." What do these gifts have in common? You cannot hold them. She left ideas, not money. Tiger Jumper left words too: Seminole legends in a book for children.

🕵️ Source Detective: The 1759 Count of Fort Mose

In 1759, Spanish leaders counted the people of Fort Mose. Much later, archaeologists dug at the site. Use both clues to learn about life there.

Primary Source: A Spanish Count (Census) of Fort Mose, 1759, and Objects Found by Archaeologists

Clue 1: The 1759 count

WhoNumber
Men37
Women15
Boys7
Girls8

The people lived in 22 houses with roofs made of palm leaves.

Clue 2: Objects found in the ground (1986–1988)

  • gunflints and musket balls (used in old guns)
  • buckles and nails
  • thimbles (for sewing)
  • pieces of pottery and glass bottles
  • burnt seeds and animal bones
Spanish count of Gracia Real de Santa Teresa de Mose (Fort Mose), 1759, as reported by historian Jane Landers. Objects dug up by the Fort Mose research team led by archaeologist Kathleen Deagan, 1986–1988.

👁 Think Like a Historian

A historian uses numbers AND objects. The count tells me who lived there. The objects tell me what people did every day. A thimble means someone sewed. Burnt seeds and bones tell what people cooked. Gunflints and musket balls fit a town with a militia.

🌴 Florida Trailblazers

Meet four Floridians who opened new paths. As you read, ask: What was new about what they did? Who followed after them?

Francisco Menéndez

lived in the 1700s

He was born in West Africa and enslaved in Carolina. He escaped to Spanish Florida. He became captain of the Fort Mose militia. He helped defend Florida in 1740. Later, he and other Fort Mose families moved to Cuba to stay free.

Zora Neale Hurston

1891–1960

An author who grew up in Eatonville. She studied how people live and collected folklore. She wrote Their Eyes Were Watching God (1937). Every year, Eatonville holds a festival in her honor.

Mary McLeod Bethune

1875–1955

Born in South Carolina to parents who had been enslaved. She opened a school and a hospital in Daytona Beach. In 2022, Florida placed her statue in the U.S. Capitol. She was the first Black American with a state statue there. It took the place of a statue of a general. He had fought for the South in the Civil War.

Betty Mae Tiger Jumper

1923–2011

Born near Indiantown, Florida. She started learning English at 14. She became the first Florida Seminole to finish high school. She worked as a nurse, helped start the Seminole News and wrote down Seminole legends for children.
