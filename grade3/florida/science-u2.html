🌊 A Storm Born Over Warm Water

A hurricane is a giant storm that spins. It starts over the ocean when the water is very warm. The Sun heats the water, and some of it evaporates into the air. The warm, wet air rises and makes big clouds.

👁 Watch Me Think

Hurricanes need warm water, at least about 80°F. That is as warm as a nice swimming pool. So I ask: when is the ocean near Florida warmest? In summer and fall! That is why hurricane season is from June to November. When a hurricane moves over land, it loses its warm water. Then it slowly gets weaker.

🧩 Picture It: How a Hurricane Grows

  • 1. The Sun warms the ocean.
  • 2. Water evaporates. Warm, wet air rises.
  • 3. Big clouds grow tall.
  • 4. The clouds and wind start to spin.
  • 5. The wind blows 74 miles per hour or faster. Now it is a hurricane!

🤝 Let's Try Together: Like Steam from Soup

Think of a bowl of warm soup. Steam rises from it. Warm water gives off water vapor. Over a warm ocean, warm, wet air rises too. It builds giant clouds. More warm water means more fuel for the storm.

🌀 The Spinning Storm and Its Eye

As a hurricane grows, its clouds and wind spin in a giant circle. In the middle is the eye. The eye is calm. Sometimes you can even see blue sky! Around the eye is the eyewall, where the wind is strongest.

👁 Watch Me Think

If the eye is calm, is the storm over? I think about the circle. The eye is in the middle. After the eye passes, the other side of the eyewall comes. The wind blows hard again! So families stay inside until grown-ups in charge say the whole storm is gone.

🧩 Picture It: A Hurricane from Space

  • Eye: the calm, round hole in the middle
  • Eyewall: the ring with the strongest wind and rain
  • Rain bands: long arms of clouds that curl out
  • Spin: near Florida, hurricanes spin opposite the way a clock's hands move
  • Categories: scientists rate hurricanes from 1 to 5. Category 5 has the fastest wind.

🤝 Let's Try Together: Draw It in the Air

Use your finger to draw a big circle. Go the opposite way a clock's hands go. That is how hurricanes near Florida spin! Now put a dot in the middle. That dot is the eye. Trace a ring around the dot. That ring is the eyewall.

🎒 How Families Get Ready

Scientists watch every hurricane closely. They make a forecast to tell people where the storm may go. That gives families days to get ready. Families pack a hurricane kit and make a plan.

👁 Watch Me Think

Wind is not the only danger. A hurricane can push ocean water onto land. This is called storm surge. So families who live where water could flood evacuate, or leave. People in Florida say, 'Run from water, hide from wind.' Many families far from the water stay home in a strong house.

🧩 Picture It: My Hurricane Kit

  • Water for every person and pet
  • Food that does not need cooking
  • Flashlight and batteries
  • Medicine
  • Phone charger
  • Pet food
  • A favorite book or game

🤝 Let's Try Together: Make a Family Plan

Ask a grown-up these questions. Do we live in an evacuation zone? Where would we go? Who would we call? Where is our kit? A plan helps everyone know what to do. Knowing what to do helps everyone feel calm.

✈️ Weather Helpers

Many people help us know when a hurricane is coming. Some fly planes. Some study the data. Some write the forecasts.

The Hurricane Hunters

Today

These pilots and scientists fly special airplanes right into hurricanes! NOAA's hurricane hunter planes are based in Lakeland, Florida. Two of them are nicknamed Kermit and Miss Piggy. The crews drop tools that measure the wind as they fall.

Karen Moore

Air Force hurricane hunter

Karen Moore flew with the Air Force hurricane hunters. She has been called the first African American woman to do this job in the Air Force. She flew into Hurricane Maria in 2017. She says, 'Go for what you want to do against all odds.'

Lixion Avila

born 1950

He grew up in Cuba and studied weather there. Then he moved to Miami. At the National Hurricane Center, he first shared hurricane warnings in Spanish. Later he forecast hurricanes there for many years.

Shirley Murillo

NOAA weather scientist

She grew up in Miami. In 1992, a big hurricane named Andrew hit South Florida. It made her want to study weather. She became a meteorologist, a scientist who studies weather. She flies on hurricane hunter planes to collect data. She tells young people to ask lots of questions.
