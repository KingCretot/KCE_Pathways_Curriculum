❓ Ask and Imagine

An engineer solves problems. First, engineers ask. What is the problem? What must the design do? Those are the criteria. What are the limits? Those are the constraints. Next, they imagine many ideas, not just one.

👁 Watch Me Think

My problem: my pencils roll off my desk. I ask what a good solution must do. It must keep pencils from rolling. That is a criterion. I can only use things I have at home. That is a constraint. Now I imagine ideas: a cup, a box, or a rubber band around them.

🧩 Picture It: The Design Process

StepWhat you do
1. AskFind the problem. List the criteria and constraints.
2. ImagineThink of many ideas. Pick the best one.
3. PlanDraw it. Label it. List the materials.
4. BuildMake a prototype.
5. TestTry it. Record what happens.
6. ImproveFix one thing. Test again.

The steps make a loop. After Improve, you can go back and test again.

🤝 Let's Try Together: Let's Try One Together

A lunch box design: "It must keep food cold." Is that a criterion or a constraint? It says what the design must do. So it is a criterion. "You may spend only 10 dollars." That is a limit. So it is a constraint.

✏️ Plan and Build a Prototype

Next, engineers plan. They draw the design and label each part. They list the materials. Then they build a prototype. A prototype is a first model to test. Today you will build a paper bridge at home!

👁 Watch Me Think

My bridge must cross a 6-inch gap between two stacks of books. It must hold pennies. My constraint is one sheet of paper. A flat sheet is floppy. What if I fold it? Folds might make it stiffer. I will draw a folded bridge and label the folds.

🔧 Test It, Fix It, Test Again

Engineers test their prototype. They write down what happens. If it fails, that is OK! A failed test gives information. Engineers improve the design. They change one thing at a time. Then they test again.

👁 Watch Me Think

My flat paper bridge sagged and fell with only 3 pennies. Why? Flat paper bends easily. What one thing can I change? I can fold it like a fan. I will keep the same paper, gap, and pennies. Then I test again and compare.

Your bridge fell. Your friend says, "Use thicker paper, more tape, AND fold it!" What is wrong with that? If you change three things and it works, you will not know which change helped. Change one thing. Fold it first. Test. Then try the next change.

🛠️ Engineers and Inventors Who Kept Fixing

These people saw a problem, built something, tested it, and made it better. None of them gave up after the first try.

Lonnie Johnson

born 1949

An engineer from Mobile, Alabama. In high school, he built a robot from scrap parts and won a science fair. Later, he worked on spacecraft for NASA, like the Galileo mission to Jupiter. At home, he was testing a new kind of pump that used water. It shot a strong stream of water across the room! That gave him the idea for the Super Soaker water toy. He built and fixed many prototypes. He has more than 100 patents.

Mary Anderson

1866–1953

An inventor from Birmingham, Alabama. On a snowy trip to New York City, she watched a streetcar driver. He had to open his window again and again to clear the snow. She designed a windshield wiper the driver could move with a handle from inside. She got a patent in 1903. At first, companies said no. Years later, wipers came on almost every car.

Margaret Knight

1838–1914

An inventor who worked in factories as a girl. She invented a machine that made flat-bottomed paper bags, like grocery bags today. A man copied her idea and said it was his. She showed her drawings and notes in court as evidence. She won and got her patent in 1871.

William Kamkwamba

born 1987

An inventor from Malawi, a country in Africa. His family could not pay for school. So he read science books at the village library. When he was about 14, he built a windmill from old bicycle parts, a tractor fan, and wood. It made electricity for lights and a radio in his home. Later he built more windmills and a solar-powered water pump for his village.
