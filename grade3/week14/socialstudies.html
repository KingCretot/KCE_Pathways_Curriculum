🚢 Why People Move

An immigrant moves to a new country to live. People come to the United States for many reasons. Some come for jobs or school. Some come to be safe, or to join family.

👁 Watch Me Think

I ask: did everyone come by choice? No! Indigenous Peoples were here first, for thousands of years. Many Africans were taken from their homes and brought here against their will. They were forced into slavery. Immigrants chose to come. So America's story has many different beginnings.

🤝 Let's Try Together: Push and Pull

A push makes people leave home, like war, hunger or no jobs. A pull draws people to a new place, like jobs, safety or family. Example: In the 1840s, potato crops failed in Ireland. Many people went hungry. That was a push. Jobs in American cities were a pull. Over a million Irish people came.

🍲 Foods, Languages and Traditions

Each group brings its culture: foods, music, languages and holidays. Families pass down traditions. Many traditions mix and become shared American ones. This makes the country diverse.

👁 Watch Me Think

Let me look at a bowl of gumbo, a Louisiana soup. Okra came from Africa. The word gumbo comes from an African word for okra. Filé, a spice made from ground leaves, came from the Choctaw people. The roux, a cooked flour sauce, came from French cooks. One bowl holds many cultures!

🇺🇸 Out of Many, One

U.S. coins say E pluribus unum. That is Latin for "Out of many, one." People from many cultures make one country. But newcomers were not always treated fairly. People worked to change unfair laws, just like in Week 13.

👁 Watch Me Think

Let me test the motto with facts. In 1882, a law kept out most workers from China. Was that "out of many, one"? No. It was unfair. In 1943, that law ended. In 1965, a new law treated people from all over the world more fairly. The country did not always live up to its motto, but people pushed it closer.

🕵️ Source Detective: A Poem and a Law

In Week 11, you met Emma Lazarus and her poem on the Statue of Liberty. Now compare her poem with a law from the same time. Was everyone welcomed?

Primary Sources: A Poem from 1883 and a Law from 1882

Source 1: A poem

Emma Lazarus wrote "The New Colossus" in 1883. In 1903, it was put on a plaque inside the Statue of Liberty. Part of it says:

"Give me your tired, your poor,
Your huddled masses yearning to breathe free"

Word help: huddled masses = big crowds pressed close together. yearning = wanting very much.

Source 2: A law

The Chinese Exclusion Act of 1882 said most workers from China could not come to the United States. Many Chinese people who did come were detained at Angel Island for weeks or even months. Some carved poems in Chinese into the wooden walls. The law ended in 1943.

Emma Lazarus, "The New Colossus" (1883), plaque at the Statue of Liberty (1903). Chinese Exclusion Act, passed by the U.S. Congress (1882). Angel Island Immigration Station, California (1910–1940).

👁 Think Like a Historian

A historian compares sources. The poem says America welcomes tired and poor people. The law says most workers from China may not come. Do they agree? No! When two sources disagree, I ask: who was welcomed, and who was left out?

🌎 Americans With Many Roots

Meet three Americans whose families came from other places. As you read, ask: How did their heritage shape their work?

Sonia Sotomayor

born 1954

She grew up in the Bronx in New York City. Her parents came from Puerto Rico. People born in Puerto Rico are U.S. citizens, so they moved but did not immigrate. She loved Nancy Drew books. In 2009, she became the first Latina justice on the U.S. Supreme Court. She writes picture books for kids too.

Maya Lin

born 1959

Her parents moved from China to the United States. She grew up in Athens, Ohio. At age 21, she was still in college. That year, she won a contest to design the Vietnam Veterans Memorial in Washington, D.C. It is a long black stone wall. It holds the names of more than 58,000 Americans who died in that war. She also designed the Civil Rights Memorial in Alabama.

Yo-Yo Ma

born 1955

He was born in Paris, France, to parents from China. His family moved to New York when he was about 7. At age 7, he played the cello for President John F. Kennedy. He started the Silkroad, a group that mixes music from many cultures. He plays music from all over the world.
