🐿️ Living or Nonliving?

A living thing is called an organism. Every organism grows. It needs food and water. It responds, or reacts, to things around it. It can make more of its own kind. A nonliving thing cannot do these things on its own.

👁 Watch Me Think

I see a squirrel. Does it grow? Yes. Does it need food and water? Yes. Does it respond? Yes, it runs when I get close. Can it have babies? Yes. Four yes answers. The squirrel is living. Now a rock. It does not grow or eat. It does not react. It cannot make baby rocks. The rock is nonliving.

🤝 Let's Try Together: A Tricky Campfire

A campfire moves. It seems to get bigger. Is it alive? Let's test it. It does not need water. Water puts it out! It cannot have baby fires on its own. So a fire is nonliving. Moving is not enough to be alive.

💧 The Four Needs

Every living thing has four needs. It needs food. It needs water. It needs air. It needs shelter, a safe space to live. Animals eat other living things for food. Plants make their own food using sunlight.

👁 Watch Me Think

Let me check a squirrel's needs. Food? It eats nuts and seeds. Water? It drinks from puddles and streams. Air? It breathes all the time. Shelter? It sleeps in a nest high in a tree. All four needs are met. The squirrel can stay alive.

🔍 Evidence: Watch Like a Scientist

Scientists do not just guess. They observe, or watch carefully. They write down what they see. The facts they collect are called evidence. Evidence helps us know what is true.

👁 Watch Me Think

My friend says, "Birds drink water." Is that a guess or evidence? I watch a bird bath for ten minutes. I see three birds dip their beaks in the water. I write it down. Now I have evidence that birds drink water.

🧩 Picture It: Guess or Evidence?

  • Guess: "I think ants like sugar."
  • Evidence: "I put sugar and salt near an anthill. After 10 minutes, I counted 20 ants at the sugar and 2 at the salt."

🤝 Let's Try Together: The Ant Test

Look at the ant notes above. Which one is evidence? The second one! It tells what was seen and counted. The first one is only a guess. A scientist would ask: was it fair? Were the sugar and salt the same distance from the anthill?

👁 Think Like a Scientist

A scientist reads each row slowly. First I look at what each plant got. Then I compare Day 1 and Day 10. Plant A went from 5 cm to 11 cm. That is 6 cm of growth. Then I read how it looked. Last, I ask: was the test fair? Only one thing changed for each plant, so yes.

🔭 Scientists Who Watch Nature

Scientists come from all over the world. These scientists learned about living things by observing them carefully.

Jane Goodall

1934–2025 · England

In 1960, Jane went to Gombe in Tanzania, in Africa. She watched chimpanzees every day for years. She saw a chimp poke a grass stem into a termite mound to catch termites. That showed chimps make and use tools. She learned by careful watching.

Wangari Maathai

1940–2011 · Kenya

Wangari saw that cut-down forests in Kenya hurt people and animals. She started the Green Belt Movement. Women in Kenya planted millions of trees. Trees give food, shelter, and clean water. She won the Nobel Peace Prize in 2004.

Ynés Mexía

1870–1938 · United States and Mexico

Ynés was a Mexican American plant scientist. She began her science work in her fifties! She traveled across Mexico and South America. She collected thousands of plants. Some were kinds scientists had never named before.
