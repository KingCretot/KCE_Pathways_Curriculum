🕊️ Many People Worked to End Slavery

Long ago, slavery was legal in parts of the United States. Enslaved people were forced to work without pay. Their families could be split apart. Many people worked hard to end this unfair system.

👁 Watch Me Think

I ask: who ended slavery? Not just one person. Many enslaved people escaped as freedom seekers. People like Harriet Tubman helped them. Almost 200,000 Black soldiers and sailors fought for the North. President Lincoln signed an order. Then the 13th Amendment ended slavery in 1865.

🧩 Picture It: Timeline: The Road to Freedom

YearWhat happened
1849Harriet Tubman escapes from slavery in Maryland.
1861The Civil War begins. Abraham Lincoln is President.
1863Lincoln signs the Emancipation Proclamation. It frees enslaved people in the states fighting against the North.
1865The war ends. The 13th Amendment ends slavery in the whole country.

🤝 Let's Try Together: Did Lincoln's Goal Change?

Lincoln said slavery was wrong. But in 1862, he wrote a letter. He said his main goal was to save the Union, or keep the country together. Later, he decided that freeing enslaved people would help win the war. Then he pushed for the 13th Amendment. His ideas changed over time. Historians look at his own letters to know this.

🚌 Unfair Rules on the Bus

Even after slavery ended, many unfair laws stayed. In the South, segregation laws kept Black and white people apart. In Montgomery, Alabama, Black riders had to give up bus seats to white riders. In 1955, people joined together to change this.

👁 Watch Me Think

Let me compare two ways to change a rule. Way 1: fight and hurt people. Way 2: refuse to ride the bus, march and speak. Dr. King chose Way 2. This is called nonviolence. A boycott is a nonviolent tool. When riders stopped using buses, the bus company lost money. Then leaders had to listen.

🧩 Picture It: The Montgomery Bus Boycott

DateWhat happened
Dec. 1, 1955Rosa Parks will not give up her bus seat. She is arrested.
Dec. 5, 1955Black riders begin the boycott. They walk, share cars and take taxis.
Nov. 1956The U.S. Supreme Court says bus segregation is against the law.
Dec. 20, 1956The boycott ends after 381 days. Riders may sit in any seat.

🤝 Let's Try Together: Who Is Missing?

Many books tell only about Rosa Parks and Dr. King. Who else made the boycott work? Thousands of Black riders walked to work for more than a year. Jo Ann Robinson and other women printed thousands of flyers overnight. And nine months before Rosa Parks, a girl named Claudette Colvin did the same. She was 15. She also refused to give up her seat. Change takes many people!

🍇 Fair Pay for Farmworkers

Farmworkers pick the fruits and vegetables we eat. In the 1960s, many in California earned very little. They worked long, hot days. Often they had no clean water or bathrooms. Many were Mexican American and Filipino American. They formed a union to ask for fair pay.

👁 Watch Me Think

How can workers with little money get power? One worker alone can be ignored. But a union speaks for many. In 1965, Filipino American grape pickers, led by Larry Itliong, went on strike. The union Dolores Huerta helped lead joined them. They asked families across the country to stop buying grapes. Many did! In 1970, grape growers signed agreements for better pay.

🤝 Let's Try Together: Sí, se puede!

In 1972, Dolores Huerta heard people say, "No, it can't be done." She answered, "Sí, se puede!" That is Spanish for "Yes, it can be done!" The words became a famous cheer. People still use it today. It means: together, we can make things fair.

🕵️ Source Detective: Rosa Parks in Her Own Words

Some people say Rosa Parks stayed in her seat because her feet were tired. Is that true? Read her own words and find out.

Primary Source: Rosa Parks, from her book about her own life

Rosa Parks wrote that people often said she kept her seat because she was tired. She said that was not true. Then she wrote:

"I was not tired physically, or no more tired than I usually was at the end of a working day. I was not old, although some people have an image of me as being old then. I was forty-two. No, the only tired I was, was tired of giving in."

Word help: physically = in my body. giving in = going along with something unfair.

Facts: Rosa Parks worked sewing clothes in a department store. She was also a leader in the Montgomery NAACP, a group that worked for civil rights.

Rosa Parks with Jim Haskins, Rosa Parks: My Story (1992), p. 116. The first two lines above are a summary; the words in quotation marks are hers.

👁 Think Like a Historian

A historian asks: who wrote this, and when? Rosa Parks wrote this herself, in a book about her life. That makes it a primary source. The "tired feet" story came from other people. When a person tells her own story, I trust her words more.

⚖️ Americans Who Worked for Fairness

Meet people who changed unfair rules. As you read, ask: What was unfair? What tool did they use? Who helped them?

Abraham Lincoln

1809–1865

The 16th President of the United States. He led the country during the Civil War. In 1863, he signed the Emancipation Proclamation. He pushed Congress to pass the 13th Amendment, which ended slavery. At first, his main goal was to keep the country together.

Harriet Tubman

about 1822–1913

She was born enslaved in Maryland. In 1849, she escaped to freedom. She went back about 13 times. She guided about 70 people to freedom on the Underground Railroad. It was a secret path of helpers and safe houses. In the Civil War, she was a scout and nurse. She helped free more than 700 people in one raid.

Rosa Parks

1913–2005

A civil rights leader in Montgomery, Alabama. On December 1, 1955, she would not give up her bus seat to a white rider. She was arrested. Her brave act helped start the Montgomery Bus Boycott. She kept working for fairness her whole life.

Martin Luther King Jr.

1929–1968

A pastor from Atlanta, Georgia. He led the bus boycott and taught nonviolence. In 1963, he gave his "I Have a Dream" speech to about 250,000 people. In 1964, he won the Nobel Peace Prize. He was killed in 1968. We honor him on Martin Luther King Jr. Day in January.

Dolores Huerta

born 1930

A teacher who grew up in California. She saw her students come to school hungry. So she worked to help their farmworker families. In 1962, she and César Chávez started a union for farmworkers. It became the United Farm Workers. She made up the cheer "Sí, se puede!"

Larry Itliong

1913–1977

He came to the United States from the Philippines at age 15. He worked on farms for many years. In 1965, he led Filipino American grape pickers on strike in Delano, California. He asked the union Dolores Huerta helped lead to join. Together, they won better pay.
