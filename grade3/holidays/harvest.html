🌕 Harvest Time and Chuseok

A harvest is when people gather ripe crops. Around the world, many people celebrate the harvest. They share food and give thanks. Not everyone celebrates the same days, and families celebrate in different ways.

👁 Watch Me Think

Let me look at Chuseok (say CHOO-suk) in Korea. It comes on the full moon in fall. This day follows a lunar calendar, so the date moves each year. I ask: why the full moon? The big, bright moon comes at harvest time. So the moon tells people when to celebrate.

🧩 Picture It: Chuseok in Korea

  • When: the 15th day of the 8th month on the lunar calendar, in September or early October
  • How long: 3 days off in South Korea
  • Food: songpyeon, small rice cakes shaped like half-moons, steamed on pine needles
  • Family: people travel to their family's hometown and honor their ancestors
  • Same full moon: families in China and Vietnam celebrate the Mid-Autumn Festival with mooncakes and lanterns

🤝 Let's Try Together: Same and Different

Let's compare Chuseok with U.S. Thanksgiving. Same: families gather and share food. They give thanks. Different: Chuseok follows the moon, so its date moves. U.S. Thanksgiving is always the fourth Thursday in November. Korean families make songpyeon. Many U.S. families make turkey.

🍚 Pongal in India and Homowo in Ghana

Pongal is a harvest festival of Tamil people. Many Tamil families in south India and Sri Lanka celebrate it. It comes in January. Homowo is the harvest festival of the Ga people of Accra, Ghana, in West Africa. It usually comes in August or September.

👁 Watch Me Think

I notice both names have meanings. Pongal means "boiling over." Families cook rice, milk and jaggery, a brown sugar, in a pot. When it bubbles over, they shout "Pongalo Pongal!" A full pot means plenty of food. Homowo means "hooting at hunger." Long ago, the Ga people had a famine, a time with too little food. Now they hoot at hunger because the harvest came.

🧩 Picture It: Two Harvest Festivals

Pongal

  • Who: Tamil people in India, Sri Lanka and around the world
  • When: January, for 3 or 4 days (one day honors cows and bulls)
  • Food: pongal, rice cooked with milk and jaggery

Homowo

  • Who: the Ga people of Ghana
  • When: usually August or September
  • Food: kpokpoi (say po-KPOY), made from corn and palm oil

🤝 Let's Try Together: Clues in a Name

A name can be a clue! Homowo means "hooting at hunger." What does that tell us? The Ga people remember a hard time with little food. Now they celebrate that the hunger ended. On Homowo, Ga chiefs walk through town and sprinkle kpokpoi on the ground. Drummers and singers walk with them.

🦃 The True Story of 1621

The Wampanoag people have lived in what is now Massachusetts for more than 12,000 years. In 1620, English colonists called Pilgrims came on the Mayflower. In fall 1621, they had a harvest. About 90 Wampanoag men and about 50 English people shared food for three days.

👁 Watch Me Think

I ask: whose story is this? Many old books tell only the Pilgrims' side. But that spring, the Wampanoag leader Ousamequin, also called Massasoit, made a peace agreement with the English. Each side promised to help the other. That is why his men came. Later, many more colonists came and took Wampanoag land. That part is true too.

🧩 Picture It: Timeline: Before and After 1621

YearWhat happened
1616–1619Sickness brought by European sailors kills many Wampanoag people.
1620The Mayflower arrives.
March 1621Ousamequin and the English agree to help each other.
Fall 1621A three-day harvest gathering.
1863President Lincoln calls for a national Thanksgiving Day.
1970Wampanoag leader Wamsutta Frank James begins the National Day of Mourning.

🤝 Let's Try Together: Two Ways to Spend One Day

On Thanksgiving, many families share a big meal and give thanks. Some families do not celebrate it. Since 1970, many Indigenous people gather in Plymouth, Massachusetts, on that same day. They call it the National Day of Mourning. They remember the land and lives their people lost. Both are part of the true story.

🕵️ Source Detective: A Letter from 1621

How do we know what happened in 1621? An English colonist named Edward Winslow was there. He wrote a letter to a friend in England. Let's read part of it.

Primary Source: Edward Winslow's Letter, December 1621

Edward Winslow wrote this letter in December 1621. It was printed in a book in 1622. Here is part of it in today's spelling. Words in [brackets] are not Winslow's. We added them to help you. Winslow used a different word for the Wampanoag, so we wrote [Wampanoag] in its place. The dots ... show where we left words out.

"Our harvest being gotten in, our governor sent four men on fowling [bird hunting]... many of the [Wampanoag] coming amongst us, and amongst the rest their greatest king Massasoit, with some ninety men, whom for three days we entertained and feasted, and they went out and killed five deer, which they brought to the plantation [the English town] and bestowed on [gave to] our governor..."

Word help: harvest being gotten in = the crops were picked. entertained and feasted = shared food and fun.

Edward Winslow, letter of December 1621, printed in Mourt's Relation (London, 1622). Modern spelling as published by Pilgrim Hall Museum, Plymouth, Massachusetts. Words in [brackets] are our edits.

👁 Think Like a Historian

A historian asks: who wrote this, and when? Edward Winslow was English, so we hear the English side. The Wampanoag did not write letters about this day. So I also listen to Wampanoag people today. They keep their history alive in stories.

🧑‍🤝‍🧑 People in the Thanksgiving Story

Meet four people from different times. As you read, ask: whose voice does each person add to the story?

Ousamequin (Massasoit)

died about 1661

He was the sachem, or leader, of the Pokanoket Wampanoag. Massasoit is a title. It means "great leader." In March 1621, he made a peace agreement with the English. In the fall, he and about 90 men joined the harvest meal. His statue stands in Plymouth today.

Tisquantum (Squanto)

died 1622

He was a Patuxet Wampanoag man. In 1614, an English captain kidnapped him and took him to Spain. He made his way to England and learned English. He came home in 1619, but sickness had emptied his village. He helped the English and Wampanoag talk. He showed the colonists how to plant corn with fish to feed the soil.

Sarah Josepha Hale

1788–1879

She was a writer and magazine editor. She wrote the poem "Mary Had a Little Lamb." For about 17 years, she wrote letters asking leaders for a national Thanksgiving. In 1863, President Abraham Lincoln said yes.

Wamsutta Frank James

1923–2001

He was an Aquinnah Wampanoag man. In 1970, he was asked to speak at a big dinner for the Pilgrims' 350th anniversary. Leaders would not let him say what he wrote about his people's losses. So on Thanksgiving Day, he spoke on a hill in Plymouth. That began the National Day of Mourning.
