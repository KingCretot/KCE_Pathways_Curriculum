🌟 Word Wrangler

Tap each card to flip it. Tap the 🔊 to hear the word and what it means.

map
A flat drawing of a place, seen from above.
globe
A round model of Earth. It shows the true shape of the land and water.
continent
One of Earth's 7 very large areas of land. Example: Africa.
ocean
One of Earth's 5 huge bodies of salt water. Example: the Pacific.
compass rose
A symbol on a map that shows directions: north, south, east and west.
map key
A box that tells what each symbol on a map means. It is also called a legend.
scale
A line or note on a map that shows real distance. Example: 1 inch = 1 mile.

🌍 Continents and Oceans

A globe is a round model of Earth. A map is a flat drawing. Earth has 7 continents and 5 oceans. Most of Earth is covered by water.

👁 Watch Me Think

Let me find where I live. I live in the United States. The United States is on the continent of North America. The Atlantic Ocean is to the east. The Pacific Ocean is to the west. So I can say: I live in North America, between two oceans.

🧭 The Compass Rose

A compass rose shows directions on a map. The four main directions are north, east, south and west. They are called cardinal directions. On most maps, north points up.

👁 Watch Me Think

I use a trick to remember the order: Never Eat Soggy Waffles. I start at the top and go around like a clock hand: north, east, south, west. If north is up, then south is down. East is on the right. West is on the left.

🗺️ Map Key, Title and Scale

A map has helpers. The title tells what the map shows. The map key tells what each symbol means. The scale tells how far apart places really are.

👁 Watch Me Think

Before I read a map, I check three things. First, the title: 'Lake Town Library Map.' Now I know what place it shows. Second, the key: a little book symbol means the reading room. Third, the scale: 1 inch = 10 feet. Two rooms are 2 inches apart on the map. So they are 20 feet apart in real life.

🧩 Picture It: Parts of a Map

  • Title — the name of the map
  • Map key (legend) — what the symbols mean
  • Compass rose — which way is north, south, east and west
  • Scale — how far apart places really are

🧭 Explorers and Mapmakers

People have mapped the world in many ways. Meet some of them. As you read, ask: Whose knowledge made the trip possible?

Matthew Henson

1866–1955

Matthew Henson was a Black explorer from Maryland. He made many trips to the Arctic with Robert Peary. He learned the Inuit language and drove dog sleds. In 1909, his team reached what they believed was the North Pole. For many years, Peary got most of the credit.

Ootah, Egingwah, Seegloo and Ooqueah

Inuit team members, 1909

These four Inuit men from Greenland were on the final team with Henson and Peary. Inuit people knew how to travel on sea ice, build sleds and stay warm in deep cold. Without Inuit knowledge, the trip could not have happened.

Indigenous trail makers

for thousands of years

Indigenous Peoples knew this land long before Europeans arrived. They made trails and knew river routes for travel and trade. The Chickasaw and Choctaw used a path now called the Natchez Trace. Many roads today follow old Indigenous trails.

Marshall Islands navigators

Pacific Ocean, long ago to today

People of the Marshall Islands made maps from sticks and shells, called stick charts. The sticks showed how ocean waves bend around islands. The shells showed where islands were. Sailors studied them before a trip.
