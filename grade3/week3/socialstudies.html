📏 Why Do We Have Rules?

A rule tells people what to do. Rules keep us safe. Rules help us take turns and get along. A rule for a whole town, state or country is called a law.

👁 Watch Me Think

Let me think about a stop sign. What if there were no stop signs? Cars would not know who goes first. People could get hurt. So the stop sign keeps people safe. When I see a rule, I ask one question: What problem does this rule solve?

⚖️ Fair or Unfair?

A fair rule treats everyone the same way. An unfair rule treats some people worse for no good reason. Long ago, some laws kept children in separate schools because of their skin color. This was called segregation, and it was unfair.

👁 Watch Me Think

I use a Fairness Test with two questions. Question 1: Does the rule treat everyone the same? Question 2: Does the rule have a good reason, like safety? 'Everyone wears a helmet on a bike' passes both. 'Only kids with brown hair may use the slide' fails both. Hair color has nothing to do with safety.

🗳️ How Unfair Rules Get Changed

People can change unfair rules in peaceful ways. They can speak up and write letters. They can vote for leaders who will change the law. They can go to court, where judges decide if a law is fair.

👁 Watch Me Think

Let me think about how school segregation ended. Families went to court. A lawyer named Thurgood Marshall argued their case. In 1954, the U.S. Supreme Court said separate schools were unfair. That changed the rule. But a new rule is only the start. People still had to be brave and follow it, like Ruby Bridges did in 1960.

🧩 Picture It: From Unfair to Fair: A Timeline

  • 1946–1947 — Courts say California schools may not keep Mexican American children apart. (Mendez v. Westminster)
  • 1954 — The U.S. Supreme Court says segregated schools are unfair. (Brown v. Board of Education)
  • 1960 — Ruby Bridges, age 6, starts at William Frantz Elementary in New Orleans.
  • 1967 — Thurgood Marshall becomes the first Black justice on the Supreme Court.

🕵️ Source Detective: A Famous Painting

In 1963, an artist named Norman Rockwell made a painting about Ruby Bridges's walk to school. It was printed in a magazine in 1964. A painting is a kind of source. Read the description slowly, like a detective.

Source: A Painting Printed in 1964

What you see in the painting:

  • A small Black girl walks in the middle. She wears a white dress and white shoes.
  • She carries a ruler and school books.
  • Four tall men walk with her. Two walk in front and two walk behind. They wear yellow armbands.
  • You cannot see the men's faces. The painting stops at their shoulders.
  • Behind them is a wall. It has a red splat where someone threw a tomato. Mean words are written on the wall.
  • The girl looks straight ahead.
Norman Rockwell, The Problem We All Live With, painted in 1963. First printed in Look magazine on January 14, 1964. Today it belongs to the Norman Rockwell Museum in Stockbridge, Massachusetts.

👁 Think Like a Historian

A historian asks four questions. Who made this? When? What do I see? What does it mean? Rockwell was not there in 1960. He painted it about three years later. So the painting shows how he saw the event. To check the facts, historians also use photos, news stories and Ruby's own words.

🌟 People Who Stood Up for Fair Rules

Meet four people who helped end unfair school rules. Each one used a peaceful way. Ask: Who helped, and who was helped?

Ruby Bridges

born 1954

In 1960, Ruby was 6 years old. She became the first Black child at William Frantz Elementary School in New Orleans. Federal marshals walked with her to keep her safe. Some grown-ups stood outside and yelled. Ruby kept coming to school every day.

Barbara Henry

Ruby's teacher, 1960–1961

Mrs. Henry was a white teacher from Boston. Many families kept their children home from Ruby's school. So Mrs. Henry taught Ruby alone, all year. She was kind and patient with her.

Thurgood Marshall

1908–1993

Thurgood Marshall was a lawyer. He helped Black families go to court. In 1954, he won Brown v. Board of Education. The Supreme Court said segregated schools were unfair. In 1967, he became the first Black justice on the Supreme Court.

Sylvia Mendez

born 1936

When Sylvia was 8, a school in Westminster, California, turned her away. She was sent to a separate school for Mexican American children. Her father was from Mexico and her mother was from Puerto Rico. Her parents, Gonzalo and Felicitas Mendez, went to court with other families. In 1946, a judge said this was unfair. In 1947, a higher court agreed.
