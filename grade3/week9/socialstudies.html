🏠 Many Nations, Many Homes

Indigenous Peoples lived in the Americas for thousands of years before Europeans arrived. There were hundreds of different nations. Each nation built homes that fit its land and climate. They used materials from nearby.

👁 Watch Me Think

I ask: what did the land give them? In the Pacific Northwest, there were huge cedar trees. So nations there built big houses from cedar planks. In the dry Southwest, there was stone and clay. So Ancestral Puebloans built stone homes in the cliffs. The land shaped the home.

🧩 Picture It: Homes That Fit the Land

HomeWho built it, where, and what it is made of
Cliff dwellingAncestral Puebloans in the Southwest: stone and adobe (mud brick)
Plank houseHaida, Tlingit and other nations of the Pacific Northwest: cedar planks
LonghouseHaudenosaunee in the Northeast: wood poles and elm bark
ChickeeSeminole and Miccosukee in Florida: cypress posts and a palm-leaf roof

🤝 Let's Try Together: Why Does It Fit?

Look at the chickee. It has a raised floor and open sides. Florida is hot and rainy. Why open sides? Breezes blow through and keep people cool. Why a raised floor? It stays above the wet ground. The chickee fits Florida's climate. Seminole and Miccosukee builders still make chickees today.

🌽 Foods and Languages

Each nation had its own foods, stories and language. Indigenous farmers in what is now Mexico first grew corn, thousands of years ago. Many English words come from Indigenous languages. Many of these languages are still spoken today.

👁 Watch Me Think

Let me check where a word comes from. Raccoon comes from the Powhatan language of Virginia. Tomato comes from tomatl in Nahuatl, the language of the Mexica. Okeechobee, the big lake in Florida, comes from Hitchiti words that mean "big water." Many Seminole ancestors spoke Hitchiti. Indigenous words are all around us!

🤝 Let's Try Together: Word Detective

The word canoe comes from the Taíno people of the Caribbean. The Taíno made long boats from tree trunks. Spanish sailors arrived in 1492 and heard the word. They took it home, and later English used it too. Words travel, just like people do!

🏛️ Indigenous Nations Today

Indigenous nations are not only in the past. Today there are more than 570 tribal nations in the United States. Each has its own tribal government, with leaders and laws. Indigenous people are doctors, teachers, artists, farmers and leaders today.

👁 Watch Me Think

Let me check a sentence: "The Cherokee lived in the Southeast." Is that the whole story? No! In the 1830s, many Cherokee were forced to move west. The Cherokee Nation is strong today. Its capital is Tahlequah, Oklahoma. It has a Principal Chief and its own laws. A better sentence: "The Cherokee have lived here for thousands of years, and they still do."

Two Cherokee words:

  • ᎣᏏᏲ = o-si-yo = "hello"
  • ᏣᎳᎩ = tsa-la-gi = "Cherokee"

Facts: The syllabary has 85 symbols today. The Cherokee Nation made it official in 1825. In 1828, the Cherokee Phoenix newspaper was printed in Cherokee and English.

Cherokee syllabary created by Sequoyah, about 1821 (symbols shown in modern print). Cherokee Phoenix newspaper, New Echota, Cherokee Nation, first printed in 1828.

🌎 First Peoples, Then and Now

Meet people who built, wrote and led. As you read, ask: How do we know their story? Who keeps it alive today?

Ancestral Puebloans

long ago to about 1300; their families live on

The Ancestral Puebloans lived in the Four Corners, where Arizona, New Mexico, Colorado and Utah meet. In the 1200s, they built stone homes in the cliffs at Mesa Verde. They grew corn, beans and squash. Scientists have found old corn cobs in their homes. Their descendants include the Hopi, the Zuni and the Pueblos of New Mexico.

Sequoyah

about 1770–1843

Sequoyah was a Cherokee silversmith. He did not read or write English. He saw that writing let people share ideas, so he worked for about 12 years to make symbols for Cherokee sounds. He finished his syllabary in about 1821. Soon, thousands of Cherokee people could read and write their own language.

Wilma Mankiller

1945–2010

Wilma Mankiller was born in Oklahoma. In 1985, she became the first woman Principal Chief of the Cherokee Nation. She worked with families to build water lines, health clinics and schools. In 2022, her picture was put on a U.S. quarter.

Maria Martinez

1887–1980

Maria Martinez was a potter from San Ildefonso Pueblo in New Mexico. Pueblo people are descendants of the Ancestral Puebloans. With her husband, Julian, she made shiny black-on-black pottery. She taught her family, and they still make pottery today.
