🗺️ Five Regions, One Big Country

A region is an area where places share features, like landforms, climate or ways people live. Many maps for students split the United States into five regions: the Northeast, Southeast, Midwest, Southwest and West. Alaska and Hawaii are part of the West.

👁 Watch Me Think

Javier finds Texas in the Southwest on his school map. But the United States Census Bureau, the government office that counts people, puts Texas in a region called the South. The Census Bureau uses only four regions: the Northeast, Midwest, South and West. So who is right? Both! People draw region lines in different ways for different jobs. I always ask: Who made this map, and why?

🏛️ States and Their Capitals

The United States has 50 states. Each state has a capital, the city where the state government works. The governor and the state lawmakers meet there. On many maps, a star marks a capital. Washington, D.C., is the capital of the whole country. It is not a state.

👁 Watch Me Think

Jamila guesses that New York City is the capital of New York, because it is the biggest city. Let me check the map key. The star is on Albany! The capital is not always the biggest city. Florida's capital is Tallahassee, but its biggest city is Jacksonville. Illinois's capital is Springfield, not Chicago. But sometimes the capital is the biggest city, like Phoenix in Arizona.

🤝 Let's Try Together: The Last States to Join

The 50 states did not all join at once. The first 13 states had been the 13 colonies. Arizona became the 48th state in 1912. Alaska and Hawaii both became states in 1959, which made 50. How many years after Arizona did Hawaii join? 1959 minus 1912 is 47. So it was 47 years later.

⛰️ Land and Resources, Part 1: Northeast, Southeast and Midwest

A landform is a natural shape of the land. A natural resource is something from nature that people use. The Appalachian Mountains run through the Northeast and the Southeast. The Midwest has wide, flat plains and the Great Lakes. The five Great Lakes hold about one-fifth of all the fresh water on the surface of Earth. Native nations have lived in every region for thousands of years, like the Haudenosaunee and Wampanoag in the Northeast, the Seminole and Cherokee in the Southeast, and the Ojibwe around the Great Lakes.

👁 Watch Me Think

Why do Midwest farms grow so much corn? I look at the land: wide, flat plains, rich soil and summer rain. Three Midwest states, Iowa, Illinois and Indiana, grow about 2 out of every 5 bushels of corn in the whole country. Land and climate help decide how people earn a living.

🏜️ Land and Resources, Part 2: Southwest and West

The Southwest has deserts, high flat lands called plateaus, and the Grand Canyon in Arizona. The Colorado River carved it over millions of years. It is about 278 river miles long and, on average, about 1 mile deep. The West has the tall Rocky Mountains, the Pacific coast and even volcanoes in Hawaii.

👁 Watch Me Think

Who lives in these lands? The Grand Canyon is home to 11 Native nations that the United States government recognizes. Nearby, the Navajo Nation covers more than 27,000 square miles in Arizona, New Mexico and Utah. It is the largest reservation in the United States. The Navajo people call themselves the Diné.

🧩 Picture It: Land and Resources in Two Regions

🕵️ Source Detective: A Poem About the Land

In 1893, a teacher named Katharine Lee Bates rode a wagon, and then a mule, to the top of Pikes Peak in Colorado. The view gave her the idea for a poem. It was first printed on July 4, 1895. Set to music, it became the song "America the Beautiful."

Primary Source: "America the Beautiful" by Katharine Lee Bates (first printed in 1895; final words in 1913)

"O beautiful for spacious skies,
For amber waves of grain,
For purple mountain majesties
Above the fruited plain!"

The verse ends: "From sea to shining sea!"

Word help: spacious = wide and open. amber = golden yellow. waves of grain = fields of wheat blowing in the wind. majesties = grand, amazing sights. fruited plain = flat land full of crops. from sea to shining sea = from the Atlantic Ocean to the Pacific Ocean.

Another voice: Long before 1893, the Ute people named this mountain Tavá, the Sun Mountain, because it was the first land to meet the sunrise each day. The Ute are the oldest people still living in Colorado today.

Katharine Lee Bates, "America the Beautiful," first printed in The Congregationalist on July 4, 1895, with a final version in 1913 (Library of Congress and National Park Service). The Ute name: Visit Colorado Springs, "The Ute and Pikes Peak."

👁 Think Like a Historian

Let me read the poem like a geographer. "Amber waves of grain" sounds like the wheat fields of the plains. "Purple mountain majesties" sounds like the Rocky Mountains, where Pikes Peak stands. "From sea to shining sea" means from one ocean to the other. Now I ask: Whose view is this? Bates was a visitor. The Ute people had a name for the mountain long before. A historian listens to both voices.

🌟 People Who Knew the Land

Meet four people from four different regions. As you read, ask: How did the land and water of their region shape their lives?

Paul Cuffe

1759–1817

He was born on Cuttyhunk Island, Massachusetts, in the Northeast. His father, Kofi, was born in West Africa, was enslaved and later became free. His mother was Wampanoag. Paul became a sea captain who built and owned ships. In 1780, he and his brother asked Massachusetts leaders for the right to vote, because they paid taxes. He also built a school on his own land for Black and white children.

Jean Baptiste Point du Sable

about 1745–1818

He was likely born in Haiti. Around 1779, he built a home and trading post by the Chicago River, in the Midwest. He was the first person who was not Indigenous to settle there, and he is honored as the founder of Chicago. His wife, Kitihawa, was likely Potawatomi. Together they ran the farm and trading post near the mouth of the Chicago River.

Annie Dodge Wauneka

1910–1997

She was Navajo, from the Southwest. In 1951, she became one of the first women elected to the Navajo Tribal Council. She taught families about a sickness called tuberculosis, and she gave health talks on the radio in the Navajo language. In 1963, she received the Presidential Medal of Freedom.

Sacagawea

born about 1788

She was Lemhi Shoshone, born in what is now Idaho, in the West. From 1805 to 1806, she traveled with the Lewis and Clark expedition as an interpreter, carrying her baby son, Jean Baptiste, on her back. William Clark wrote that she had been "of great Service to me as a pilot through this Country." (Spelling and capital letters were less fixed back then.)
