🌙 Why the Moon Changes Shape

The Moon does not make its own light. Moonlight is really sunlight bouncing off the Moon. The Sun always lights up half of the Moon, the half that faces the Sun. The Moon orbits, or travels around, Earth. It takes about 27.3 days for the Moon to orbit Earth. As it moves, we see different amounts of its lit half. The different shapes we see are called the Moon's phases. It takes about 29.5 days to go from one new moon to the next new moon.

👁 Watch Me Think

Look at the picture. After a new moon, the lit part we see grows a little each night. Waxing means the lit part is growing. After the full moon, the lit part gets smaller each night. Waning means the lit part is shrinking. Is Earth's shadow making the dark part? No! That is a common mistake. The dark part is simply the half of the Moon that faces away from the Sun.

⭐ Our Sun Is a Star

A star is a huge, hot ball of glowing gas that makes its own light. Our Sun is a star! It is the only star in our solar system. The Sun is about 93 million miles from Earth. The next closest star, Proxima Centauri, is about 4.24 light-years away. A light-year is the distance light travels in one year, about 6 trillion miles. That is why other stars look like tiny dots: they are very, very far away.

👁 Watch Me Think

Is the Sun the biggest star? No. The Sun is a medium-sized star. Scientists have found stars about 1,000 times wider than the Sun, and stars only one tenth as wide. Stars also come in different colors. The hottest stars glow blue, and cooler stars glow red. The Sun looks big and bright to us only because it is so much closer than any other star.

✨ Constellations and the North Star

A constellation is a group of stars that people imagined as a picture and gave a name. The stars in a constellation are not connected. Some are much farther away from us than others. Over time, cultures around the world have named different star pictures. Today there are 88 official constellations. The ones you can see depend on where you live on Earth and the time of year.

👁 Watch Me Think

How do I find the North Star? First I find the Big Dipper in the northern part of the sky. It looks like a big ladle, or dipper, for soup. Then I find the two stars at the end of the cup, away from the handle. They are called the pointer stars, because when I line them up, they point to Polaris, the North Star.

🏠 Try It at Home: Moon Phase Model

Make a model to see why the Moon has phases. You need a lamp with the shade taken off, a dark room, and a foam ball that a grown-up pushes onto a pencil. The lamp is the Sun. The ball is the Moon. Your head is Earth! A grown-up sets up and handles the lamp.

👁 Watch Me Think

I stand facing the lamp and hold the ball out at arm's length, a little above my head. When the ball is between me and the lamp, I see its dark side: that is the new moon. I turn slowly to my left, a quarter turn. Now the lamp is on my right, and I see the right half of the ball lit: first quarter. Another quarter turn puts the lamp behind me, and the whole face I see is lit: full moon.

Source: U.S. Naval Observatory, Astronomical Applications Department, Moon phase data for 2027. (The Naval Observatory calls the third quarter the "last quarter.")

👁 Think Like a Scientist

Counting on the calendar, from the new moon on January 7 to the new moon on February 6 is 30 days. That is close to the average of about 29.5 days. Each phase happens at a certain hour, not always at midnight, so counting whole days can come out a little long or a little short.

🔭 Annie Jump Cannon, Sorter of Stars

She sorted more stars than almost anyone in history.

Annie Jump Cannon

1863–1941

Annie Jump Cannon was born in 1863 in Dover, Delaware. Her mother first taught her the constellations. She studied physics and astronomy at Wellesley College. After an illness called scarlet fever, she became nearly deaf, but she kept working. At the Harvard College Observatory, she studied photographs of starlight and sorted stars into groups called O, B, A, F, G, K, and M. Scientists later learned that the groups go in order of temperature, from O, the hottest, to M, the coolest. She classified about 350,000 stars in her lifetime. Our Sun is a G star.

🔎 More People Who Study the Sky

These people looked up and asked, "What is out there?"

Abd al-Rahman al-Sufi

903–986

A Persian astronomer who wrote The Book of Fixed Stars in about 964. It describes more than 1,000 stars and shows pictures of the constellations. In it, he wrote about a "little cloud" near the constellation Andromeda. Today we know it is the Andromeda Galaxy. His note is the earliest known record of a galaxy beyond our own Milky Way.

Beth A. Brown

1969–2008

An astrophysicist at NASA's Goddard Space Flight Center. She grew up loving space and studied physics and astronomy at Howard University. In 1998, she became the first African American woman to earn a doctorate in astronomy from the University of Michigan. She studied galaxies and worked to help more young people become scientists.

Māori star watchers

Aotearoa (New Zealand)

For a very long time, Māori people have watched the Pleiades star cluster, which they call Matariki. When Matariki rises before dawn in June or July, it marks the start of the Māori New Year, a time to remember ancestors and celebrate the harvest. In 2022, New Zealand held its first Matariki public holiday.
