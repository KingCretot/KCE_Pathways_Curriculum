Tale 1 · Told by Fafa · Akan people, Ghana
Anansi and the Pot of Wisdom
A folktale of the Akan people of Ghana
1

Long ago, the Akan people of Ghana say, Kwaku Anansi the spider decided to gather all the wisdom in the world. He went from place to place and collected every bit of good sense and clever thinking he could find. He put it all into one big clay pot.

2

“If I keep all the wisdom,” Anansi thought, “everyone will have to come to me for help.” He did not want to share. So he decided to hide the pot at the top of the tallest tree in the forest, where no one else could reach it.

3

Anansi tied the pot to the front of his body and began to climb. But the big pot was in the way. Every time he reached up, the pot bumped against the tree trunk, and he slid back down. He tried again and again.

4

Anansi’s young son, Ntikuma, was watching from below. “Father,” he called, “why don’t you tie the pot on your back? Then you could climb easily.” Anansi stopped. His son was right! Anansi had a pot full of wisdom, but a child had found the answer he could not see.

5

Anansi felt angry and embarrassed. In his anger, he threw the pot down, and it broke. The wisdom spilled out. Rain washed it into the rivers, and it spread all over the world. That is why, the story says, no one has all the wisdom, but everyone has a little.

6

Anansi stories are so loved that the Akan people call folktales Anansesem, which means “spider stories.” Anansi stories later traveled to the Caribbean and the Americas, where people still tell them. Storytellers tell this tale in different ways. In one old version, Anansi’s son is named Kweku Tsin.

Tale 2 · Told by Fernando · Puerto Rico
Juan Bobo Goes to Work
A folktale from Puerto Rico
1

Juan Bobo lived with his mother in a small house in the countryside of Puerto Rico. One morning, his mother said, “Juan, it is time for you to find work.” So off he went.

2

A farmer paid Juan Bobo a few coins for shelling beans. Juan put the coins in his pocket, but his pocket had holes. By the time he got home, every coin had fallen out. “Ay, Juan!” said his mother. “Next time, carry your pay in a sack.”

3

The next day, a dairy farmer paid Juan with a pail of milk. Juan remembered his mother’s words, so he poured the milk into a sack! Drip, drip, drip. By the time he got home, the sack was empty. “Ay, Juan!” said his mother. “Next time, carry it on your head.”

4

The next day, the grocer paid Juan with a big piece of cheese for sweeping the floor. Juan put the cheese on his head and walked home under the hot sun. The cheese melted and dripped down his face. “Ay, Juan!” said his mother. “Next time, tie a string around it.”

5

The next day, Juan was paid with a whole ham. He tied a string around the ham and dragged it home along the dusty road. The neighborhood cats and dogs followed him and nibbled all the way. When he got home, only the string was left! His mother shook her head. Each thing Juan carried needed its own way, and he had to think about it.

6

People in Puerto Rico have told funny Juan Bobo stories for many generations. His name means something like “Silly John.” Juan Bobo is not a real person. He is a story character whose mistakes make listeners laugh and teach a lesson. Different storytellers tell this tale in different ways.

Tale 3 · Told by Fallon · Japan
Momotarō, the Peach Boy
A folktale from Japan
1

Long ago in Japan, an old man and an old woman had no children. One day, the old man went to the mountains to gather firewood, and the old woman went to the river to wash clothes. Suddenly, a giant peach came floating down the river! She carried it home.

2

When the couple cut the peach open, they found a healthy baby boy inside. They were overjoyed! They named him Momotarō, which means “Peach Boy.” Momotarō grew up kind and strong.

3

One day, Momotarō learned that ogres, called oni, were troubling the villages and taking people’s treasures. The ogres lived on Onigashima, the Island of Ogres. Momotarō decided to go and stop them. For his journey, his mother made kibi dango, millet dumplings.

4

On the way, Momotarō met a dog. “Please give me one of your dumplings,” said the dog, “and I will come with you.” Momotarō shared a dumpling, and the dog joined him. Next he met a monkey, and then a pheasant, a colorful bird. Each one asked for a dumpling. Momotarō shared with each one, and each one joined the team.

5

At the island, the dog, the monkey, and the pheasant each used their own skills, and Momotarō was brave. Working together, they beat the ogres. The ogre chief gave up and promised to stop causing trouble.

6

The ogres gave back the treasures they had taken. Momotarō and his friends carried them home, and the village celebrated. Today in Okayama, Japan, a statue of Momotarō and his three animal friends stands in front of Okayama Station, and kibi dango are a well-known treat there.

🌍 What Is a Folktale?, Part 1

A folktale is a made-up story that people told out loud for many years before anyone wrote it down. Each folktale comes from a culture, the way of life of a group of people. Folktales often have patterns, like events that repeat, and they usually teach a lesson or explain why something is the way it is. Because people told them out loud, storytellers tell the same tale in different ways.

🔁 Retelling a Folktale, Part 1

When you retell a story, you tell it again in your own words. A good retelling has the beginning, the middle, and the end, in the right order. Keep the most important events, and leave out small details. Do not add new events or change what happens. When a story comes from another culture, retell it accurately and respectfully: use the real names of the characters, places, and foods.

💡 Finding the Central Message, Part 1

The central message is the big lesson a story teaches. To find it, ask: What problem does the main character have? What does the character do, and what happens? What does the character learn? Then write the lesson as a full sentence that could help anyone, like “It is better to share than to keep everything for yourself.” One word, like “peaches,” is a topic, not a message. Use details from the story to prove it.

👁 Watch Me Think

Anansi tried to keep all the wisdom for himself. Then his young son gave him an idea he had not thought of. The pot broke, and wisdom spread everywhere. Anansi learned he did not have all the wisdom after all. So the central message is: No one has all the wisdom, so we should share and learn from others.

⚖️ Comparing Tales From Three Cultures, Part 1

To compare folktales, look for what is alike and what is different. All three of today’s tales were passed down out loud, and each one teaches a lesson. 👁 Watch Me Think
