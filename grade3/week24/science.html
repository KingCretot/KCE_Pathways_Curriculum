🌋 Environments Change

An environment is everything around a living thing. Environments change all the time. Some changes are natural, like a drought, a flood, a wildfire, or a volcano erupting. Some changes are caused by people, like building roads and towns, draining wetlands, polluting water, or bringing in plants and animals from far away. When an environment changes, three things can happen: some living things survive and stay, some move away, and some die. Over time, many places can recover.

👁 Watch Me Think

On May 18, 1980, a volcano called Mount St. Helens erupted in Washington State. Ash and rock covered the land for miles. Did everything die? No! Some toads were resting underground, and they survived. Then, in June 1982, a scientist named Charlie Crisafulli spotted one prairie lupine blooming on the bare ash. Lupines add nitrogen, a plant food, to poor soil. That helped other plants grow, and then more animals came back.

🐍 Invasive Species: Pythons in the Everglades

A native species has lived in a place naturally for a very long time. An invasive species is brought to a new place, spreads, and harms the living things there. Burmese pythons are native to Southeast Asia, in places like India, southern China, and the Malay Peninsula. People brought them to Florida to sell as pets, and some escaped or were let go. The first python was recorded in Everglades National Park in 1979. By 2000, scientists confirmed pythons were breeding there. Most adult pythons caught in Florida are 6 to 9 feet long, but some have been over 18 feet! They eat mammals, birds, and even alligators.

👁 Watch Me Think

Why did the number of pythons grow so fast? A female python lays about 40 eggs a year, on average. And very few animals in Florida can eat a big adult python. So more and more pythons hatched and grew, and they ate more and more native animals. That is what makes a species invasive: it spreads and causes harm.

🌱 Helping Habitats Recover

People can help a changed habitat recover. With today's tools, scientists cannot remove every python, but they can keep their numbers down. Trained hunters and scientists remove pythons, and the Florida Python Challenge invites trained grown-ups from the public to help. Thousands of pythons have been removed. A pet owner who can no longer keep an exotic pet can give it to the Exotic Pet Amnesty Program of the Florida Fish and Wildlife Conservation Commission, with no penalty, instead of letting it go. In 2000, Congress approved a big plan to restore the Everglades, which includes sending more fresh water south to Everglades National Park. Planting native plants helps too.

👁 Watch Me Think

How do you find a python hiding in a huge swamp? Biologists at the Conservancy of Southwest Florida use scout snakes. They put a small radio transmitter in a male python and let him go. Males go looking for the biggest females, so the scientists follow the radio signal and remove the females, along with all the eggs they would have laid.

🏠 Try It at Home: The Invasion Game

Play the Invasion Game to see how an invasive species can change a habitat fast. You need small squares of paper in two colors. Green squares are native animals. Red squares are pythons. A paper plate is the habitat. A grown-up helps cut the paper.

👁 Watch Me Think

Start with 20 green squares and 1 red square on the plate. Each round, every python eats 2 native animals, so I take away 2 green squares for each red one. Then 1 new python hatches, so I add 1 red square. Round 1: 1 python eats 2, so 20 minus 2 leaves 18. Now there are 2 pythons. Round 2: 2 pythons eat 4, so 18 minus 4 leaves 14. Now there are 3 pythons.

🧪 Lab: The Everglades Mammal Survey

Scientists drove roads in Everglades National Park at night and counted the mammals they saw. They compared what was seen before 2000 with what was seen from 2003 to 2011, after pythons had spread.

Night Road Survey: How Sightings Changed

Sightings in 2003 to 2011, compared with before 2000.

MammalChangeWhat it means

Source: a 2012 study by Dorcas and other scientists in the science journal Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences, listed by the U.S. Geological Survey (USGS).

👁 Think Like a Scientist

The drop happened in the same places and at the same time that pythons spread. Mammals were more common where pythons had only just arrived. That is strong evidence, so scientists think pythons caused the drop.

🐍 The Irula Snake Trackers

Two expert snake trackers came all the way from India to help Florida find pythons.

Masi Sadaiyan and Vadivel Gopal

snake trackers

Masi Sadaiyan and Vadivel Gopal belong to the Irula people, a tribe of southern India famous for its skill at finding and catching snakes. In January 2017, they came to Florida for a two-month project with the University of Florida, paid for by the Florida Fish and Wildlife Conservation Commission. Instead of searching only along roads, they searched thick brush for tiny clues, like a ripple in the sand, a tunnel through the grass, or a sparkle of snakeskin. In their first two weeks, they caught 14 Burmese pythons, including a 16-foot female.

🔎 More Scientists Who Help Habitats

These scientists ask, "What happened to this place, and how can it recover?"

Ian Bartoszek

wildlife biologist today

A biologist with the Conservancy of Southwest Florida. His team started its python program in 2013 and uses scout snakes to find big females. In 2022, a scout snake led them to a female python that weighed about 215 pounds, the heaviest ever caught in Florida. She was nearly 18 feet long and carried 122 developing eggs. By then the program had removed more than 1,000 pythons.

Virginia Dale

ecologist today

An ecologist who was on the first team of scientists to reach the blast zone after Mount St. Helens erupted in 1980. She went back again and again to see which plants returned. She has counted more than 150 kinds of wildflowers, shrubs, and trees growing back.

Charlie Crisafulli

ecologist today

An ecologist with the U.S. Forest Service. In June 1982, on the bare ash at Mount St. Helens, he spotted a single blooming prairie lupine with seedlings around it. Within four years, the lupines in his study area had grown to about 16,000 plants.
