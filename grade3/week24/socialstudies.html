💵 Earn, Save, Spend, Share and Borrow

People earn money by working. Then they choose what to do with it. They can spend it on needs and wants, save it for later, or share it to help others. Sometimes people borrow money, and borrowed money must be paid back. A plan for your money is called a budget.

👁 Watch Me Think

Haruto earns $12 raking leaves. His budget: $6 to save, $4 to spend and $2 to share. 6 + 4 + 2 = 12, so every dollar has a job. He wants a $30 bike helmet. If he saves $6 each week, how long will it take? 30 divided by 6 is 5. Five weeks!

🏦 What Do Banks Do?

A bank is a business that keeps money safe. When you put money into a bank account, that is a deposit. The bank does not just lock your money away! It lends some of it as loans to people who want to buy a house or a car, or to start a business.

👁 Watch Me Think

Why would a bank pay me for saving? The bank uses saved money to make loans. Borrowers pay the bank back, plus extra money called interest. The bank shares some of that with savers. So savers earn interest, and borrowers pay interest. Haruto keeps $100 in a savings account. In one year, the bank pays him $2 of interest. Now he has $102.

🤝 Let's Try Together: Is Money Safe in a Bank?

Long ago, if a bank failed, people could lose all their savings. In 1933, during the Great Depression, a new law created the Federal Deposit Insurance Corporation, or FDIC. It protects savings in the banks it insures. The FDIC says that since it began, no one has lost a penny of their insured deposits.

🐚 Money in America, Part 1: Before Our Coins

Long before coins, Native nations traded with each other across long distances. Native peoples along the Atlantic coast made wampum, small beads cut from white whelk shells and purple quahog clam shells. Wampum also traveled inland through trade to nations such as the Haudenosaunee, who wove it into belts.

👁 Watch Me Think

Was wampum just money? Not to Native nations. Wampum belts recorded treaties and agreements, and they were used in ceremonies. But in the 1600s there were not enough coins, so Dutch and English colonists began using wampum beads as money. In Massachusetts, a law let people pay with wampum starting in 1637. The colony stopped that law in 1661.

🤝 Let's Try Together: "Not Worth a Continental"

In 1775, the Continental Congress printed paper money, called Continentals, to pay for the Revolutionary War. It printed too much. Each bill bought less and less, and people stopped trusting it. Soon people said something useless was not worth a Continental. Lesson: money only works when people trust it.

🪙 Money in America, Part 2: Dollars, Banks and New Faces

In 1792, Congress passed the Mint Act. It set up our coin system of dollars and cents and created the United States Mint in Philadelphia. Its first director was a scientist, David Rittenhouse. In March 1793, the Mint delivered its first coins: 11,178 copper cents. In 1861, the government printed Demand Notes, nicknamed "greenbacks" for their green ink.

👁 Watch Me Think

Did banks treat everyone fairly? Not always. In 1865, President Lincoln signed a law that created the Freedman's Savings Bank for newly free Black Americans. More than 70,000 people saved money there. Frederick Douglass became its last president. But the bank closed in 1874. The government had not promised to protect the savings, so many families lost money they had worked hard to save. That is one reason protections like the FDIC matter.

🕵️ Source Detective: A Coin and the Constitution

Both of these sources come from the same year, 1787. One is an object you can hold. The other is a document.

Primary Sources: The Fugio Cent and the Constitution (both 1787)

Source 1: The Fugio cent (a coin from 1787, the first coin approved by Congress)

Front: a sun, a sundial, the word "FUGIO" and the words "Mind Your Business." Back: a chain of 13 links around the words "We Are One."

Word help: Fugio = Latin for "I fly," meaning time flies. Mind your business = take care of your work. The 13 links = the 13 states joined together.

Source 2: The Constitution, Article I, Section 8 (1787, part)

"To coin Money, regulate the Value thereof, and of foreign Coin ..."

Word help: coin Money = make coins. regulate the Value = decide what money is worth. foreign Coin = money from other countries.

Simple drawings of the coin’s two sides, not photos. The real coin has more details.

Fugio cent, 1787, the first coin authorized by Congress (examples are in the National Numismatic Collection at the Smithsonian). Constitution of the United States, Article I, Section 8 (signed in 1787), National Archives.

👁 Think Like a Historian

Let me compare. Both sources are from 1787. Source 1 is an object, and its words and pictures give advice: time flies, so work hard, and the states are stronger together. Source 2 is a document. It gives Congress the power to make coins. That is why our coins come from one national Mint, not from each state. I also ask: who decided what goes on our money?

🌟 People in the Story of American Money

Meet four people connected to American money. As you read, ask: What did each person do, and whose story does it help tell?

A. P. Giannini

1870–1949

He was born in San Jose, California, to Italian immigrant parents. In 1904, he started the Bank of Italy in San Francisco. It lent money to small farmers and business owners. After the 1906 earthquake and fire, he opened again before most other banks. His bank later became the Bank of America.

Maya Angelou

1928–2014

She was a poet and writer whose words inspired many people. In January 2022, her quarter was the first coin of the American Women Quarters. It made her the first Black woman shown on a United States quarter.

Nina Otero-Warren

1881–1965

She was born near Los Lunas, New Mexico, into a Hispanic family. She worked for women's right to vote and led the public schools in Santa Fe. In 1922, she was the first Latina to run for the United States Congress. Her 2022 quarter says "Voto para la mujer," which means "votes for women."

Anna May Wong

1905–1961

She was born in Los Angeles's Chinatown and became the first Chinese American Hollywood star. In October 2022, her quarter made her the first Asian American shown on United States money.
