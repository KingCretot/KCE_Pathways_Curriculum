📖 Read: Two Sources About Bald Eagles

Giulia, age 9, and her cousin Gideon, age 8, live in Alton, Illinois, a river town on the Mississippi River. Every winter, from about late December to early March, hundreds of bald eagles come to the trees and bluffs along the Mississippi and Illinois Rivers near Alton. Giulia and Gideon want to research one question for the library’s eagle display: How did the bald eagle make a comeback in the United States? The librarian, Ms. Goldie, gives them two sources. Researchers read more than one source, take notes, and keep track of which source said what. (Both sources were written for this lesson, using facts from the United States Fish and Wildlife Service.)

Read both sources, or tap 🔊 to hear them. As you read, look for facts that answer the question: How did the bald eagle make a comeback? Notice which facts are in Source A, which are in Source B, and which are in both.

Source A · A magazine article
The Eagle Comeback
from Wild River Kids, a nature magazine
1

In 1782, America chose the bald eagle as its national symbol. Back then, there may have been as many as 100,000 nesting bald eagles in the land that is now the lower 48 states (all of the states except Alaska and Hawaii).

2

Then the eagles got into trouble. After World War II, people sprayed a bug-killing chemical called DDT (its long name is dichloro-diphenyl-trichloroethane). DDT got into the rivers, lakes, and fish that eagles eat. It made the eagles’ eggshells thin and weak, so many eggs broke or did not hatch.

3

By 1963, only 417 nesting pairs were left in the lower 48 states. The national symbol was in danger of disappearing.

4

People worked to help. In 1972, the United States banned DDT. In 1978, the bald eagle was listed as endangered in most of the lower 48 states, so the law gave eagles and their nests strong protection.

5

The plan worked, and the eagles made a comeback. In 2007, the bald eagle was removed from the list of endangered and threatened species. By 2019, there were about 316,700 bald eagles in the lower 48 states. Laws still protect them today.

Source B · A fact card
Bald Eagle Fact Card
from the library’s eagle display
1

Looks: An adult bald eagle has a white head, a white tail, a dark brown body, and a yellow beak. Young eagles are mostly brown. They get their white heads when they are about 4 or 5 years old.

2

Size: A bald eagle can weigh up to 14 pounds. Its wings can stretch more than 6 feet from tip to tip. Scientists believe bald eagles can live 30 years or longer in the wild.

3

Food and nests: Bald eagles eat mostly fish. They also eat ducks, snakes, and mice. They build giant nests of sticks. A nest can be up to 10 feet across and weigh up to 2,000 pounds, which is a whole ton.

4

Comeback numbers: In 1963, there were only 417 nesting pairs in the lower 48 states. By 2019, there were more than 71,000 nesting pairs! Laws still protect them today. In 2024, a new law made the bald eagle the official national bird of the United States.

5

Where to see them: Bald eagles live near lakes, rivers, and coasts, where they can catch fish. In winter, hundreds gather along the Mississippi and Illinois Rivers near Alton, Illinois, to fish in the open river water.

❓ Ask a Research Question, Part 1

Research means looking for facts to answer a question. Start with a research question, like “How did the bald eagle make a comeback?” Pick out the key words in your question: bald eagle, comeback. Then read your sources and look for facts that answer the question. Some facts are interesting but do not answer your question. Save those for another day!

👁 Watch Me Think

Our question is about the comeback. Source A says DDT made eggshells weak, and then the United States banned DDT in 1972. That helps explain how the eagle came back, so I will take a note. Source B says eagles also eat ducks and snakes. That is interesting, but it does not answer our question about the comeback.

Find: facts that answer it
Skip: facts that do not answer it

📝 Take Notes in Your Own Words, Part 1

Researchers take notes: short reminders of the facts. A good note uses key words, not whole sentences. Write it in your own words instead of copying. But be careful: never change a fact! Names, dates, and numbers must stay exactly the same. Write a letter next to each note, A or B, to show which source it came from.

👁 Watch Me Think

Source A says, “By 1963, only 417 nesting pairs were left in the lower 48 states.” My note: 1963: only 417 pairs left (A). I used short words of my own, but I kept the year and the number exactly the same, and I wrote A so I remember where the fact came from.

🧩 Picture It: A Good Note

Short: key words only
Own words: do not copy
Exact: keep names, dates, and numbers

📄 Source says

“By 1963, only 417 nesting pairs were left in the lower 48 states.”

📝 My note

1963: only 417 pairs left (A)

🤝 Let's Try Together

Let’s try one together. Source A says DDT made the eagles’ eggshells thin and weak, so many eggs broke. A good note: DDT → thin eggshells → eggs broke (A). It is short, it is in my own words, and the facts stay true.

📚 Two Sources: Who Said What?, Part 1

Good researchers read more than one source. When two sources say the same fact, you can feel more sure it is true. Each source can also give new facts that the other one does not have. Keep track of who said what: Source A, Source B, or both. In a report, you can tell readers where a fact came from: “According to Source B, ...”

👁 Watch Me Think

Both Source A and Source B say there were only 417 nesting pairs in 1963. Two sources agree, so I feel sure about that fact. Only Source A tells about DDT and the year 2007. Only Source B tells about giant nests and what eagles eat. Using both sources gives me more facts.

🧩 Picture It: Who Said What?

Both sources: 417 pairs in 1963; laws still protect eagles
Only A: DDT, 1972, and 2007
Only B: nests, food, and white heads

Only A

DDT and thin eggshells · 1972 · 2007 · 316,700 eagles

Both

417 pairs in 1963 · laws still protect eagles

Only B

looks · size · food · giant nests · more than 71,000 pairs

To plan a report, sort your notes into groups. For the eagle comeback, the groups could be: the problem (why eagles almost disappeared), the help (what people did), and the comeback (how eagles are doing now). Write a topic sentence that tells what the report is about. Put the facts in order, add a conclusion, and list your sources at the end.

Source A · A magazine article
The Eagle Comeback
from Wild River Kids, a nature magazine
1

In 1782, America chose the bald eagle as its national symbol. Back then, there may have been as many as 100,000 nesting bald eagles in the land that is now the lower 48 states (all of the states except Alaska and Hawaii).

2

Then the eagles got into trouble. After World War II, people sprayed a bug-killing chemical called DDT (its long name is dichloro-diphenyl-trichloroethane). DDT got into the rivers, lakes, and fish that eagles eat. It made the eagles’ eggshells thin and weak, so many eggs broke or did not hatch.

3

By 1963, only 417 nesting pairs were left in the lower 48 states. The national symbol was in danger of disappearing.

4

People worked to help. In 1972, the United States banned DDT. In 1978, the bald eagle was listed as endangered in most of the lower 48 states, so the law gave eagles and their nests strong protection.

5

The plan worked, and the eagles made a comeback. In 2007, the bald eagle was removed from the list of endangered and threatened species. By 2019, there were about 316,700 bald eagles in the lower 48 states. Laws still protect them today.

Source B · A fact card
Bald Eagle Fact Card
from the library’s eagle display
1

Looks: An adult bald eagle has a white head, a white tail, a dark brown body, and a yellow beak. Young eagles are mostly brown. They get their white heads when they are about 4 or 5 years old.

2

Size: A bald eagle can weigh up to 14 pounds. Its wings can stretch more than 6 feet from tip to tip. Scientists believe bald eagles can live 30 years or longer in the wild.

3

Food and nests: Bald eagles eat mostly fish. They also eat ducks, snakes, and mice. They build giant nests of sticks. A nest can be up to 10 feet across and weigh up to 2,000 pounds, which is a whole ton.

4

Comeback numbers: In 1963, there were only 417 nesting pairs in the lower 48 states. By 2019, there were more than 71,000 nesting pairs! Laws still protect them today. In 2024, a new law made the bald eagle the official national bird of the United States.

5

Where to see them: Bald eagles live near lakes, rivers, and coasts, where they can catch fish. In winter, hundreds gather along the Mississippi and Illinois Rivers near Alton, Illinois, to fish in the open river water.
