🏞️ Lands That Belong to Everyone

Public lands belong to all the people of the country. On August 25, 1916, President Woodrow Wilson signed a law that created the National Park Service. Today it cares for 434 places, often called parks, covering more than 85 million acres in all 50 states. Other public lands protect animals. In 1903, Pelican Island in Florida became the first national wildlife refuge, to protect nesting birds.

👁 Watch Me Think

Whose land was it before? Let me check the United States Fish and Wildlife Service. The Ais people lived at Pelican Island and the land around it from about 4,000 years ago until the middle 1600s. So a place can be "first" in a law, but people knew it long before. The Audubon Society hired Paul Kroegel as the first warden, to guard the birds on Pelican Island.

🌋 Yellowstone: The First National Park

On March 1, 1872, Yellowstone became the first national park in the world. It is in Wyoming, Montana and Idaho. The National Park Service says the law made it a park "for the benefit and enjoyment of the people." In 1880, Harry Yount became its first park ranger.

👁 Watch Me Think

Does Yellowstone's story start in 1872? No! The National Park Service says people arrived about 11,000 years ago. They hunted, fished, gathered plants and used the hot springs. The Tukudika, a band of Mountain Shoshone, lived there for thousands of years. Their name means "eaters of the mountain sheep." Today, 27 tribes are associated with Yellowstone. A historian always asks: Whose story starts before the park?

🤝 Let's Try Together: Who Cared for the Park?

At first, there was no National Park Service. In 1886, the United States Army took charge of Yellowstone to protect it. The National Park Service took over in 1918. How long after the park began was that? 1918 minus 1872 is 46 years.

🐊 Saving the River of Grass

The Everglades in South Florida is a huge wetland. Long ago, the Calusa people lived there. The Seminole and Miccosukee people have lived in the area since the 1700s. But by the 1880s, builders were digging ditches to drain the water, because they thought the Everglades was a worthless swamp. That hurt the plants and animals.

👁 Watch Me Think

Who helped save it? Many people! The Florida Federation of Women's Clubs worked to save it. Marjory Stoneman Douglas, a writer in Miami, wrote a book in 1947 called The Everglades: River of Grass. It helped people see the Everglades as a living ecosystem, not a useless swamp. That same year, Everglades National Park was set up. Words can protect land!

🤲 Caring for the Land Together

Canyon de Chelly National Monument in Arizona is different from most parks. It is on Navajo Nation land, and Diné (Navajo) families still live there. They raise livestock, grow crops and call the canyon Tséyi'. People have lived in these canyons for more than 4,000 years. The National Park Service and the Navajo Nation work together to protect it.

👁 Watch Me Think

What does a good steward do? A steward takes care of something for others. At Canyon de Chelly, the families who live there and the park work together. Visitors can help, too: stay on the trail, pack out trash, and never feed wild animals. Kids can become Junior Rangers by visiting a park, doing the Junior Ranger activities and taking the pledge.

🕵️ Source Detective: The Law That Made the Park Service, 1916

On August 25, 1916, President Woodrow Wilson signed a law that created the National Park Service. People often call it the Organic Act. Here is the part that tells the purpose, or main job, of the national parks, word for word.

Primary Source: Act to Establish a National Park Service (1916; part)

"...which purpose is to conserve the scenery and the natural and historic objects and the wild life therein and to provide for the enjoyment of the same in such manner and by such means as will leave them unimpaired for the enjoyment of future generations."

Word help: conserve = protect and save. scenery = the land you can see. natural and historic objects = things from nature and from history. wild life therein = the wild animals in the parks (people wrote it as two words back then). the same = those things. unimpaired = not harmed. future generations = people who are not born yet.

Act of August 25, 1916, which established the National Park Service (often called the Organic Act). Text as printed by the National Park Service on its Gateway National Recreation Area page about the 1916 Organic Act.

👁 Think Like a Historian

Let me read like a historian. The law gives the parks two jobs: protect nature and history, AND let people enjoy them. Can both happen at once? Yes, if visitors enjoy the parks without harming them. Who is this law for? "Future generations," which means kids like you and people not born yet! I also ask: Whose voices are not in this law? The Native nations who first cared for these lands are not named in it.

🌟 People Who Protected the Land

Meet people from different backgrounds who cared for America's lands. As you read, ask: What did each one protect, and how?

Marjory Stoneman Douglas

1890–1998

She moved to Florida in 1915 and wrote for the Miami Herald newspaper. Her 1947 book, The Everglades: River of Grass, changed how people saw the Everglades. She wrote, "There are no other Everglades in the world." At 79, she started Friends of the Everglades. In 1993, she received the Presidential Medal of Freedom.

John Muir

1838–1914

You met him in Week 8. Here is more of his story. In May 1903, President Theodore Roosevelt visited Yosemite. He left the crowds behind and camped outdoors for three nights with Muir as his guide. They talked about protecting forests and wild places. Later, Roosevelt helped protect millions of acres of public land.

George Melendez Wright

1904–1936

He was born in San Francisco. His mother was from El Salvador. He studied forestry at the University of California, Berkeley, and became a naturalist at Yosemite in 1927. He was the first Hispanic person in a professional job with the National Park Service. He led a study of the wildlife of the national parks and paid for it with his own money at first. He warned that feeding bears so crowds could watch was bad for the bears.

The Diné families of Canyon de Chelly

long ago to today

Diné (Navajo) families live in the canyon they call Tséyi'. About 40 families live inside the park today. They raise livestock and grow crops there. Their homes and images in the canyon tell their stories. Today, the Navajo Nation and the National Park Service work together to protect the canyon.
