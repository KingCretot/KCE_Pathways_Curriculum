🌊 Floods and Tornadoes

A natural hazard is something in nature that can harm people or homes. In Florida Focus, you learned about hurricanes. Today you meet four more. Floods happen when water covers land that is usually dry, often after heavy rain. Floods are the most common disaster in the United States. Just 6 inches of moving water can knock a person down, and 1 foot of moving water can sweep a car away. That is why safety experts say, "Turn Around, Don't Drown!" A tornado is a spinning column of air that reaches from a thunderstorm down to the ground. The United States has more tornadoes than any other country, more than 1,000 a year.

👁 Watch Me Think

Bruno's family is driving home after a big rain. Water is rushing across the road ahead. It does not look very deep. Should they drive through? No! Moving water is strong, and the road under it could even be washed away. They turn around and find another way. Turn Around, Don't Drown!

🌎 Earthquakes: When the Ground Shakes

Deep underground, Earth is made of huge pieces of rock. A crack in the rock where the two sides can move is called a fault. Rock pushes and pushes against rock. When it suddenly slips, the ground shakes. That is an earthquake! Scientists cannot predict exactly when an earthquake will happen. But they know which places have them most, like California and Alaska. So people get ready ahead of time. When the shaking starts: Drop to your hands and knees, Cover your head and neck with your arms, and Hold On to something sturdy until the shaking stops.

👁 Watch Me Think

Kenzo lives in Los Angeles, California. He wonders, "Why do some buildings stay up in an earthquake?" Engineers design them! Some buildings sit on special pads made of layers of rubber and steel. The ground shakes fast, but the pads let the building move slowly and gently. Other buildings are made to bend and sway a little instead of cracking.

🔥 Wildfires

A wildfire is a fire that spreads through grass, brush, or forest. Every fire needs three things: heat, fuel (something to burn, like dry plants), and air. Lightning can start wildfires. But nearly 85 out of every 100 wildfires in the United States are started by people, like campfires left burning. Hot, dry, windy weather helps fires spread fast. Wind can carry glowing bits called embers far ahead of the fire. People protect homes by clearing dead leaves and dry plants from the space right around the house, at least 5 feet out.

👁 Watch Me Think

Keziah's family camps in a forest in Montana. They make a campfire in a fire ring with a grown-up in charge. Before bed, they pour water on it and stir until it is cold. Why? A campfire left burning can start a wildfire. Most wildfires are started by people, so people can prevent them, too!

🌪️ Ted Fujita, "Mr. Tornado"

Ted Fujita studied tornadoes by looking closely at the damage they left behind. Colleagues called him "Mr. Tornado."

Tetsuya "Ted" Fujita

1920–1998

Ted Fujita grew up in Japan. In 1953, he came to the University of Chicago to study storms. He walked through tornado damage and later flew over it in small airplanes to see the whole path from above. In 1971, he made the Fujita scale. It rates how strong a tornado was from the damage it left, from F0 to F5. Weather scientists still use an updated version, the Enhanced Fujita scale. He also discovered microbursts, sudden strong winds that rush down out of a storm and are dangerous for airplanes.

🔬 More Hazard Scientists

These scientists asked, "How can we understand hazards, and how can people be safer?"

Lucy Jones

seismologist

Lucy Jones is a seismologist, a scientist who studies earthquakes. She worked at the U.S. Geological Survey for more than 30 years. In 2008, her work on a pretend big California earthquake helped start the ShakeOut, an earthquake drill where people practice Drop, Cover, and Hold On. Millions of people around the world now take part in it.

Gilbert F. White

1911–2006

Gilbert White was an American geographer called the "father of floodplain management." A floodplain is low land next to a river that floods. He said walls and dams are not enough. People should also make smart choices, like not building homes where floods often happen.

Inge Lehmann

1888–1993

Inge Lehmann was a scientist from Denmark. She studied the waves that earthquakes send through Earth. In 1936, the waves helped her discover that Earth has a solid inner core, deep in its center.
