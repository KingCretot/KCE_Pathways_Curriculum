🌟 Word Wrangler

Tap each card to flip it. Tap the 🔊 to hear the word and what it means.

culture
The way of life of a group of people: their food, languages, clothes, beliefs and celebrations. Example: Each pen pal's culture shows in her or his food, language and celebrations.
tradition
Something people do the same way for many years and pass down to their children. Example: Flying carp streamers on Children's Day is a tradition in Japan.
celebration
A special time when people come together for something important. Example: Diwali is a five-day celebration of lights.
staple food
A food people eat often, as a main part of their meals. Example: Ugali, made from corn flour, is a staple food in Kenya.
uniform
Matching clothes that everyone in a group wears. Example: Faraji wears a school uniform every school day.
stereotype
An idea that everyone in a group is the same. It leaves out most of the real story. Example: Thinking that every kid in Kenya lives near lions is a stereotype.
compare
To tell how things are alike and how they are different. Example: Quinn likes to compare her school day with each pen pal's school day.

🇯🇵 Quinn's Pen Pals, Stop 1: Fumika in Japan

Quinn is a homeschooler in Florida. Her Pen Pal Club trades letters and video calls with four kids: Fumika in Japan, Faraji in Kenya, Fernanda in Mexico and Falguni in India. Each pen pal tells about one family in one city. A whole country has millions of people, so no one kid can speak for everyone.

Fumika lives in an apartment in Osaka, a big city in Japan. When her family comes home, they take off their shoes at the door. In Japan, the school year starts in April. At lunch, students take turns serving food to their classmates. After lunch or at the end of the day, students clean their own classrooms and hallways.

👁 Watch Me Think

Why would students clean their own school? Fumika says everyone helps keep the school nice, so everyone takes care of it. That reminds me of chores at home. So cleaning is part of how Fumika's school teaches kids to help their community. That is part of her school's culture.

🤝 Let's Try Together: Alike or Different?

Let's compare. Quinn's family also has a rule at home: wash your hands before dinner. Fumika's family has a rule too: shoes off at the door. The rules are different, but they are alike in one way. Both help keep a home clean. When we compare, we look for what is alike, not only what is different.

🇰🇪 Stop 2: Faraji in Kenya

Faraji lives in Mombasa, Kenya's chief port city, on the Indian Ocean. More than 1.2 million people live in Mombasa County. Faraji wears a school uniform every school day. Kenya's school year starts in January and ends in November, with three terms and breaks in between.

Kenya's two official languages are Kiswahili and English, and many families also speak other languages at home. Faraji's family often eats ugali, made from corn flour cooked in boiling water, with sukuma wiki, a leafy green. Every December 12, Kenyans celebrate Jamhuri Day. It marks Kenya's independence from Britain in 1963.

👁 Watch Me Think

Some people picture Kenya as only lions and safaris. That is a stereotype. It takes one small piece of a country and pretends it is the whole story. Faraji's real day is school, homework, soccer with friends and dinner with his family in a busy city. So I ask: Where did my idea come from, and what am I missing?

🇲🇽 Stop 3: Fernanda in Mexico

Fernanda lives in a house in Oaxaca City, in southern Mexico. Her school year starts in late summer and ends in July. Most people in Mexico speak Spanish, and Mexico also recognizes 68 Indigenous languages. In the state of Oaxaca, many families speak Zapotec or Mixtec. Fernanda's grandmother speaks Zapotec.

People in Mexico first grew corn about 9,000 years ago. Today, corn tortillas are part of many meals. Each year, from the end of October to early November, many families celebrate Día de Muertos (Day of the Dead). They make an ofrenda, an altar with photos, marigold flowers and the favorite foods of loved ones who have died, to remember them.

👁 Watch Me Think

Is Día de Muertos the same as Halloween? They come at about the same time of year, so some people mix them up. But Día de Muertos is a time for families to remember and honor people they love. In 2008, UNESCO (the United Nations Educational, Scientific and Cultural Organization) listed it as an important world tradition. So the dates are close, but the meaning is different.

🤝 Let's Try Together: What Does Guelaguetza Mean?

The word Guelaguetza comes from the Zapotec language. It is usually explained as sharing gifts and helping each other. At the festival, groups from Indigenous communities across Oaxaca perform their own dances. So one festival shows many cultures, not just one. That is a clue that Mexico has many cultures, too.

🇮🇳 Stop 4: Falguni in India

Falguni lives in Ahmedabad, in the state of Gujarat, India. In 2017, Ahmedabad became the first city in India on the World Heritage list of UNESCO, the United Nations Educational, Scientific and Cultural Organization. India has more people than any other country, about 1.43 billion. India's Constitution lists 22 languages. Falguni speaks Gujarati at home and learns Hindi and English at school.

In Gujarat, the new school year starts in June. Government schools across India serve a free hot lunch to children in grades 1 to 8, and the program covers about 118 million children. In the fall, Falguni's family celebrates Diwali, the festival of lights, for five days. They light small clay lamps called diyas and make floor art from colored rice, sand or flower petals. In January, on Uttarayan, the sky over Ahmedabad fills with kites.

👁 Watch Me Think

Does every family in India celebrate Diwali? No. Diwali is a big festival for Hindus, Jains and Sikhs. But India is home to people of many religions, so families celebrate many different holidays. Falguni's best friend's family celebrates Eid. So I should say "Falguni's family celebrates Diwali," not "everyone in India does."

🕵️ Source Detective: A Promise to the World's Children

On November 20, 1989, the United Nations adopted the Convention on the Rights of the Child. It is a written promise about what every child should have. Read two parts of its real words.

Primary Source: Convention on the Rights of the Child (1989)

Source 1: Article 28 (part of it)

"States Parties recognize the right of the child to education ... Make primary education compulsory and available free to all;"

Word help: States Parties = the countries that agreed to it. compulsory = required. primary education = elementary school.

Source 2: Article 31 (paragraph 1)

"States Parties recognize the right of the child to rest and leisure, to engage in play and recreational activities appropriate to the age of the child and to participate freely in cultural life and the arts."

Word help: leisure = free time. recreational activities = things you do for fun. cultural life = the music, art, festivals and traditions of a community.

Convention on the Rights of the Child, adopted by the United Nations General Assembly on November 20, 1989, Articles 28 and 31.

👁 Think Like a Historian

Who wrote it? Leaders from many countries, working together at the United Nations. Every member country has ratified it, which means agreed to follow it, except one: the United States signed it in 1995 but has not ratified it. A promise on paper is a start. So I also ask: Does every child really get school and play? In Week 19 we learned that laws do not fix everything at once.

🌟 People Who Made Life Better for Kids

Meet four real people, one from each pen pal's country. As you read, ask: What problem did each person see, and what did they do about it?

Tetsuko Kuroyanagi

born 1933 · Japan

She grew up in Tokyo. Her first school asked her to leave, so her mother found a new school that gave children more freedom to learn in their own way. In 1981 she wrote a true book about it, Totto-chan: The Little Girl at the Window. It became one of the best-selling books in Japan's history. In 1984 she became a Goodwill Ambassador for UNICEF (the United Nations Children's Fund), the first from Asia, to help children around the world.

Wangari Maathai

1940–2011 · Kenya

She was born in Nyeri, Kenya. She saw that cutting down trees hurt the land and the families who lived on it. In 1977 she started the Green Belt Movement, and women across Kenya planted millions of trees. In 2004 she became the first African woman to win the Nobel Peace Prize.

Xóchitl Guadalupe Cruz López

Mexico

She is from the state of Chiapas in southern Mexico. When she was 8 years old, she built a solar water heater from recycled things, like bottles and hoses, so her family could have warm water using the sun. She said some families cannot buy heaters, so they cut trees for firewood. She became the first child to receive a science award for women from the Institute of Nuclear Sciences at the National Autonomous University of Mexico.

Kailash Satyarthi

born 1954 · India

He was born in Vidisha, India. He saw children working instead of going to school, just like the kids you met in Week 19. He and his team helped free about 83,000 children from forced work. In 2014 he shared the Nobel Peace Prize with Malala Yousafzai of Pakistan, for standing up for every child's right to go to school.
