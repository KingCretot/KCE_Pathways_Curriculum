🌷 Picture It: Saturday at Pike Place Market

It's a busy Saturday morning at Pike Place Market in Seattle, Washington. Farmers and shop owners fill the stalls with flowers, fruit, bread, and fresh fish. Ms. Akana runs a flower stall, and her neighbor Mr. Bashir sells fruit.

Ms. Akana asks Arlo, Beatriz, and Bodhi to collect data about what the stalls sell and turn it into graphs. Their friend Anouk will check their work. A good graph lets a seller see in one quick look what sold the most.

🐟 Pike Place Market Facts

  • Pike Place Market opened on August 17, 1907, in Seattle, Washington. That makes it more than 100 years old!
  • In 1971, Seattle voters chose to protect the Market, so its old buildings would not be torn down.
  • Rachel is a bronze pig that is also a piggy bank. She has stood at the Market since 1986 and weighs about 550 pounds. The coins people drop in help pay for services for Market neighbors who need help.
  • The fish sellers at the Market are famous for tossing big fish to each other over the counter.
  • Hmong American farm families, whose families came from Laos, have grown and sold flowers at the Market for many years.

Today you will read picture graphs and bar graphs, use the key or the scale to find each number, and solve how many more and how many fewer problems.

🌟 Word Wrangler

Flip each card to learn this week's vocabulary! Each card shows a sentence from the lesson. Tap the speaker to hear the word or the meaning.

data
Facts or numbers that we collect, such as counts of things sold.
“Ms. Akana asks Arlo, Beatriz, and Bodhi to collect data about what the stalls sell and turn it into graphs.”
picture graph
A graph that uses pictures to show data. Each picture can stand for more than 1.
“Ms. Akana's picture graph shows how many bouquets each kind of flower sold.”
key
The part of a picture graph that tells how many each picture stands for.
“The key at the bottom says that each flower picture stands for 2 bouquets.”
bar graph
A graph that uses bars of different lengths to show data.
“Beatriz made a bar graph of how many people patted Rachel the Pig each hour.”
scale
The numbers on a bar graph that the grid lines count by, such as 2, 5, or 10.
“The scale counts by 10, so each grid line is worth 10 more than the line below it.”
compare
To find how two amounts are alike or different, such as how many more or how many fewer.
“To compare two bars, subtract the smaller number from the bigger number.”

Here is a memory trick: a key unlocks what each picture means, and a scale is like a staircase where every step is the same size.

🌷 Reading Picture Graphs with a Key

Ms. Akana's picture graph shows how many bouquets each kind of flower sold. The key at the bottom says that each flower picture stands for 2 bouquets. The roses row has 4 flowers, so Ms. Akana skip-counts by 2: 2, 4, 6, 8. She sold 8 rose bouquets.

👁 Watch Me Think

First I read the key. Then I count the pictures in a row and skip-count by the key. A half picture stands for half of the key, so when the key is 2, a half flower means 1 bouquet.

🧩 Picture It

Roses: 4 flowers → 2, 4, 6, 8 → 8 bouquets
Sunflowers: 3 flowers = 6, plus half a flower = 1 → 7 bouquets

🤝 Let's Try Together

The daisy row has 6 flowers. Skip-count by 2 six times: 2, 4, 6, 8, 10, 12. Ms. Akana sold 12 daisy bouquets. If the key said 5 instead, the same 6 pictures would mean 30 bouquets, so always read the key first!

📊 Reading Bar Graphs with a Scale

Beatriz made a bar graph of how many people patted Rachel the Pig each hour. The scale counts by 10, so each grid line is worth 10 more than the line below it. The 9 a.m. bar stops right on the 30 line, so 30 people patted Rachel at 9 a.m.

👁 Watch Me Think

I read the scale first. Then I run my finger from the top of a bar straight across to the numbers. If a bar stops halfway between two grid lines, its number is halfway between them. Halfway between 40 and 50 is 45.

🧩 Picture It

9 a.m.: the bar stops on the 30 line → 30 people
10 a.m.: the bar stops halfway between 40 and 50 → 45 people

🤝 Let's Try Together

The noon bar stops halfway between 20 and 30. Halfway between 20 and 30 is 25, so 25 people patted Rachel at noon. On a graph with a scale of 2, halfway between 6 and 8 would be 7.

⚖️ How Many More? How Many Fewer?

Bodhi looks at Beatriz's bar graph. At 11 a.m., 60 people patted Rachel the Pig. At noon, only 25 did. How many more people patted Rachel at 11 a.m.? He subtracts: 60 − 25 = 35 more people.

👁 Watch Me Think

“How many more” and “how many fewer” both ask for the difference between two amounts. To compare two bars, subtract the smaller number from the bigger number. On a picture graph, I can also count how many more pictures one row has, then use the key.

🧩 Picture It

Bar graph: 60 − 25 = 35 → 35 more people at 11 a.m.
Picture graph: daisies 6 flowers, roses 4 flowers → 2 more flowers → 2 × 2 = 4 more bouquets

🤝 Let's Try Together

How many fewer sunflower bouquets than daisy bouquets did Ms. Akana sell? Daisies: 12. Sunflowers: 7. 12 − 7 = 5, so she sold 5 fewer sunflower bouquets.

✏️ Make Your Own Graph

Now it's Arlo's turn to make a graph! Mr. Bashir's fruit stand sold 30 boxes of cherries, 25 boxes of peaches, and 15 boxes of plums. Arlo picks a key where each picture stands for 5 boxes. 30 ÷ 5 = 6, so he draws 6 pictures for cherries.

👁 Watch Me Think

To make a picture graph, I pick a key that fits all my numbers. Then I ask: how many groups of the key are in each number? To make a bar graph, I pick a scale and stop each bar at its number, even if that is halfway between two grid lines.

🧩 Picture It

Cherries: 30 ÷ 5 = 6 pictures
Peaches: 25 ÷ 5 = 5 pictures
Plums: 15 ÷ 5 = 3 pictures

🤝 Let's Try Together

What if Arlo used a key of 10 instead? Cherries: 30 is 3 groups of 10, so 3 pictures. Peaches: 25 is 2 groups of 10 and half of another group, so 2 and a half pictures. Plums: 15 would be 1 and a half pictures.
