🤝 What Citizens Do

A citizen is a member of a country, with rights and duties. Some rights belong to everyone who lives in the United States, like freedom of speech and freedom of religion. Some rights are only for citizens, like voting in national elections and running for a national office. Citizens also have responsibilities. Only citizens can serve on a jury, a group of citizens who hear the facts in a court case and decide.

👁 Watch Me Think

Jiya asks, "Can kids be good citizens if they cannot vote yet?" Let me think. Everyone who lives here must obey the laws, and grown-ups pay taxes. Kids can follow rules, learn about their community, help their neighbors and speak up kindly about problems. So yes! Being a good citizen starts long before your first vote.

🗳️ How an Election Works

An election is a way for people to choose leaders or decide on ideas by voting. People who want a job, like mayor, are candidates. Voters learn about the candidates first. Then many voters go to a polling place, check in with a poll worker and mark a ballot in private. Many voters can also send in a ballot by mail.

👁 Watch Me Think

How do we know the count is right? Let me think. Poll workers compare how many voters checked in with how many ballots were cast. The two numbers should match! After the election, many places also do an audit, which means checking the results again to make sure they are correct. Checking twice helps everyone trust the results.

➗ Most Votes, or More Than Half?

In many elections, the candidate with the most votes wins. But some elections need a majority. A majority means more than half of all the votes. If nobody gets more than half, some places hold a second election, called a runoff, between the top two.

👁 Watch Me Think

The Kids' Choice election had 3 names and 40 votes. Sunflower Spot got 18, Story Nook got 15 and Book Garden got 7. Sunflower Spot has the most votes. But is that a majority? Half of 40 is 20. A majority must be MORE than 20, so at least 21. 18 is less than 21. So no name has a majority, and the kids hold a runoff between Sunflower Spot and Story Nook.

🛤️ The Long Road to the Vote

In Week 2, you saw a voting timeline. Now let us look closer. The right to vote grew step by step. In 1870, the 15th Amendment said citizens cannot be kept from voting because of race. In 1920, the 19th Amendment said citizens cannot be kept from voting because of sex. But words on paper did not reach everyone. Unfair rules and threats kept many Black citizens in the South from voting until the Voting Rights Act of 1965 helped protect them.

👁 Watch Me Think

After 1920, who was still missing? Let me look closely. The amendments protect citizens. Native Americans were made citizens in 1924, but states made their own voting rules. Arizona and New Mexico kept them from voting until 1948, and Utah until 1957 on reservations. Chinese immigrants could not become citizens until 1943, and many other Asian immigrants not until a law in 1952. A right on paper is not the same as a right in real life.

🕵️ Source Detective: The 19th Amendment, 1920

An amendment is a change added to the Constitution, the highest law of the United States. Congress passed this amendment on June 4, 1919. Enough states approved it on August 18, 1920. Here is the whole amendment, word for word.

Primary Source: The 19th Amendment to the United States Constitution (1920)

"The right of citizens of the United States to vote shall not be denied or abridged by the United States or by any State on account of sex."

"Congress shall have power to enforce this article by appropriate legislation."

Word help: denied = refused. abridged = made smaller or cut short. on account of = because of. sex = being a woman or a man. enforce = make sure people follow it. article = this part of the law. legislation = laws.

Nineteenth Amendment to the United States Constitution, passed by Congress June 4, 1919, ratified August 18, 1920. Text from the National Archives, "The Constitution: Amendments 11-27."

🌟 People Who Widened the Vote

Meet four people from four different communities who worked so more citizens could vote. As you read, ask: Who was left out, and what did each person do about it?

Ida B. Wells

1862–1931

She was born enslaved in Holly Springs, Mississippi, and became a brave newspaper writer. In January 1913, she started the Alpha Suffrage Club in Chicago for Black women. At a big march for women's votes in the nation's capital in 1913, leaders told Black women to march at the back. She refused. She waited at the side, then stepped in to march with the women from Chicago.

Mabel Ping-Hua Lee

1896–1966

She was born in Guangzhou, China, and later moved to New York City. In 1912, as a teenager, she rode a horse in a huge march for women's votes on Fifth Avenue. A law kept her from becoming a citizen, so she could not vote when the 19th Amendment passed in 1920. In 1921, she earned a doctorate in economics from Columbia University. She later directed the First Chinese Baptist Church in New York City's Chinatown.

Mary McLeod Bethune

1875–1955

She was born near Mayesville, South Carolina. Her parents had been enslaved. In 1904, she opened a school in Daytona Beach, Florida, with six students. It grew into Bethune-Cookman College. In September 1920, she led a drive to help Black women in Florida register to vote, even when people threatened them.

Miguel Trujillo

1904–1989

He was a member of Isleta Pueblo in New Mexico and served in the Marines in World War II. In 1948, he tried to register to vote, but he was told no, because New Mexico would not let many Native Americans vote. He went to court and won. After his case, Native Americans in New Mexico could vote.
