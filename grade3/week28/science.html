🦴 Your Super Skeleton

Your skeleton is the frame of bones inside your body. An adult has 206 bones. A baby is born with about 300 bones, and some of them grow together as the child grows. Bones give your body its shape, hold you up, and protect the soft organs inside you. Your skull protects your brain. Your ribs make a cage around your heart and lungs. Your spine, or backbone, is made of 33 small bones called vertebrae. Your bones are alive, and they grow and change. Bone marrow, the soft part inside many bones, makes blood cells.

👁 Watch Me Think

Which bone is the longest? The femur, my thigh bone, which goes from my hip to my knee. Which bone is the smallest? The stirrup bone, deep inside my ear, behind my eardrum. It is only about one tenth of an inch long! Can I feel my bones? Yes! I can feel the bumps of my spine in the middle of my back, and my ribs when I take a deep breath.

💪 Muscles and Joints

A joint is a place where two bones meet. Your elbows and knees have hinge joints. They bend one way, like a door on its hinges. Your shoulders and hips have ball-and-socket joints. The round end of one bone fits into a cup in another bone, so they can move in many directions. You have more than 600 muscles. Tough cords called tendons connect your muscles to your bones. Muscles can pull, but they cannot push. So they work in pairs. Your biceps, on the front of your upper arm, pulls to bend your elbow. Your triceps, on the back of your upper arm, pulls to straighten it.

👁 Watch Me Think

Are all my muscles the kind I can control? No! There are three kinds. Skeletal muscles move my bones when I want them to, like when I kick a ball. Smooth muscles work on their own, like the ones that move food through my stomach. Cardiac muscle is the muscle my heart is made of, and it works all day and night without me thinking about it.

🫁 Heart and Lungs Team Up

Your heart is a muscle about as big as your fist. It sits a little to the left of the middle of your chest. It pumps blood all around your body, all day and all night. Blood carries oxygen and food to every part of you and carries waste away. Your lungs bring oxygen into your body when you breathe in. They send a waste gas called carbon dioxide out when you breathe out. Air goes in through your nose or mouth, down your windpipe, and into your lungs. Under your lungs is a big muscle called the diaphragm. When you breathe in, your diaphragm moves down and pulls air into your lungs. When you breathe out, it moves back up.

👁 Watch Me Think

What is the lub-dub sound a doctor hears in my chest? My heart has four rooms, called chambers, and valves that work like doors. They open to let blood move forward, then snap shut so it cannot flow backward. Lub-dub is the sound of the valves closing. My heart is fast, too: it pumps blood to every cell in my body in less than one minute!

🏠 Try It at Home: Paper Bone Tubes

Your long bones, like your femur, are shaped a bit like tubes. Is a tube a strong shape? Let's test it! You need 6 sheets of printer paper, tape, and a stack of paperback books. Roll 3 sheets into tubes of the same size and tape them. Fold the other 3 sheets in half to make tent shapes. Stand the 3 tubes up close together, then gently add books on top, one at a time, until the tubes start to bend. Count the books. Then do the same with the 3 tents.

👁 Watch Me Think

To make it a fair test, I change only one thing: the shape of the paper. I use the same paper, the same books, and the same table. I predict the tubes will hold more books. Real bones are not paper, of course! But long bones are hard on the outside and spongy inside, and that helps make your skeleton very strong and very light.

🫀 Vivien Thomas, Heart Surgery Pioneer

He never went to college, but he helped invent an operation that saved children's lives.

Vivien Thomas

1910–1985

Vivien Thomas was born in 1910 in Louisiana and grew up in Nashville, Tennessee. He saved money to go to college and become a doctor, but during the Great Depression his savings were wiped out. In 1930, he became a laboratory assistant for the surgeon Alfred Blalock at Vanderbilt University. In 1941, they moved to Johns Hopkins in Baltimore, Maryland. There, the heart doctor Helen Taussig asked for help with "blue babies." Too little blood reached these babies' lungs, so their skin looked bluish. Thomas worked out how to do an operation to fix the blood flow and practiced it again and again in the lab. In November 1944, during the first operation on a child, he stood on a step stool behind Dr. Blalock and coached him. For years, Thomas was paid on a janitor's pay scale, and his name was left off the operation. In 1976, Johns Hopkins gave him an honorary doctorate.

🔎 More People Who Helped Us See Inside the Body

These people asked, "How can we learn what is happening inside a living body?"

Helen Taussig

1898–1986

An American heart doctor for children at Johns Hopkins. Reading was hard for her because of dyslexia, and an illness called whooping cough damaged her hearing, so a stethoscope was hard for her to use. Instead, she felt heartbeats with her hands. She asked for a way to help blue babies, which led to the 1944 operation. In 1964, she received the Presidential Medal of Freedom.

René Laennec

1781–1826

A French doctor in Paris. Around 1816, he rolled a piece of paper into a long tube. He put one end on a patient's chest and the other end at his ear, and he could hear the heart. He later made a wooden tube and named it the stethoscope. Doctors still use stethoscopes to hear the lub-dub of your heart.

Wilhelm Röntgen

1845–1923

A German scientist. On a November evening in 1895, he discovered X-rays. They pass through some things, like cardboard and wood, but bone blocks them. One of his first X-ray pictures showed the bones of his wife Anna Bertha's hand, with her ring. In 1901, he won the very first Nobel Prize in Physics.
