🏛️ Who Makes the Laws?

A rule is for one place, like your home or your class. A law is a rule for a whole town, state or country, and everyone there must follow it. Lawmakers are people that voters elect to write laws. Each level has its own lawmakers.

👁 Watch Me Think

Who would make a rule about how fast cars drive on my street? That is a town problem, so the city council decides. A new rule for every public school in Florida? That is a state problem, so the Florida Legislature decides. A rule for coins used across the country? That is a job for Congress. I match the problem to the level.

📜 From Idea to Law

Every law starts as an idea, and the idea can come from anyone, even a kid! First, a lawmaker writes the idea as a bill. Next, a committee, a small group of lawmakers, studies the bill and listens to people. Then the bill must pass a vote in both parts of the legislature. Last, the governor or the President signs it, and it becomes a law.

👁 Watch Me Think

Let me follow a real bill. In 1987, 17 students at South Terrace Elementary School in Carlton, Minnesota, had an idea: make the blueberry muffin a state symbol. They wrote letters and drew pictures for their lawmakers and the governor. In March 1988, they visited the state Capitol. The bill passed, and in April 1988 Governor Rudy Perpich signed it. Idea, bill, votes, signature: a law!

🧩 Picture It: The Path of a Bill

1. IdeaAnyone can suggest a new law.

🤝 Let's Try Together: What If the Answer Is No?

What if the governor does not like a bill? The governor can say no. That is called a veto. Then the bill goes back to the lawmakers. If two-thirds of the lawmakers in BOTH parts vote yes again, the bill becomes a law anyway. Two-thirds means 2 out of every 3.

📣 Three Ways Kids Can Speak Up

You cannot vote until you are 18, but you can still be heard! Kids can write a letter or email to a lawmaker. They can start a petition, a paper signed by many people who want the same change. And they can testify, which means speaking to a committee to share facts and their story.

👁 Watch Me Think

Does one letter matter? In 2016, Mari Copeny was 8 years old. She lived in Flint, Michigan, where the water was not safe to drink. She wrote a letter to President Barack Obama and asked to meet him. The President wrote back. He said he was coming to Flint on May 4, and he did. One kid's letter helped bring the President to her city.

✊ Young People Who Changed Big Laws

On May 2, 1963, more than 1,000 Black students marched in Birmingham, Alabama. They marched peacefully against segregation, laws that kept Black and white people apart. Hundreds were arrested. People around the world heard about the young marchers. In 1964, Congress passed the Civil Rights Act, a national law against segregation in public places.

👁 Watch Me Think

Does speaking up always work right away? In March 1968, thousands of Mexican American high school students in East Los Angeles walked out of class. They asked for better school buildings, classes in Spanish and English, and more Mexican American teachers. The school board agreed to some ideas, but many changes came slowly. Speaking up can take a long time.

🕵️ Source Detective: The Constitution

The United States Constitution is the plan for our national government. Read two real parts of it. The original pages are kept at the National Archives in Washington, D.C.

Primary Sources: Two Parts of the Constitution (1787 and 1791)

Source 1: Article I, Section 7 (written in 1787, part)

"Every Bill which shall have passed the House of Representatives and the Senate, shall, before it become a Law, be presented to the President of the United States; If he approve he shall sign it ..."

Word help: presented to = handed to. approve = agrees with it.

Source 2: The First Amendment (added in 1791, part)

"... the right of the people peaceably to assemble, and to petition the Government for a redress of grievances."

Word help: peaceably = calmly, without fighting. assemble = gather in a group. redress of grievances = fixing the problems people complain about.

The Constitution of the United States, Article I, Section 7 (signed in 1787), and the First Amendment, part of the Bill of Rights (added in 1791). National Archives.

👁 Think Like a Historian

Let me compare. Source 1 tells the steps: a bill must pass BOTH the House and the Senate, then go to the President. Source 2 protects our right to gather peacefully and to petition. Who wrote these words? Men chosen by the states in 1787. Women, enslaved people and Native nations had no say. So I ask: who was missing, and how have people used these words since to be heard?

🌟 Young People Who Spoke Up

Meet four young people who spoke up. As you read, ask: How did each one speak up, and what changed?

Janice Kelsey

marched in 1963, age 16

She was 16 when she marched with other students in Birmingham, Alabama, on May 2, 1963. They marched against segregation. She was arrested and kept in jail for four days. The young marchers helped the whole country see why fair laws were needed.

Paula Crisostomo

a high school senior in 1968

She went to Lincoln High School in East Los Angeles. She and other students asked students about their schools and took the answers to leaders. When the leaders did not listen, she helped organize the 1968 walkouts. Her teacher, Sal Castro, helped the students.

Mari Copeny

age 8 in 2016

She lives in Flint, Michigan, where the water was not safe to drink. People call her "Little Miss Flint." She wrote a letter to President Barack Obama and asked to meet him. He wrote back and came to Flint. She kept speaking up for clean water.

Christina Huang

age 17 in 2022

She was a high school student in Ridgewood, New Jersey. She worked to get Asian American history taught in New Jersey public schools. In 2022, the governor signed the law. New Jersey was the second state with a law like this, after Illinois.
