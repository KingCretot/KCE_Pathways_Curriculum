🐟 Why Live in a Group?

Some animals live alone, but many live in groups. A group of elephants or bison is a herd. A family of wolves is a pack. Ants and honeybees live in colonies. Fish swim in schools. Why live together? A group has many eyes, so someone is more likely to spot danger. When thousands of fish twist and turn together, it is hard for a predator to pick just one. Groups can also stay warm together, find food together, and keep their young safe.

👁 Watch Me Think

A shark swims toward a school of sardines. If one sardine were alone, the shark could chase it easily. But the whole school swirls together, so the shark has trouble picking one fish to follow. Swimming together even saves energy, the way bike riders save energy when they ride close in a line. So living in a school helps each fish survive.

🐝 Jobs in a Colony

A honeybee colony is like a busy town. It has one queen, some male bees called drones, and thousands of worker bees. All the worker bees are female. The queen lays the eggs. Worker bees do almost every other job, and their jobs change as they get older. Young workers stay inside to clean, feed the young, make wax, and guard the door. Older workers fly out to collect nectar, pollen, and water. A worker who finds good flowers does a waggle dance that tells the others which way to fly and how far to go.

👁 Watch Me Think

Why share the jobs? One bee could never do everything. If every bee went out for food, no one would feed the young or guard the door. Splitting up the work gets every job done. Ants do this too. In a harvester ant colony, some ants look for food, some fix the nest, and some patrol.

🐘 Families and Teamwork

Many groups are families. An African elephant family is led by the matriarch, usually the oldest female. The family follows her to food and water. In Yellowstone National Park, a wolf pack is a family of about 10 wolves. The wolves cooperate to hunt elk, animals much bigger than one wolf. Meerkats in southern Africa take turns as lookouts, called sentinels. A sentinel watches from a high spot and gives an alarm call if a predator comes. Living in a group has costs too: the animals must share food, and sickness can spread. That is why some animals, like most adult tigers, live alone.

👁 Watch Me Think

In 1993, a terrible drought hit Tarangire National Park in Tanzania. Many elephant families left the park and found food and water, and fewer of their babies died than in the families that stayed. The families that left belonged to groups with older females who had lived through an earlier drought, from 1958 to 1961. Scientists think those older matriarchs may have remembered where to go. Scientists are still testing that idea, but it shows how an older leader may help the whole family survive.

🏠 Try It at Home: Build a Penguin Huddle

Build a penguin huddle with cups of warm water! Each cup is a penguin, and the warm water is its body heat. You will test whether a cup in the middle of a huddle stays warmer than a cup standing alone. A grown-up must fill the cups with warm tap water, never hot water.

👁 Watch Me Think

I set 7 cups in a tight group: 1 in the middle and 6 around it. I set 1 more cup far away, all alone. I predict the middle cup will stay warmest, because warm cups surround it on every side. After 30 minutes, I will check each cup with a fingertip, or with a kitchen thermometer if we have one.

🐘 Cynthia Moss, Elephant Scientist

Cynthia Moss has spent more than 50 years watching the same elephant families in Africa.

Cynthia Moss

born 1940

Cynthia Moss is an American scientist. In September 1972, she and scientist Harvey Croze started the Amboseli Elephant Research Project in Amboseli National Park in Kenya. It is the longest-running study of wild elephant behavior. The project has followed the lives of more than 2,000 elephants. Her work showed that elephants live in close families led by a matriarch. She is famous for studying a matriarch named Echo. When Echo died in 2009, her family had grown from 7 elephants to 37. Cynthia Moss wrote books about them, like Echo of the Elephants.

🔎 More Animal Behavior Scientists

Scientists who study how animals act ask, "Why does the animal do that, and how does it help?"

Karl von Frisch

1886–1982

A scientist born in Vienna, Austria. He watched honeybees for many years and discovered that a bee does a waggle dance to tell other bees the direction and distance of food. In 1973 he shared the Nobel Prize, one of the highest awards in science, for his studies of animal behavior.

Deborah M. Gordon

biologist today

A biologist at Stanford University in California. She has studied harvester ant colonies in the desert of Arizona. She found that no ant gives orders. The queen just lays eggs. Each ant does a job, like finding food, fixing the nest, or patrolling, and ants can switch jobs when things change.

Shermin de Silva

biologist today

A biologist who studies Asian elephants in Udawalawe National Park in Sri Lanka. She found that these elephant families do not have one clear leader, the way African savanna elephant families follow a matriarch. One idea is that Sri Lanka has no large predators like lions, and food and water are easier to find. She started a group called Trunks and Leaves to help protect elephants.
