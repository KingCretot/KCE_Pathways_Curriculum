one-room school

slate
A small, dark board that students wrote on with chalk. Example: Students practiced spelling on their slates.
recite
To say a lesson out loud for the teacher. Example: Students sat on a bench at the front to recite their lessons.
segregation
Keeping people apart because of their race or where their families came from. Example: Segregation kept Mamie Tape out of her neighborhood school.
boarding school
A school where children live, far from home. Example: Many Native American children were sent to boarding schools.
integrate
To open a place to everyone, so people of all races are together. Example: In 1960, Ruby Bridges helped integrate a school in New Orleans.
equality
Being treated as equals, in a fair way. Example: A judge said public schools need equality and must be open to all children.

🏫 A Day in a One-Room School

Long ago, many children went to a one-room school. One teacher taught every child, from about age 6 up to teenagers, all in one room. Little kids sat in front. Big kids sat in back and helped the little ones. Students wrote on slates with chalk. A stove kept the room warm.

👁 Watch Me Think

How do we know what these schools were like? I check many sources. Some old schoolhouses still stand, and some are museums now. People who went there told their stories. Old photos show the desks and the stove. Many clues tell one story.

🚫 Not Everyone Could Go

Long ago, not every child was allowed to learn. Before slavery ended in 1865, many Southern states had laws against teaching enslaved people to read. Some brave people learned in secret anyway.

👁 Watch Me Think

I ask: who is missing from the one-room school? Many Native American children were sent far from home to boarding schools. A big one, Carlisle, opened in Pennsylvania in 1879. Children came from about 140 Indigenous nations, like the Lakota, Cheyenne and Apache. They were not allowed to speak their own languages. Many missed their families very much.

✊ Families Who Fought for School

Many families went to court so their children could learn. In 1884, a school in San Francisco turned away 8-year-old Mamie Tape because her family was Chinese. Mamie was born in California. Her parents, Mary and Joseph Tape, went to court. In 1885, a judge said Mamie could go to public school.

👁 Watch Me Think

Did that fix everything? Not yet. The city opened a separate school just for Chinese children, and Mamie was sent there. That is segregation. It took many more years, and many more families, to change this.

🌈 School Today: Open to All

Today, every child has the right to go to public school. That includes children of every race, children whose families just arrived, and children with disabilities. In 1970, schools taught only about 1 in 5 children with disabilities. In 1975, a new law said every child with a disability has the right to a free public education.

👁 Watch Me Think

Let me compare then and now. Then: one teacher, one room, and many children left out. Now: many classrooms, and a right to learn for every child. Did the change just happen? No. Families, teachers, lawyers and kids worked for it, step by step.

🕵️ Source Detective: A Mother's Letter and a Judge's Words

Compare two primary sources, written 61 years apart. Both are about who may go to school.

Primary Sources: A Letter from 1885 and a Court Ruling from 1946

Source 1: A letter

In April 1885, Mamie's mother, Mary Tape, wrote to the San Francisco school board. A newspaper printed her letter. Part of it says:

"Is it a disgrace to be Born a Chinese? Didn't God make us all!!!"

Word help: disgrace = something to be ashamed of.

Source 2: A judge's ruling

In 1946, Judge Paul McCormick ruled in the Mendez family's case in California. He wrote:

"A paramount requisite in the American system of public education is social equality. It must be open to all children by unified school association regardless of lineage."

Word help: paramount requisite = the most important need. social equality = everyone treated as equals. regardless of lineage = no matter who your family is or where they came from.

Mary Tape, letter to the San Francisco Board of Education, printed in the Daily Alta California, April 16, 1885. Judge Paul J. McCormick, ruling in Mendez v. Westminster School District, U.S. District Court, Los Angeles, 1946.

👁 Think Like a Historian

Source 1 is from a mother. Source 2 is from a judge. One asks a question with strong feelings. One gives a rule. Do they agree? Yes! Both say school should be open to every child. When a mother and a judge agree, I ask: why did it take 61 years?

🍎 People Who Opened School Doors

Meet more people who helped children learn. As you read, ask: What did each person do, and who did they help?

Susie King Taylor

1848–1912

She was born enslaved in Georgia. It was against the law for her to learn to read. She learned anyway, at secret schools run by Black women in Savannah. In 1862, as a young teenager, she started a school for freed children on St. Simons Island. She taught children by day and grown-ups at night. In 1902, she wrote a book about her life.

Booker T. Washington and Julius Rosenwald

1856–1915 and 1862–1932

Booker T. Washington was a Black teacher who led Tuskegee Institute in Alabama. Julius Rosenwald was the head of Sears, a big store company. Starting in 1912, they worked together to build schools for Black children in the South. Each community helped pay with money, land, wood or work. In the end, more than 5,000 buildings, mostly schools, went up in 15 states.

Zitkála-Šá

1876–1938

Her name means Red Bird. She was Yankton Dakota, from what is now South Dakota. At age 8, she was sent to a boarding school in Indiana. Later, she wrote true stories about her school days so people would know. She played the violin and helped write an opera. In 1926, she started a group that worked for Native American rights.
