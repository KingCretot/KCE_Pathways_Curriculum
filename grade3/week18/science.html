🧲 Poles: Attract and Repel

Every magnet has two poles: a north pole and a south pole. The pull is strongest at the poles. Opposite poles attract: north and south pull together. Like poles repel: north and north push apart, and south and south push apart. In Week 12 you saw magnets pull without touching. Today we go deeper.

👁 Watch Me Think

I hold two bar magnets. The ends that face each other push apart. So they must be the same kind of pole. I flip one magnet around. Now a north faces a south. They snap together! Flipping the magnet changed which poles were facing.

🧩 Picture It: Pole Rules

Poles facing each otherWhat happens
north and southattract (pull together)
south and northattract (pull together)
north and northrepel (push apart)
south and southrepel (push apart)

🤝 Let's Try Together

A magnet breaks into two pieces. Does each piece get only one pole? No! Each piece becomes a new, smaller magnet with its own north pole and south pole. You can never get a magnet with just one pole.

🔩 What Do Magnets Pull?

Things a magnet pulls are magnetic. Iron is magnetic. Most steel is magnetic, because steel is made mostly of iron. Two other metals you may know, nickel and cobalt, are magnetic too. But most metals are not! Magnets do not pull aluminum, copper, brass, gold, or silver. Even some steel, like many stainless steel spoons, is only a little magnetic or not magnetic at all.

👁 Watch Me Think

A penny is metal. Will my magnet pull it? A penny made after 1982 is mostly zinc with a thin coat of copper. Neither metal is magnetic. My test shows the penny does not move. Being metal is not enough. It needs iron, nickel, or cobalt in it.

🌍 Pulling Without Touching

Magnetism is a force that works without touching. It can pull right through paper, plastic, water, and cardboard. But the pull gets weaker as the magnet moves farther away. Earth acts like a giant magnet too! A compass needle is a tiny magnet. It lines up with Earth's magnetism and points north. Remember Shen Kuo from Week 12? He wrote about a needle like this almost 1,000 years ago.

👁 Watch Me Think

I hold a magnet far above a paper clip. Nothing happens. I move it closer, closer... jump! The clip leaps up to the magnet. The pull was there all along, but far away it was too weak to lift the clip. Closer means stronger.

🧩 Picture It: Forces Without Touching

🤝 Let's Try Together

A compass needle points north. Now you hold a magnet right next to the compass. The needle swings toward the magnet! Why? The magnet is much closer than Earth's far-away north, so its pull on the needle is stronger. That is why you keep magnets away from a compass when you need to find north.

🎈 Static Electricity: The Balloon Trick

Everything has tiny bits of electric charge inside it. When you rub a balloon on your hair, tiny bits called electrons move from your hair onto the balloon. Now the balloon and your hair have opposite charges. Opposite charges attract, so your hair lifts toward the balloon! This built-up charge is called static electricity. "Static" means it stays in place instead of flowing.

👁 Watch Me Think

My rubbed balloon sticks to the wall. Is the balloon a magnet? No! A magnet would not stick to a painted wall. The balloon has static electricity. Its charge pulls on tiny charges inside the wall. Static electricity and magnetism are different forces, but both work without touching.

🧩 Picture It: Balloon Charge Rules

ChargesWhat happens
opposite charges (hair and rubbed balloon)attract (pull together)
the same charge (two rubbed balloons)repel (push apart)

Safety: A grown-up must blow up and tie the balloon. If a balloon pops, a grown-up should pick up every piece right away, because balloon pieces can block breathing. Skip this if anyone has a latex allergy.

Fun fact: Lightning begins as static charges in a storm cloud. It is the same idea, but giant!

🤝 Let's Try Together

With a grown-up, rub the balloon on your hair for about 10 seconds. Hold it over tiny bits of paper. They jump up to the balloon! Now hold it near a thin, slow stream of water from a faucet. The water bends toward the balloon. The charge pulls without touching.

🛠️ Magnets at Work: Design It!

Engineers use magnets to solve problems. Many refrigerator doors have a magnet strip hidden in the rubber seal. It pulls the door shut tight and keeps cold air in. Cabinet doors use magnet catches. Some very fast trains, called maglev trains, use strong magnets to lift the train and make it float just above the track as it zooms along.

👁 Watch Me Think

Problem: my cabinet door keeps swinging open. Criteria: it must stay shut, but open with a gentle pull. My idea: put a magnet on the door and a small steel plate on the frame. When the door closes, the magnet pulls the steel. A gentle tug opens it.

🔬 Scientists of Magnets and Static

These people studied magnets and static electricity, then used what they learned in new ways.

William Gilbert

1544–1603

An English doctor and scientist who tested magnets for many years. He made a ball-shaped magnet and called it a terrella, which means "little Earth." When he moved a compass around it, the needle acted like a compass on Earth. In 1600 he wrote a book that said Earth itself is a giant magnet.

Marie Curie

1867–1934

A scientist born in Poland who worked in France. Her first big science job was to study how magnetic different kinds of steel were. A French group that helped factories and industry paid for the work, and she finished it in 1897. Later she and her husband, Pierre, discovered two new elements and became the first person to win two Nobel Prizes.

Masato Sagawa

born 1943

An engineer from Japan. In 1982, he invented the neodymium magnet, made of neodymium, iron, and boron. It is the strongest kind of permanent magnet in the world. Magnets like his are inside electric cars, wind turbines, and computer hard drives.

Chester Carlson

1906–1968

An inventor who grew up poor in the United States. At work he needed many copies of papers, and copying by hand was slow. He found a way to copy with light and static electricity. In 1938, in a small lab in Astoria, New York, he and his helper Otto Kornei made the first copy: "10-22-38 Astoria." Copy machines and laser printers use the same kind of idea today.
