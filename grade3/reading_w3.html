🏕️ The Beginning: Theo Plans a Trip

Every story has a beginning — where we meet the characters and find out what they're about to do. Read the start of Theo's new adventure:

"Dad, can we camp overnight this time?" Theo asked, spreading the trail map across the kitchen table. His father grinned. "I was hoping you'd ask." They packed the tent, the sleeping bags, and a box of matches. Theo's little sister Mia begged to come too, and this time, their parents said yes.

👁 Watch Me Think

This is the BEGINNING of the story. What do I learn here? WHO is in it: Theo, his dad, and now Mia too. WHAT are they about to do: go camping overnight, not just a day hike like before. The beginning sets up everything that's about to happen — it's like the first domino that starts the whole sequence falling.

🧩 Picture It: What a Beginning Does

BEGINNING → introduces WHO is in the story
BEGINNING → introduces WHAT is about to happen
BEGINNING → sets up everything that comes next

🤝 Let's Try Together

Notice this beginning is DIFFERENT from Week 1's beginning (just Theo and his dad, a day hike) — now Mia is coming too, and they're staying overnight. New story, new beginning, even though it's the same family and the same trail.

⛳️ The Middle: Setting Up Camp

The middle of a story is where most of the action happens. Transition words like FIRST, NEXT, and THEN help us follow the order of events. Read what happens next:

FIRST, they found a flat clearing near the stream and pitched the tent, hammering each stake into the ground. NEXT, Theo gathered dry branches while Dad built a ring of stones for the fire. THEN, once the fire crackled to life, Mia helped stir a pot of chili bubbling over the flames.

👁 Watch Me Think

Notice the words FIRST, NEXT, and THEN — those are transition words, and they're doing a lot of work. Without them, I'd have to guess the order things happened. WITH them, I know exactly: tent first, then fire, then food. If I mixed up the order — cooking before there's even a fire — the story wouldn't make sense!

🧩 Picture It: The Middle in Order

FIRST: pitch the tent
NEXT: gather wood, build fire ring
THEN: cook chili over the fire

Each transition word signals: this happens AFTER the last thing.

🤝 Let's Try Together

What would happen if you tried to cook chili BEFORE building the fire? It wouldn't work — there'd be nothing to cook it on! That's why sequence matters: some steps genuinely depend on the ones before them.

⭐ The End: Stars Over the Campsite

The end of a story wraps everything up. Read how Theo's camping trip ends:

LAST, after the dishes were washed in the stream, the family lay back on a blanket and looked up. The sky above the campsite had no city lights to compete with — just thousands of stars, more than Theo had ever counted. Mia fell asleep first, her head on Dad's shoulder. Theo stayed awake a little longer, thinking that some things — like counting stars with your family — are worth staying up for.

👁 Watch Me Think

This is the END. What makes it feel like an ending? The action slows down — no more tasks like pitching a tent or building a fire. Instead, everyone is still, looking at the sky. The word LAST tells me this is the final event. And the story leaves us with a feeling: Theo appreciating the moment. That's what a good ending does — it doesn't just stop, it wraps up the FEELING of the whole story.

🧩 Picture It: Beginning, Middle, End

BEGINNING: Theo asks to camp overnight, packs with his family
MIDDLE: they pitch the tent, build a fire, cook chili (in order!)
END: they watch the stars together, Theo feels grateful

🤝 Let's Try Together

If someone told you ONLY the ending (stars, Mia asleep) without the beginning or middle, would you understand the whole story? Not really — you'd be missing why they're camping at all, and how they got the fire going. That's why sequence matters: each part needs the ones before it to make full sense.
