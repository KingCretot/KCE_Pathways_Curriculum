matter
Anything that has mass and takes up space. Air, water, rocks and your body are all matter.
property
A trait you can observe or measure to describe matter, such as color, hardness, or whether it is magnetic or dissolves in water.
mixture
Two or more substances combined so that each one keeps its own properties and can be separated again. Example: sand and iron filings.

🧊 Properties of Matter

Matter is anything that has mass and takes up space, and it is made of particles far too small to see. Scientists describe matter by its properties: color, hardness, texture, smell, whether it is magnetic, whether it lets electricity or heat pass through, and whether it dissolves in water. Matter can be a solid, a liquid or a gas, and heating or cooling can change its state, as you saw with ice, water and water vapor in the water cycle. Because each material has its own set of properties, scientists can test properties to identify a mystery material, the way a detective uses clues.

👁 Watch Me Think

I have two shiny, silver-colored objects. They look alike, so is color enough to tell what they are made of? No. Let me test another property. I bring a magnet close: one object jumps to the magnet and the other does not. Iron and steel are magnetic, but aluminum is not. So the first is probably steel and the second is probably aluminum. One property can fool me, so I test more than one before I decide.

🤝 Let's Try Together: The Mystery Powders

Three cups hold white powders: salt, cornstarch and baking soda. They look the same, and in a lab we never taste anything. Let's test properties instead. Test 1: stir a spoonful of each into water. Powder A disappears completely and the water stays clear. Powder B makes the water cloudy and settles to the bottom. Test 2: add a few drops of vinegar to a dry spoonful of each. Only Powder C fizzes. Our conclusion: A is salt (it dissolves), B is cornstarch (it does not dissolve) and C is baking soda (it fizzes with vinegar).

🥣 Mixtures and Solutions

A mixture is two or more substances combined so that each one keeps its own properties. In a bowl of trail mix, the raisins are still raisins and the peanuts are still peanuts, so you can separate them again. A solution is a special mixture in which one substance dissolves evenly into another, like salt in water or the gases mixed together in air. Even though you can't see the salt in salt water, it is still there, and you can get it back. We separate mixtures using the properties of their parts: a magnet pulls out iron, a filter catches grains that are too big to pass, and evaporation leaves dissolved salt behind when the water turns to vapor.

👁 Watch Me Think

My little brother says the salt in his glass of water is gone because he can't see it. How could I gather evidence that it is still there? I could weigh the glass before and after adding salt: if the mass went up and stayed up, the salt is still in the water. I could also leave a dish of the salt water in the sun. When the water evaporates, salt crystals will be left behind. Two different tests that point to the same answer make my evidence strong.

🤝 Let's Try Together: Separate the Super Mixture

A jar holds gravel, sand, iron filings and salt, all stirred together. Let's plan the steps using properties. Step 1: pour it through a screen. The gravel is too big to pass, so it stays on top. Step 2: sweep a magnet (wrapped in a plastic bag) through what is left. The iron filings stick to it. Step 3: add water and stir. The salt dissolves, but the sand does not. Step 4: pour it through filter paper. The sand stays in the filter, and salt water drips through. Step 5: let the salt water evaporate in a sunny dish. Salt crystals are left. All four parts are back!

⚖️ Chemical Changes and Conservation of Mass

In a physical change, matter changes size, shape or state, but it is still the same substance, like ice melting or paper being torn. In a chemical change, the particles rearrange to make a new substance with new properties. Clues of a chemical change include gas bubbles, a new color, a new smell, light or heat being given off, or a new solid forming. Rusting iron, burning wood, baking a cake and mixing baking soda with vinegar are all chemical changes. In every physical or chemical change, matter is never created or destroyed, so in a closed container the total mass stays the same. This rule is called conservation of mass.

👁 Watch Me Think

A student mixed baking soda and vinegar in an open cup on a scale, and afterward the mass was lower. Was some matter destroyed? Let me look at the clues. The mixture bubbled, and bubbles are a gas. The gas, carbon dioxide, floated up out of the open cup into the air, so the scale could not weigh it. To test my idea, I would repeat the experiment in a sealed bottle with a balloon over the top. If my thinking is right, the balloon fills with gas and the mass stays the same.

🤝 Let's Try Together: The Sealed Bag Test

Let's test conservation of mass ourselves. Put an ice cube in a zip-top bag, seal it, and weigh it: 52 grams. Wait an hour until the ice melts and weigh again: 52 grams. The water changed state, but nothing left the bag, so the mass did not change. Now try it with the bag open on a hot day and wait much longer. Some water may evaporate and escape into the air, and the scale reads a little less. That is not matter being destroyed. It is matter leaving the system. Closing the container is what makes the test fair.

🧪 Chemists Who Changed the World

These scientists studied the properties of matter, mixed and changed substances, and measured carefully. Their work saved lives, protected the planet and proved a law of nature.

Alice Ball

1892–1916

Alice Ball grew up in Seattle, Washington, and studied chemistry and pharmacy at the University of Washington. In 1915 she became the first woman and the first African American to earn a master's degree from the College of Hawaii, now the University of Hawaiʻi. Doctors treated leprosy, now called Hansen's disease, with oil from the chaulmoogra tree, but the thick oil was hard to swallow and painful to inject. Ball chemically changed the oil into a form the body could absorb by injection. Her 'Ball Method' was used to treat patients for about 25 years, until better medicines arrived in the 1940s. She died at just 24, and for years another chemist got the credit, until others made sure her name was remembered.

Mario Molina

1943–2020

Mario Molina was born in Mexico City. As a boy, he turned a bathroom in his family's home into a chemistry lab. In 1974, working with F. Sherwood Rowland in California, he showed that chemicals called CFCs, used in spray cans and refrigerators, rise high into the sky and destroy ozone. The ozone layer protects living things from the Sun's harmful ultraviolet rays. Their warning led countries to sign the Montreal Protocol in 1987 and phase out CFCs. In 1995 Molina shared the Nobel Prize in Chemistry, the first Mexican-born scientist to win it.

Antoine and Marie-Anne Lavoisier

1743–1794 and 1758–1836

In France in the 1770s and 1780s, Antoine Lavoisier weighed substances very carefully before and after changes such as burning, often in sealed containers. He found that the total mass stayed the same, which is the law of conservation of mass. He also named oxygen and hydrogen. Marie-Anne Paulze Lavoisier was his partner in the lab: she recorded results, translated science writing from English, and made the detailed drawings of equipment in his famous 1789 chemistry book. After Antoine died in 1794, Marie-Anne kept sharing their work with other scientists.

Stephanie Kwolek

1923–2014

Stephanie Kwolek was a chemist at the DuPont company. In 1965 she made a thin, cloudy liquid that looked like a failure, and many chemists would have thrown it out. She noticed its unusual properties and convinced a coworker to spin it into fibers. The fibers were amazingly strong: the material, later named Kevlar, is about five times stronger than steel of the same weight. Today it is used in protective vests, helmets, gloves, ropes and tires. She was inducted into the National Inventors Hall of Fame in 1995.
