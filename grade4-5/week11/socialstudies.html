🗺️ The Louisiana Purchase and Lewis and Clark

In 1803, President Thomas Jefferson bought the Louisiana Territory from France for about $15 million, roughly doubling the size of the United States. But France did not really own that land: dozens of Indigenous nations, such as the Osage, Lakota, Pawnee and Mandan, lived there. France sold only its claim, meaning the right to take control ahead of other European countries. In 1804, Jefferson sent Meriwether Lewis and William Clark on an expedition up the Missouri River to the Pacific Ocean, and the journey depended on Indigenous nations who fed them, guided them and traded with them.

👁 Watch Me Think

When I hear that the United States "bought" Louisiana, I ask: bought from whom? France had a claim on paper, but the Osage, Lakota and many other nations were living there and governing themselves. So the purchase did not move anyone off the land in 1803. It only decided which outsider would try to take control. To understand what happened next, I have to follow both sides: the government with the paper claim and the nations who lived on the land.

🤝 Let's Try Together: Who Made the Journey Possible?

Let's list who helped. Sacagawea, a Lemhi Shoshone woman, spoke Shoshone and helped the group trade for horses with her own brother, Cameahwait, a Shoshone leader. A woman with a baby also showed other nations that the group was not a war party. York, a Black man enslaved by William Clark, hunted, paddled, scouted and helped care for the sick, like any other member. The Nez Perce fed the starving explorers. In November 1805, the group took a vote on where to spend the winter, and Clark wrote down York's and Sacagawea's choices. So who made the journey possible? Many more people than just Lewis and Clark.

📜 The Cherokee Nation and the Indian Removal Act

The Cherokee Nation lived in the Southeast, in parts of today's Georgia, Tennessee, North Carolina and Alabama. By 1825, thanks to Sequoyah's syllabary, thousands of Cherokee people could read and write their own language. In 1827, the Cherokee wrote a constitution and governed from their capital, New Echota, and in 1828 they began printing the Cherokee Phoenix, a newspaper in Cherokee and English. But white settlers wanted the land, especially after gold was found in north Georgia in 1829. In 1830, President Andrew Jackson pushed Congress to pass the Indian Removal Act, a law that let the president trade for Indigenous land east of the Mississippi River and move the nations west.

👁 Watch Me Think

I notice that the Cherokee did everything many white leaders said they should do: they wrote a constitution, printed a newspaper and ran schools. Yet Georgia still wanted them gone. So I ask: was removal really about how the Cherokee lived? The evidence points to land and gold. When a reason given for a policy does not match the facts, a historian looks for the real reason underneath.

🤝 Let's Try Together: A Close Vote in Congress

The Indian Removal Act did not pass easily. In the Senate, the vote was 28 to 19. In the House of Representatives, it was 102 to 97. Let's do the math: 102 minus 97 is 5, so if just three House members had switched sides, the law would have failed. Who voted no? Many members from the North, some religious groups who sent petitions, and even Davy Crockett, a congressman from Tennessee. Jackson signed the law on May 28, 1830. A close vote tells a historian that Americans at the time disagreed, so removal was a choice, not something that had to happen.

⚖️ The Courts, a False Treaty and the Trail of Tears

The Cherokee fought removal with laws, not weapons. In 1832, in the case Worcester v. Georgia, Chief Justice John Marshall and the Supreme Court ruled that the Cherokee Nation was a distinct community with its own sovereignty, where Georgia's laws had no force. Jackson did nothing to enforce the ruling. In 1835, a small group of Cherokee men with no permission from their government signed the Treaty of New Echota, giving up the homeland; Principal Chief John Ross sent Congress a petition against it signed by more than 15,000 Cherokee people. In 1838, U.S. soldiers forced about 16,000 Cherokee people from their homes into camps and then on a march west in fall and winter, and thousands died; this became known as the Trail of Tears.

👁 Watch Me Think

I want to say this plainly and fairly. The Cherokee won in the Supreme Court, and they still lost their homeland, because the president would not enforce the ruling. That shows something important about government: a court decision only protects people if the other branches carry it out. Remember checks and balances from Week 9? Here, the check failed. Then I ask who paid the cost: Cherokee families, and also the Black people the wealthiest Cherokee families enslaved, who were forced to make the march too.

🤝 Let's Try Together: Did Jackson Really Say It?

You may read that after Worcester v. Georgia, Jackson said, "John Marshall has made his decision; now let him enforce it." Let's check it like historians. Is there a speech or letter where Jackson wrote those words? No. The quote first appeared in a book by Horace Greeley, a newspaper editor, more than 30 years later. So most historians doubt Jackson said those exact words. But here is what the evidence does show: Jackson did not use his power to protect the Cherokee, and he kept pushing for removal. A famous quote can be shaky even when the action behind it is real.

🔍 Source Detective: The Cherokee Speak to the Nation

In July 1830, just weeks after the Indian Removal Act became law, the General Council of the Cherokee Nation, led by Principal Chief John Ross, wrote an Address to the People of the United States, which newspapers printed. Two years later, the Supreme Court gave its ruling in Worcester v. Georgia. Read a short part of each. The words are the real ones; the ... marks show where words were left out.

Primary Sources: Address of the Cherokee Nation to the People of the United States (1830) and Worcester v. Georgia (1832)

Part 1, the General Council of the Cherokee Nation, July 1830: "We are aware that some persons suppose it will be for our advantage to remove beyond the Mississippi. We think otherwise. Our people universally think otherwise... We wish to remain on the land of our fathers. We have a perfect and original right to remain without interruption or molestation. The treaties with us, and laws of the United States made in pursuance of treaties, guaranty our residence and our privileges and secure us against intruders. Our only request is that these treaties may be fulfilled, and these laws executed."

Part 2, Chief Justice John Marshall, 1832: "The Cherokee nation, then, is a distinct community occupying its own territory... in which the laws of Georgia can have no force."

Word help: universally = all, every one; molestation = being bothered or attacked; in pursuance of = following; guaranty = promise, protect; intruders = people who come in without permission; executed = carried out; distinct = separate.

Address to the People of the United States, General Council of the Cherokee Nation, July 1830, printed in the Cherokee Phoenix, July 24, 1830; Worcester v. Georgia, 31 U.S. 515 (1832), opinion of Chief Justice John Marshall. Real wording, shortened; ... marks show where words were left out.

👁 Think Like a Historian

First I check who is speaking. Part 1 is the Cherokee Nation speaking for itself, so it is a primary source from the people facing removal. Part 2 is the Supreme Court. Then I look at the kind of argument each one uses. The Cherokee do not ask for pity; they point to treaties and laws. When a writer uses the other side's own laws as evidence, that is a strong strategy.

🧑‍🤝‍🧑 People of the West and the Removal Era

These people shaped what happened as the United States pushed west. Read each card and ask: what power did this person have, and how did they use it?

Sacagawea

about 1788 – 1812 (debated)

A Lemhi Shoshone woman who was captured as a girl by Hidatsa raiders. In 1805 she set out with Lewis and Clark, carrying her newborn son. She translated, found food plants, and helped the group get horses from her brother's people. Most historians accept a written record that she died in 1812, but some Shoshone oral histories say she lived to old age in Wyoming.

York

about 1770 – unknown

A Black man enslaved by William Clark, and a full member of the expedition who hunted, scouted and voted on where to camp. Many nations along the way had never seen a Black man and treated him with great interest. After the journey, York asked for his freedom, and Clark refused for years. York was freed only long after the expedition ended.

Sequoyah

about 1770 – 1843

A Cherokee silversmith who could not read any language, yet spent years creating a syllabary of 86 symbols for the Cherokee language. After the Cherokee council accepted it in 1821, thousands of Cherokee people learned to read within a few years. His syllabary is still used today.

John Ross (Guwisguwi)

1790 – 1866

Principal Chief of the Cherokee Nation from 1828 to 1866. He fought removal with lawsuits, petitions and trips to Washington, never with war. When removal came anyway, he led his people west and helped rebuild the Cherokee Nation in Indian Territory, today's Oklahoma.

Andrew Jackson

1767 – 1845

The seventh president, from 1829 to 1837. Many white Americans admired him as a war hero and a leader for ordinary people. He was also an enslaver: when he died in 1845, about 150 enslaved people lived and worked at his Tennessee plantation, the Hermitage. He pushed the Indian Removal Act through Congress and did not enforce the Supreme Court's ruling that protected the Cherokee.

Samuel Worcester

1798 – 1859

A white missionary who lived in the Cherokee Nation and helped print the Cherokee Phoenix. Georgia jailed him for refusing to get a state permit to live on Cherokee land. His case reached the Supreme Court in 1832, and he won. In 1835 he moved to Indian Territory, where he kept printing in the Cherokee language.
