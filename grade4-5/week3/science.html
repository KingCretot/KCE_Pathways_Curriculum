🦆 Structural Adaptations: Bodies Built to Survive

An adaptation is an inherited trait that helps a living thing survive and reproduce in its environment. A structural adaptation is a body part or feature: its shape, size, covering or color. A polar bear's thick fur and layer of fat under the skin hold in heat in the freezing Arctic. A duck's webbed feet push water like paddles, and an Arctic fox's white winter fur gives it camouflage, helping it blend in with snow. Plants have structural adaptations too: a cactus stores water in its thick stem, and its spines, which are actually leaves, lose very little water.

👁 Watch Me Think

When I see a body part, I ask: what problem in this environment does it solve? A hawk has a sharp, hooked beak and strong, curved claws called talons. Its problem is that it eats animals it must catch and tear apart. So those structures are adaptations for hunting. Then I check one more thing: is it inherited? Yes. Hawks are born with what they need to grow those beaks and talons. They do not learn them.

🧩 Picture It: Structure and the Problem It Solves

  • Arctic fox: white winter fur. Helps it hide in snow (camouflage).
  • Camel: a hump of fat and long eyelashes. Stores energy and blocks blowing sand.
  • Hummingbird: a long, thin beak. Reaches nectar deep in flowers.
  • Cactus: spines and waxy skin. Saves water and keeps animals away.

🤝 Let's Try Together: The Snowshoe Hare

A snowshoe hare has very large back feet. Let's reason it through. Its environment has deep, soft snow. Big feet spread the hare's weight out, like snowshoes, so it does not sink as it runs from a lynx. So the large feet are a structural adaptation for moving on snow. Notice the steps: environment, problem, structure, how it helps.

🦋 Behavioral Adaptations: Instinct or Learned?

A behavioral adaptation is something an organism does that helps it survive. Many monarch butterflies migrate up to about 3,000 miles to central Mexico for the winter, ground squirrels hibernate, and opossums play dead when attacked. Some behaviors are instincts: the animal does them correctly the first time, with no one to teach it, like a spider spinning its first web. Others are learned behaviors, gained through experience or by watching others, like young chimpanzees learning from their mothers to fish for termites with sticks. Inherited traits are passed from parents to offspring, but learned skills and things that happen during a life, like a scar, are not.

👁 Watch Me Think

To sort a behavior, I ask: could the animal do it the very first time, alone, with no one to copy? A baby sea turtle hatches at night and crawls toward the brightest horizon, which is usually the ocean. Its mother left the nest weeks earlier, so no one taught it. That is evidence of an instinct. A dolphin doing a trick for a trainer is different: it learned the trick through practice and rewards. Many behaviors are a mix: the urge is instinct, and the skill improves by learning.

A kitten raised away from its mother still pounces on a moving string. But kittens that watch their mothers hunt become better hunters. Let's sort it together. A scientist would say this behavior is partly inherited and partly learned.

🌍 When Environments Change

Environments change: droughts, floods, new predators, new cities, warmer temperatures. Members of a population are not all alike; their traits vary a little. When the environment changes, individuals whose traits fit the new conditions are more likely to survive and have offspring, and they pass those traits on. Over many generations the whole population changes, and scientists call this kind of change evolution. If a species cannot survive, move away, or change over generations fast enough, it can become extinct, with no members left alive.

👁 Watch Me Think

Let me look at peppered moths in England. In the 1800s, smoke from factories covered tree bark near cities with dark soot. Light-colored moths stood out on the dark bark, and birds ate more of them. Dark moths were hidden, so more of them survived and had young. Soon most moths near those cities were dark. Did any moth change its color to hide? No! Both colors already existed. The environment changed which moths survived. Later, after laws cleaned up the air, light moths became common again.

🧩 Picture It: Peppered Moth Timeline

  • Before the 1800s: Tree bark is light. Most peppered moths are light.
  • Late 1800s, near factory cities: Soot darkens the bark. Most moths there are dark.
  • After clean-air laws (1950s and later): Bark gets lighter again. Light moths become common again.

🤝 Let's Try Together: Predict the Hares

Imagine an island where winters become much snowier for many years. Some hares on the island have lighter fur than others. Let's predict. Lighter hares are harder for foxes to spot on snow, so more of them survive and have young. After many generations we would expect more light-furred hares. To check, scientists would count fur colors in the population every year and compare.

🔬 Data Lab: The Finches and the Drought

In 1977 a severe drought hit Daphne Major, a tiny island in the Galápagos. Plants made few seeds. Small, soft seeds were eaten up fast, and mostly large, hard seeds were left. Scientists Peter and Rosemary Grant had measured almost every medium ground finch on the island. Study the data and decide what it shows. 🧑‍🔬 Scientists Who Studied How Life Works

Our featured scientist studied living cells in sea animals. Two more scientists studied animal behavior and how populations change. Notice how each one used careful evidence.

Ernest Everett Just

1883–1941

Born in Charleston, South Carolina, Just graduated from Dartmouth College with high honors in 1907 and taught at Howard University for most of his career. Each summer he studied the eggs of sea animals such as sea urchins and marine worms at the Marine Biological Laboratory in Woods Hole, Massachusetts. He showed how the outer surface of an egg cell changes the moment it is fertilized, and argued that the cell's surface helps control how life develops. In 1915 he received the first Spingarn Medal from the NAACP. Because racism kept him out of jobs at major American universities and labs, he did much of his later research in Europe.

Charles Henry Turner

1867–1923

A zoologist from Cincinnati, Ohio, Turner earned a PhD from the University of Chicago in 1907 and taught high school in St. Louis. He trained honeybees in experiments on colors and patterns, and his results suggested that bees can tell them apart. He also showed that insects such as ants and cockroaches can learn and remember, so learning is not only for animals with big brains.

Peter and Rosemary Grant

both born 1936

These British-born biologists began studying finches on the tiny Galápagos island of Daphne Major in 1973, measuring the birds year after year. They watched the population's beaks change after the 1977 drought, showing that evolution can happen fast enough for scientists to measure.
