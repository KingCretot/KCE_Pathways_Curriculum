🏝️ Tenochtitlan and the Inca Roads

In about 1325, the Mexica (meh-SHEE-kah) people founded Tenochtitlan on an island in Lake Texcoco, in central Mexico. They built causeways, raised roads across the water, to connect the city to the shore, and an aqueduct carried in fresh water from springs. On chinampas, farm fields built up from lake mud, they grew corn, beans and squash. Far to the south, in the Andes Mountains of South America, the Inca built a road network called the Qhapaq Ñan (KAH-pahk NYAHN), with rope bridges and relay runners called chasquis who carried messages. Historians estimate the roads stretched for more than 18,000 miles.

👁 Watch Me Think

When I read that the Mexica built a city on an island, I ask: what problems would that cause? They need food, fresh water and a way to reach land. Then I look for the solutions in the text: chinampas for food, an aqueduct for water, causeways for travel. Each problem has an engineering answer. That tells me the Mexica were skilled planners, not just lucky.

🧩 Picture It: Tenochtitlan Around 1500

Picture a wide lake in a high valley ringed by mountains. In the middle sits an island city with canals instead of many streets, full of canoes. Three long causeways stretch like spokes to the shore. Around the edges lie rows of green chinampas. In the center is a huge plaza with the Templo Mayor, the great temple. Historians estimate that 200,000 or more people may have lived there. Today, Mexico City stands on the same spot, and most of the lake has been drained.

🤝 Let's Try Together: Problem and Solution

Problem: an island city needs more farmland. Solution: build chinampas in the shallow lake. Problem: Inca leaders need news from far away, fast. Solution: chasquis ran in relays along the roads, each runner passing the message to the next. Let's say it together: geography created a problem, and people engineered a solution.

🌲 Pacific Northwest Nations

Along the rainy coast of today's Alaska, British Columbia in Canada, and Washington State live nations such as the Tlingit (KLINK-it), Haida (HY-duh) and Kwakwaka'wakw. Giant cedar trees and rivers full of salmon gave them rich resources, so they built large, settled villages without needing big farms. Families lived in large plank houses made of cedar boards, and carvers made tall poles that show family crests and histories. At a potlatch, a host family gives gifts and shares food to mark an important event, such as a birth, a marriage or a new leader. Canada banned the potlatch from 1884 to 1951, but many families kept it alive, and it is still practiced today.

👁 Watch Me Think

The text says these nations built large villages without big farms. That surprised me, because many societies farm so they can stay in one place. So I look for the reason: salmon returned to the rivers every year, and the forests gave cedar for houses, canoes and clothing. A dependable food supply let people stay put. Geography explains the how.

🧩 Picture It: A Plank House

Picture a long building made of split cedar planks, with a low doorway. Inside, several related families share one big room, each with its own space, around fires in the middle. A tall carved pole stands at the front, showing crest animals such as Raven, Eagle or Bear that tell who the family is and where it came from.

🤝 Let's Try Together: Whose Story? The Potlatch Ban

In 1884 the Canadian government banned the potlatch. Who wanted the ban? Government officials and missionaries who wanted Indigenous families to give up their traditions. Who paid the cost? The Kwakwaka'wakw and other nations, who lost a way to pass down names, rights and history. How do we know? From the law itself, and from the oral histories of families who kept holding potlatches in secret.

🐪 Ghana, Mali and Songhai: Gold, Salt and Learning

In West Africa, three great empires rose one after another: Ghana, then Mali, then Songhai. They grew rich by controlling trade: gold came from mines to the south, and salt came from the Sahara Desert to the north. Camel caravans crossed the desert carrying salt, cloth, books and other goods. The city of Timbuktu became a center of learning, where scholars studied and copied books on religion, law, math, astronomy and medicine. Thousands of these handwritten books, called manuscripts, are still kept by Timbuktu families and libraries today.

👁 Watch Me Think

Why would anyone trade gold for salt? Gold is beautiful, but people need salt to stay healthy in a hot climate and to keep food from spoiling. The desert had salt but no gold. The lands to the south had gold but little salt. Each side needed what the other had, so whoever controlled the trade routes in the middle grew rich. That is the secret of Ghana, Mali and Songhai.

🤝 Let's Try Together: Follow the Caravan

Step 1: a caravan loads blocks of salt in the Sahara. Step 2: camels carry it south across the desert for weeks. Step 3: in trading cities, salt is traded for gold from the south. Step 4: the emperor taxes the trade, so the empire grows rich. Now ask: who benefited? Rulers and traders. Who did the hardest work? Miners and camel drivers, and some salt miners in the desert were enslaved people.

🔍 Source Detective: Gold in Cairo

In 1324, Mansa Musa passed through Cairo, Egypt, on his pilgrimage to Mecca. A writer named al-Umari, who lived in Cairo a few years later, collected reports from people who had seen the visit. Read his words carefully.

Primary Source: Al-Umari describes Mansa Musa's visit to Cairo

"This man [Mansa Musa] flooded Cairo with his benefactions. He left no court emir nor holder of a royal office without the gift of a load of gold."

"Gold was at a high price in Egypt until they came in that year. ... From that time its value fell ... and has remained cheap till now."

Word help: benefactions = gifts; court emir = an important official of Egypt's ruler; till now = about 12 years later, when al-Umari was writing.

Ibn Fadl Allah al-Umari, Masalik al-Absar (written in the 1330s). English translation in Levtzion and Hopkins, Corpus of Early Arabic Sources for West African History. Shortened.

👁 Think Like a Historian

A historian asks: who wrote this, and when? Was the writer an eyewitness? Al-Umari was not there in 1324. He talked to people in Cairo who were. That makes his account very useful, but secondhand. I also notice he describes something he could check himself: the price of gold, years later.

👥 People Who Shaped Their World

Meet leaders and artists from West Africa, the Andes and the Pacific Northwest. Notice how each one helped their people's power, knowledge or art last.

Sundiata Keita

Founded Mali about 1235

The founder of the Mali Empire. His life story has been kept for centuries by griots, West African oral historians who pass history down through speech and song.

Mansa Musa

Ruled Mali about 1312–1337

Emperor of Mali. On his pilgrimage to Mecca in 1324, he spent and gave away so much gold that its value fell in Cairo. He brought back scholars and an architect, and he supported mosques and learning in Timbuktu.

Pachacuti

Ruled about 1438–1471

The Inca ruler who turned a small kingdom around the city of Cusco into a large empire. He and the rulers after him expanded the road system that tied the empire together.

Jennie Thlunaut

About 1891–1986

A master Tlingit Chilkat weaver from Klukwan, Alaska. Chilkat robes are woven from mountain goat wool and cedar bark and can take a year or more to make. She taught younger weavers so the art would not be lost.

Bill Reid

1920–1998

A Haida artist and carver whose mother was Haida. He studied old Haida carvings and helped bring Haida art back to wide attention. One of his huge sculptures of a canoe full of figures stands at the Vancouver airport.
