🖼️ Picture It: Inside a Persuasive Letter

A persuasive letter tries to convince a reader to agree and to act. A strong one states a clear claim, backs each reason with evidence, states the other side’s counterargument fairly, answers it with a rebuttal, and ends by politely asking the reader to act. Transitions guide the reader from point to point.

Model LetterA Bike Rack for the Library

A made-up example

PartModel sentence
GreetingDear Library Board,
ClaimPlease add a bike rack by the front door of our library.
Reason + evidenceFirst, riders need a safe place to lock up. On three visits, I counted bikes leaning on the railing each time.
CounterargumentSome people worry that a rack would block the sidewalk.
RebuttalHowever, a rack along the side wall would leave the sidewalk clear.
Closing + call to actionFor these reasons, I urge you to approve a bike rack. Sincerely,
🚧

Three Reasoning Traps

Bandwagon: “Everybody wants it, so you should too.” Popularity is not proof. Overgeneralization: “Kids NEVER clean up.” Words like always, never, everyone, and no one claim too much. Attacking the person: “Only grumpy people would vote no.” Insults are not reasons. A strong argument on EITHER side avoids all three.

🌟 Vocabulary Round-Up

Flip each card to review today’s words. The last two come straight from today’s texts.

counterargument
The other side’s point, which a strong writer states fairly before answering it.
rebuttal
A writer’s answer to the counterargument.
transition
A word or phrase that shows how ideas connect, such as however or as a result.
bandwagon
A reasoning trap that says to agree because many people do.
overgeneralization
A claim about ALL of a group, often with always or never, without enough evidence.
compromise
An agreement in which each side gives up part of what it wants.
shielded
Covered so that something is blocked. “If lights are ever added, city rules would require shielded lights that point down toward the ground.”
hazard
A danger. “That is a hazard for younger children.”

⚖️ Judging an Argument, Fairly

🪜

Four Tests for Any Argument

  1. Relevant? Does the evidence really connect to the reason and the claim?
  2. Enough? Is there enough evidence, or just one example?
  3. Reliable? Where did it come from? Could it be checked?
  4. Fair reasoning? Does the speaker avoid bandwagon, overgeneralization, and attacking the person, and treat the other side fairly?
SpeakerStrong points and weak spots
Nizhoni (Site 1): strongCounted real skaters in traffic; states and answers the noise worry
Nizhoni: weakCounted on one day only; the survey did not ask about sites; a bandwagon ending
Mr. Nolan (Site 2): strongUses the fact sheet’s distances; admits the bus-stop problem and offers a fix
Mr. Nolan: weakNo proof for “many of us work night shifts”; an overgeneralization at the end
🎯

Judge the Reasoning, Not the Side

Both speakers want a skate park; they disagree about where. Each makes strong points, and each slips into one trap. Your job as a reader is not to decide who is “nice” but to ask which reasons have the best support, and whether a compromise could answer both sides’ worries.

📝 Writer’s Turn: Your Letter to the City Council

Your Writing Is Checked by Three Fair Rules

You may argue for EITHER site. Your answer must (1) have enough words, (2) not copy 4 or more words in a row from the speeches or the fact sheet (names like Site 1 can stay), and (3) include every key idea listed. Write one or two sentences in your own words. If a rule is missed, the feedback tells you which one.

+6

Write the opening of your letter: state a CLAIM for Site 1 OR Site 2, then give one reason, starting the reason with a transition such as first, one reason, or to begin.

Ideas to choose from: Site 1 is in the neighborhood, near a bus stop, and gets skaters out of parking lots. Site 2 is farther from homes and already has sidewalks and parking.
  • Use at least 16 words.
  • Do not copy 4 or more words in a row from the speeches or the fact sheet.
  • Name the site you choose: Site 1 or Site 2.
  • State your claim with a word like should, urge, or please.
  • Begin your reason with a transition such as first or one reason.

+6

Write a counterargument and rebuttal: state the OTHER side’s strongest point fairly, then answer it, using a transition such as however or on the other hand.

Remember: Site 1 has no crosswalk yet and is close to homes. Site 2 has no bus stop yet and is 1.5 miles from the east side.
  • Use at least 18 words.
  • Do not copy 4 or more words in a row from the speeches or the fact sheet.
  • State the other side fairly: begin with words like “Some neighbors worry” or “Supporters argue.”
  • Answer it with a transition such as however or on the other hand.
  • Name the real issue: the crosswalk, the bus stop, the noise, or the distance.

+6

Write the closing of your letter: restate your claim politely, give a short reason, and ask the City Council to act. Avoid every reasoning trap.

Avoid: bandwagon (“everybody wants it”), overgeneralization (“skaters never…”), and attacking the person.
  • Use at least 16 words.
  • Do not copy 4 or more words in a row from the speeches or the fact sheet.
  • Restate your claim: name the site or the skate park.
  • Ask the council to act: please, urge, vote, or approve.
  • Give a short reason with because, since, or for these reasons.
