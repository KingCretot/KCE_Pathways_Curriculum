🌎 How People Change Earth

People change Earth in many ways, and scientists sort the changes into four areas: land, water, air and living things. On land, clearing forests and grasslands for farms, roads and cities removes the habitats where plants and animals live. In water, sewage, oil and trash can pollute rivers, lakes and oceans. In the air, burning fuels in cars, factories and power plants releases pollutants, substances that make air, water or soil harmful. Living things suffer when their habitats disappear or when poisons build up in their food. By 1982, only about 22 California condors were left in the world, and the National Park Service says lead poisoning is still the main cause of death for condors. Some changes reach high into the sky. High above the clouds, the ozone layer shields Earth from much of the Sun's harmful ultraviolet radiation. Chemicals called chlorofluorocarbons (CFCs), once used in refrigerators and spray cans, drifted up and thinned that layer. Impacts do not always fall on everyone equally. When researchers mapped the landfills of Houston, Texas, in 1979, they found that three of the city's four privately owned landfills were in Black neighborhoods, even though Black residents were a minority of the city's population. Working so that every community gets the same protection from pollution is called environmental justice. The good news is that many impacts can be reversed. Scientists measure a problem, find its cause, test a solution and keep measuring to check whether it works. People who protect Earth's natural resources this way are practicing conservation.

👁 Watch Me Think

Pooja, in Sacramento, California, is sorting news headlines for her science journal. "Smoke from a factory chimney" goes under air. "Trash floating in a river" goes under water. "A forest cleared for a parking lot" goes under land, and it also hurts living things, because the animals there lose their homes. Here is how I think it through: many impacts land in more than one area at once. So when I judge a solution, I ask three questions. What is the cause? Which areas does it harm? Does the solution stop the cause, or does it only clean up the effect? A solution that stops the cause usually helps every area at once.

💨 Clearing the Air and Healing the Ozone Layer

Air pollution can harm people's health and the environment. The Clean Air Act of 1970 allowed the nation to set rules limiting pollution from factories and power plants and from cars and trucks, and the Environmental Protection Agency (EPA) was created on December 2, 1970, to carry out those rules. The results show up in the data. The EPA reports that between 1970 and 2025, total emissions of the six principal air pollutants dropped by 78 percent, even though the economy, driving and the population all grew. Lead is a striking example. Lead, a poisonous metal, was once added to gasoline. After the permanent phase-out of leaded gasoline, the EPA says airborne lead concentrations in the United States decreased 98 percent between 1980 and 2005. The ozone layer has a comeback story too. In Week 11 you met Mario Molina, who warned in 1974 that CFCs could destroy ozone. In August 1986, a team led by the atmospheric chemist Susan Solomon traveled to McMurdo Station in Antarctica. Their data showed high levels of a chlorine chemical released when CFCs break apart, evidence that CFCs were destroying the ozone. In 1987 nations agreed to the Montreal Protocol to phase out CFCs. The EPA says it was signed by 197 countries, the first treaty in the history of the United Nations to achieve universal ratification. In 2023, a panel of scientists backed by the United Nations reported that nearly 99 percent of banned ozone-depleting substances have been phased out. If current policies stay in place, the ozone layer should recover to its 1980 values by about 2040 over most of the world, by 2045 over the Arctic and by around 2066 over Antarctica.

👁 Watch Me Think

Portia, in Saint Paul, Minnesota, asks a sharp question: "If CFCs have mostly been phased out, why will the ozone over Antarctica take until about 2066 to heal?" Here is how I reason it out. A NASA Goddard video explains that CFCs can last 50 to 100 years or more in the atmosphere, and some are still released from old appliances in landfills. So the CFCs made long ago are still drifting up and destroying ozone, a little less each year. A solution can be working even when the results take decades. That is why scientists keep measuring: each new ozone report shows whether the healing is on track.

🤝 Let's Try Together: Percent Drops

Let's turn a percent drop into an amount. Suppose a city's air once held 100 units of lead. A 98 percent drop means 98 of every 100 units are gone, so only 100 minus 98, or 2 units, are left. Now try 300 units: 2 percent of 300 is 2 times 3, or 6 units left. The trick: subtract the percent drop from 100 to find the percent that is LEFT, then find that percent of the starting amount. Try It at Home, with a grown-up: look up today's air quality forecast for your town on the EPA's AirNow website (airnow.gov). Record it for five days and look for a pattern.

🦅 Bringing Living Things Back

When a kind of plant or animal is in danger of disappearing forever, scientists call it endangered. On December 28, 1973, the Endangered Species Act became law in the United States to protect endangered species. The World Wildlife Fund says 99 percent of the species covered by the Act have avoided extinction. The American alligator was first protected in 1967, after poaching had severely reduced its numbers. In 1987 the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service removed it from the endangered list, saying it was biologically secure throughout its range. The California condor, a vulture with a 9- to 10-foot wingspan, came even closer to disappearing. There were about 60 condors when it was listed as endangered in 1967 and only about 22 by 1982. By 1987, every remaining wild condor had been placed into a captive breeding program, and in 1992 the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service began releasing captive-bred condors into the wild. By 2022 there were 561 California condors: 347 in the wild and 214 in captivity. Condors survive by eating dead animals, including animals that were shot and lost during hunts and the gut piles hunters leave behind. Lead fragments from hunting bullets poison them. So since 2012, the Utah Division of Wildlife Resources has offered free non-lead ammunition to hunters in the Zion area, where condors fly. Communities lead this work too. On May 3, 2022, the Yurok Tribe released condors in Redwood National and State Parks in Northern California, the first condors there since 1892.

👁 Watch Me Think

Porter lives in St. George, Utah, near Zion National Park, and his uncle hunts deer in the fall. Porter wonders whether one hunter's choice of bullets really matters to condors. Here is how I reason with the Utah Division of Wildlife Resources numbers. More than 22,000 hunters have received coupons for free non-lead ammunition. In recent years, about 80 percent of hunters report using non-lead ammunition or bringing their gut piles out of the field. But biologists estimate that 85 to 90 percent is needed for the condors to survive. So the program is working, but it is not finished. Every hunter who switches moves the number closer to the goal, and the solution works on the cause: lead in the condors' food.

🤝 Let's Try Together: How Big a Comeback?

Let's measure the condor comeback. In 1982 there were about 22 condors. In 2022 there were 561. Round 561 to 550, then divide: 550 divided by 22 is 25. So there were about 25 times as many condors. Now find the wild share in 2022: 347 out of 561. Half of 561 is about 280, and 347 is more than that, so more than half of all condors now fly free. 👁 Think Like an Environmental Scientist

Polina's cousin says, "The only way to clean the air is for everyone to stop driving." Here is how I test that claim with the data. Driving went UP 197 percent, yet total emissions went DOWN 78 percent. So the air got cleaner even while people drove more. The data cannot tell us every reason by itself. For lead, the EPA credits the permanent phase-out of leaded gasoline along with other national and state rules. Data like this helps us judge which solutions really work.

🧑‍🔬 People Protecting Earth

Scientists and community leaders from different backgrounds gathered evidence, spoke up and brought living things back.

Susan Solomon

born 1956

Susan Solomon is a Chicago native who got hooked on science as a child watching The Undersea World of Jacques Cousteau on television. In August 1986 she led a team to McMurdo Station in Antarctica, and a second expedition followed in 1987. Their data showed high levels of a chlorine chemical released when CFCs break down. She proposed that icy clouds high in the winter sky over Antarctica, called polar stratospheric clouds, give the ozone-destroying reactions surfaces where they can happen quickly. She received the National Medal of Science in 1999. In 2011 she joined the Massachusetts Institute of Technology (MIT) as the Ellen Swallow Richards Professor, named for the water scientist you met in Week 27.

Robert D. Bullard

born 1946

Robert Bullard was born in Elba, Alabama, and earned advanced degrees in sociology, the science of how people live in groups. Today he teaches at Texas Southern University in Houston. In 1979 his wife, the attorney Linda McKeever Bullard, asked him to help map Houston's landfills. His students found that three of the city's four privately owned landfills were in Black neighborhoods. His later research found that, even after accounting for income, Black communities were more often near landfills, chemical plants and other hazards. His 1990 book, Dumping in Dixie: Race, Class, and Environmental Quality, became known as the "environmental justice bible," and he is often called the father of environmental justice.

Tiana Williams-Claussen

Yurok Tribe

Tiana Williams-Claussen is a member of the Yurok Tribe and director of its Wildlife Department, which she helped build from scratch in 2008. To the Yurok, condor feathers decorate regalia worn at world-renewal ceremonies, and condors are believed to carry prayers to help keep the world in balance. The Yurok name for the condor is Prey-go-neesh. After 14 years of work with Redwood National and State Parks, the tribe released its first pair of condors on May 3, 2022. "It's been a hard 14 years," she said. The tribe plans to release 4 to 6 condors a year for at least 20 years.

Archie Carr

1909–1987

Archie Carr was born in Mobile, Alabama, and spent his career as a scientist at the University of Florida. He wrote 11 books and more than 120 scientific articles about sea turtles and their habitats, and his writing drew the first international attention to their decline. He was the founding scientific director of the Sea Turtle Conservancy. In 1991 the Archie Carr National Wildlife Refuge was created along 20.5 miles of Florida beach between Melbourne Beach and Wabasso Beach. It protects the most important loggerhead turtle nesting area in the world.
