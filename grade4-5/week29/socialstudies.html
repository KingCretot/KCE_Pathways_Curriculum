🗽 The Immigration Act of 1965 and New Communities

From 1924 to 1965, a quota system decided who could move to the United States. The National Origins Act of 1924 set a quota, a strict limit, for each country. It barred almost all Asian immigrants and gave most places to a few countries in northern and western Europe. On October 3, 1965, at the base of the Statue of Liberty, President Lyndon B. Johnson signed the Immigration Act of 1965. It ended the quota system based on country of origin. Instead, it gave preference to people with family in the United States and to people with needed skills. After 1970, most immigrants came from countries in Latin America and Asia. In 1970, about 9.6 million people in the United States, or 4.7 percent, had been born in another country. By 2010, the number was 40.0 million, or 12.9 percent. Some newcomers were refugees. After April 1975, when the war in Vietnam ended, many Vietnamese refugees began new lives in the United States.

👁 Watch Me Think

I notice that a law changed who could become our neighbors. Under the 1924 quotas, Germany had a limit of 51,227 immigrants a year, but Australia had only 121, and Asian immigrants were barred. The 1965 law ended that system. Then I look for new communities I can check. A 2013 resolution of the California legislature says that more than 1.5 million Vietnamese refugees became permanent residents and citizens of the United States, and more than 600,000 settled in California. In June 1988, California put up freeway signs officially naming the Vietnamese business district in Westminster and Garden Grove "Little Saigon." So I ask: how do we know how the nation changed? The United States Census counts every person every 10 years, so historians can compare the numbers.

💻 The Computer and Internet Age

During World War II, Grace Hopper, a mathematician in the Navy, became one of the first three programmers of the Mark I, an early computer at Harvard University. In 1952 she created the first compiler, a program that turns instructions people can read into code a machine can follow. She later helped design COBOL, a computer language that by the 1970s was the most widely used in the world. In 1956, mathematician Gladys West began work at a Navy base in Dahlgren, Virginia, where she was the second Black woman hired. She spent years calculating precise models of Earth's shape, and her work became a backbone of GPS, the Global Positioning System. On October 29, 1969, the first message was sent over the ARPANET, a computer network that grew into the internet. In 1989, British scientist Tim Berners-Lee, working at the CERN laboratory in Europe, invented the World Wide Web. On April 30, 1993, CERN made the Web's software free for anyone to use.

👁 Watch Me Think

I notice that the internet and the World Wide Web are not the same thing. The internet is the network that links computers. The Web is the system of linked pages that travels on that network. Here is a true story about the first message. Leonard Kleinrock, a professor at the University of California, Los Angeles, remembers that at 10:30 at night on October 29, 1969, his programmer Charley Kline tried to log in to a computer at the Stanford Research Institute. Kline typed L and asked on the telephone, "Did you get the L?" Then he typed O. When he typed G, the system crashed! So the first message on the internet was "lo." Then I ask: whose story is this? It is the story of mathematicians, programmers and engineers, including women like Grace Hopper and Gladys West whose names were left out of many early histories.

Ronan, a fourth grader in Fredericksburg, Virginia, learns that Gladys West worked at the Navy base in nearby Dahlgren for 42 years. 🌱 Earth Day and Young Citizens

In 1962, biologist Rachel Carson's book Silent Spring warned about the dangers of the pesticide DDT. In 1969, an oil spill near Santa Barbara, California, and a fire on the Cuyahoga River in Cleveland, Ohio, made people talk about pollution. Senator Gaylord Nelson of Wisconsin founded a national day to teach about the environment. On April 22, 1970, the first Earth Day brought together an estimated 20 million people, with events at about 2,000 colleges and universities and about 10,000 elementary and high schools. In December 1970, the Environmental Protection Agency (EPA) was created, and Congress passed the Clean Air Act of 1970 and the Clean Water Act of 1972. Young people were changing civic life too. Before 1971, most states did not let citizens vote until age 21. The 26th Amendment protects the right to vote for citizens 18 and older. It was ratified on July 1, 1971, in just 100 days, faster than any other amendment. Today, young people take part in civic life when they volunteer, serve on a student council or speak at a public meeting.

👁 Watch Me Think

I notice that stories about the river fire often mix fact and myth. The National Park Service says the Cuyahoga fire on June 22, 1969, lasted less than half an hour, and the river had already burned more than 10 times in the century before. Some people say the fire alone caused the new laws. Is that correct? The real story is complex. Changes were already in motion: in 1968, Cleveland voters had approved $100 million for sewers and water treatment. The fire became a symbol after news stories spread it around the nation. Then I look at the 26th Amendment. The National Archives says the slogan "Old enough to fight, old enough to vote" was first heard during World War II and was used again by student activists during the Vietnam War. On July 5, 1971, at the White House ceremony certifying the new amendment, President Richard Nixon invited three 18-year-olds to sign it as witnesses. So I ask: who can take part in civic life? Everyone, even kids who are too young to vote.

🔍 Source Detective: Three Laws from 1965, 1970 and 1971

Here are three short primary sources: parts of two laws and one amendment to the Constitution. Read each part slowly, then ask what each one changed.

Primary Sources: 1965, 1970 and 1971

Part 1 (Immigration Act of 1965, Public Law 89-236, signed October 3, 1965): "No person shall receive any preference or priority or be discriminated against in the issuance of an immigrant visa because of his race, sex, nationality, place of birth, or place of residence . . ."

Part 2 (National Environmental Policy Act, signed January 1, 1970): "The Congress . . . declares that it is the continuing policy of the Federal Government . . . to create and maintain conditions under which man and nature can exist in productive harmony, and fulfill the social, economic, and other requirements of present and future generations of Americans."

Part 3 (26th Amendment to the Constitution, ratified July 1, 1971, Section 1): "The right of citizens of the United States, who are eighteen years of age or older, to vote shall not be denied or abridged by the United States or by any State on account of age."

Word help: preference or priority = being put ahead of others; discriminated against = treated unfairly; immigrant visa = permission to move to the United States to live; nationality = the country a person belongs to; productive harmony = getting along in a way that works well; generations = groups of people born around the same time; denied or abridged = taken away or cut down; on account of = because of.

Part 1: Public Law 89-236, Section 2 (79 Statutes at Large 911), amending Section 202(a) of the Immigration and Nationality Act. Part 2: National Environmental Policy Act of 1969, Public Law 91-190, Section 101(a), signed January 1, 1970 (83 Statutes at Large 852). Part 3: Constitution of the United States, Amendment XXVI, Section 1 (National Archives). Real wording; short parts.

👁 Think Like a Historian

First I check what kind of source each part is. Parts 1 and 2 are laws passed by Congress. Part 3 is an amendment, a change to the Constitution itself, which is much harder to change than a law. Next I ask what each part changed. Part 1 says a person's race or place of birth cannot be used against them when they ask for an immigrant visa, which ended the old country quotas. Part 2 makes caring for nature an official goal of the government, for people today and for "future generations." Part 3 protects the right to vote for citizens 18 and older. Then I notice a pattern from Week 27: like Title VI and Title IX, Parts 1 and 3 name who must not be treated unfairly, and why.

🌟 People Who Changed a Nation

They wrote, organized, calculated and programmed. Read each card and ask: what did this person notice, and how did they help change the country?

Rachel Carson

1907–1964

Born in Springdale, Pennsylvania, she became a junior aquatic biologist for the federal government in 1936, one of only two women there at a professional level. Her 1962 book Silent Spring warned about the pesticide DDT and helped start the modern environmental movement.

Gaylord Nelson

1916–2005

Born in Clear Lake, Wisconsin, he served as governor of Wisconsin and then as a United States senator. He founded Earth Day, and the first one, on April 22, 1970, was organized in about six months.

Grace Hopper

1906–1992

She earned a doctorate in mathematics from Yale in 1934. In the Navy, she was one of the first three programmers of the Mark I. She created the first compiler in 1952 and retired from the Navy as a rear admiral at age 79.

Gladys West

1930–2026

Born in Sutherland, Virginia, she graduated first in her high school class. In 1956 she began work at the Navy base in Dahlgren, Virginia, where she worked for 42 years. Her models of Earth's shape became a backbone of GPS. She died in January 2026 at age 95.

Leonard Kleinrock

Internet pioneer

A professor at the University of California, Los Angeles, he ran the lab where the first message was sent over the ARPANET on October 29, 1969. His programmer, Charley Kline, typed the letters L and O before the system crashed.
