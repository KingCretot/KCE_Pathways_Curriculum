🖼️ Picture It: Myths and Allusions

Long ago, people in ancient Greece, Rome, Hawaiʻi, and many other places told myths: traditional stories, often about gods, heroes, and magical beings, that explain something about the world or teach a lesson. Many myths are so well known that English speakers still use pieces of them every day. When a writer mentions a famous story, person, or event and expects you to recognize it without an explanation, that is an allusion.

Model TextThe Messy Room

An original story

Melvin stared at the mountain of clothes, books, and building blocks covering his bedroom floor. “Cleaning this up,” he sighed, “is going to be a Herculean job.”

StepCracking “a Herculean job”
1. Spot the name“Herculean” comes from Hercules, the Roman name for the Greek hero Heracles.
2. Recall the storyIn the myth, Hercules had to complete twelve almost impossible tasks, called his labors.
3. Find the key featureThe tasks were huge and needed enormous strength.
4. Apply itMelvin means cleaning his room will be a huge, exhausting job.
💡

Myth or Allusion?

A myth is the whole story. An allusion is a quick mention of it, often just a name or a phrase like “a Herculean job.” To understand the allusion, you have to know the myth, so today you will read both.

🌟 Vocabulary Round-Up

Flip each card to review today’s words. The last two come straight from today’s story.

myth
A traditional story, often about gods or heroes, that explains something about the world or teaches a lesson.
allusion
A quick reference to a well-known story, person, or event that the writer expects you to recognize.
origin
The place or story something comes from, such as where a word or phrase began.
mortals
Humans, who do not live forever, unlike the gods in Greek myths.
consequence
Something that happens because of an action or a choice.
retelling
A new telling of an old story. A good retelling keeps the important events accurate.
gilded
Covered with a thin layer of gold. “a huge gilded statue of a man seemed to fly through a ring”
wobbled
Shook or moved unsteadily from side to side. “her knees wobbled anyway.”

Meera, Maddox, and Aunt Maren use phrases that come from myths. Notice each allusion, and notice the paragraph numbers; you will use them.

Text A • Realistic StoryGold Above the Rink

An original story. The words on the wall are the real inscription behind the statue.

1Meera had practiced skating at her neighborhood rink for weeks, but when she stepped onto the ice at Rockefeller Center in New York City, her knees wobbled anyway. At the far end of the rink, against a granite wall, a huge gilded statue of a man seemed to fly through a ring marked with the signs of the zodiac.

2“That’s Prometheus,” said Aunt Maren, gliding up beside her. “In the Greek myth, he stole fire from the gods and gave it to people.” She pointed to the words carved into the wall behind the statue and read them aloud: “Prometheus, teacher in every art, brought the fire that hath proved to mortals a means to mighty ends.”

3Just then, Meera’s cousin Maddox zoomed past, spun twice, and stopped in a spray of ice. Meera rolled her eyes. “Of course,” she muttered. “Maddox has the Midas touch. Everything he tries turns to gold.” Aunt Maren laughed. “Careful,” she said. “Things did not turn out so well for Midas.”

4Meera pushed off again, but on her first left turn, her skates slid out from under her, and she landed with a thump. “Left turns are my Achilles’ heel,” she groaned. Maddox skidded over and held out a hand. “Everybody has one weak spot,” he said. “Mine is stopping without spinning.”

5For the next hour, Maddox coached her. “Bend your knees,” Aunt Maren called from the rail. “Bend your knees!” Maddox repeated in a squeaky voice. “Look where you want to go,” said Aunt Maren. “Want to go!” squeaked Maddox. Aunt Maren shook her head and smiled. “All right, Echo, that’s enough.”

6Finally, Meera leaned into a left turn and glided all the way around without a wobble. She threw her arms up and cheered. Then she turned to her cousin. “Can we come back next Saturday? And the Saturday after that?” Maddox groaned and looked at Aunt Maren. “Uh-oh,” he said. “I think I just opened Pandora’s box.”

🏛️ Text B: The Myth Cards

These six cards retell the myths behind today’s allusions. Each card also tells where the story comes from, because myths change as they are retold. Read closely; you will need every card.

Text B • Informational TextWhere Our Words Come From: Six Myth Cards

Written for this lesson from museum, national park, and dictionary sources

Card 1 • Prometheus (ancient Greece)In ancient Greek myths, Prometheus was a Titan, one of an older family of powerful beings. He took fire from the gods, hid it inside a hollow plant stalk, and carried it to humans. Zeus, the king of the gods, was furious. He punished Prometheus by chaining him to a mountain, and he planned a punishment for humans, too.
Card 2 • Pandora (ancient Greece)Zeus ordered the gods to make Pandora, the first woman, and several gods gave her gifts. She was sent to Epimetheus, the brother of Prometheus. Pandora lifted the lid of a large jar, and troubles such as sickness and hard work flew out into the world. Only hope stayed inside. In the oldest telling, by the Greek poet Hesiod about 2,700 years ago, the container was a jar. In 1508, a Dutch scholar named Erasmus called it a box, and that word stuck. Today, opening a “Pandora’s box” means starting something that leads to many problems no one expected.
Card 3 • King Midas (ancient Greece and Rome)King Midas ruled Phrygia, a land in what is now Turkey. When Midas helped Silenus, a companion of Dionysus, the god of wine, Dionysus offered him any wish. Midas wished that everything he touched would turn to gold. At first he was thrilled, but then his food turned to gold the moment it touched his lips. He begged Dionysus to take the wish back, and the god told him to wash in the river Pactolus. Midas was cured, and the river’s sand sparkled with gold. In the 1800s, the American writer Nathaniel Hawthorne added a daughter who turns to gold, but she is not in the ancient Roman poet Ovid’s telling. Today, someone with “the Midas touch” seems to make everything succeed.
Card 4 • Echo and Narcissus (ancient Rome)In the Roman poet Ovid’s telling, Echo was a nymph who chattered to keep the goddess Juno distracted. As punishment, Juno made Echo able to repeat only the last words she heard. Echo loved a young man named Narcissus, but he turned her away, and she faded until only her voice was left. Later, Narcissus fell in love with his own reflection in a pool and wasted away. A flower grew in his place, and it still carries his name. Today, an echo is a sound that bounces back, and the narcissus is a spring flower.
Card 5 • Achilles (ancient Greece)Achilles was the greatest Greek warrior in the war against Troy. Homer’s ancient poem the Iliad tells of his anger, but it never mentions a weak heel. That detail comes from later tellings: his mother, the sea nymph Thetis, dipped baby Achilles in the River Styx to protect him, holding him by one heel, so that spot stayed unprotected. In the end, Paris of Troy shot an arrow, guided by the god Apollo, that struck Achilles in the heel, and the wound ended his life. Today, an “Achilles’ heel” is one weak spot in someone or something strong. The Achilles tendon, just above your heel, is named after him.
Card 6 • Māui (Hawaiʻi)In a Hawaiian moʻolelo (story), the sun once raced across the sky so fast that Māui’s mother, Hina, could not dry her kapa, a cloth made from bark. Māui braided a strong rope from coconut fiber, carried it to the top of Haleakalā, and snared the sun’s rays. The sun promised to travel slowly, and the days grew longer. Haleakalā, a volcano on the island of Maui, is often translated as “house of the sun.” Today, visitors still travel to the top of Haleakalā to watch the sunrise.

📝 Retell It Right

🪜

Four Steps to Crack Any Allusion

  1. Spot the name or phrase that points to a story (Midas, Echo, Pandora).
  2. Recall the myth: who, what happened, and how it ended.
  3. Find the one key feature the writer is borrowing (a golden touch, one weak spot).
  4. Apply it: explain what the allusion means in THIS sentence.
🧪

Myths Change When They Are Retold

Myths were told aloud and written down by many people over thousands of years, so details changed. An accurate retelling says which version it follows.

  • Pandora: Hesiod’s jar became a “box” when Erasmus wrote about it in 1508.
  • Midas: his golden daughter was added in the 1800s by Nathaniel Hawthorne; Ovid’s Midas suffers because he cannot eat.
  • Achilles: Homer’s Iliad never mentions the weak heel; that detail comes from later tellings.
🎯

Weak or Strong Explanation?

❌ Weak: “Pandora’s box” means a box with something inside.
That reads the words literally and skips the myth.

✅ Strong: “Pandora’s box” means starting something that causes many unexpected problems, because in the myth, troubles flew out when Pandora lifted the lid.
It gives the meaning AND the myth behind it.
