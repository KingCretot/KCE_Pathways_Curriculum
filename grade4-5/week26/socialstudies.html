🏫 From Mendez to Brown: Fighting for Fair Schools

When Sylvia Mendez was 8 years old, she was turned away from the 17th Street School in Westminster, California, and sent to a separate school for Mexican American children. Her parents, Gonzalo and Felícitas Mendez, and four other families sued. On February 18, 1946, a federal judge ruled for the families, and on April 14, 1947, a higher court agreed in Mendez v. Westminster. That June, Governor Earl Warren signed a law ending school segregation in California. Seven years later, Earl Warren was Chief Justice of the United States. On May 17, 1954, in Brown v. Board of Education, the Supreme Court ruled unanimously, 9 to 0, that separating children by race in public schools was against the Constitution. Lawyer Thurgood Marshall argued the case for the National Association for the Advancement of Colored People (NAACP). Many Southern leaders fought the ruling, so children had to be brave to integrate their schools.

👁 Watch Me Think

I notice a link that is easy to miss. The same man, Earl Warren, signed California's law in 1947 and wrote the Court's opinion in Brown in 1954. I also notice who started these cases: parents and children, not famous leaders. Then I ask what happened after the ruling. A court decision is only words on paper until people act on it. In September 1957, nine Black teenagers, the Little Rock Nine, tried to start classes at Central High School in Little Rock, Arkansas. Governor Orval Faubus sent the Arkansas National Guard to keep them out. President Dwight D. Eisenhower then sent soldiers of the Army's 101st Airborne Division, and on September 25, 1957, the nine students walked in under their protection. In May 1958, Ernest Green became the school's first Black graduate.

🚌 Boycotts on the Buses: Montgomery and Tallahassee

In Montgomery, Alabama, city law made Black riders sit in the back of the bus and give up their seats to white riders. On March 2, 1955, Claudette Colvin, a 15-year-old high school student, refused to give up her seat and was arrested. On December 1, 1955, Rosa Parks, a 42-year-old seamstress and a leader in the local NAACP, refused too and was arrested. That night, Jo Ann Robinson and the Women's Political Council made thousands of flyers calling for a one-day boycott. On December 5, Black riders stayed off the buses, and leaders chose a 26-year-old minister, Dr. Martin Luther King Jr., to lead the new Montgomery Improvement Association. The boycott lasted 381 days. People walked, shared rides in about 300 carpool cars and kept going even when they were threatened. In November 1956, the Supreme Court agreed that bus segregation was against the Constitution, and on December 21, 1956, Black riders could take any open seat.

👁 Watch Me Think

I notice that Rosa Parks was not the first. Claudette Colvin was arrested nine months earlier, and she later became one of four plaintiffs, the people who bring a case to court, in Browder v. Gayle, the lawsuit that ended bus segregation in Montgomery. So the boycott put pressure on the bus company's money, and the lawsuit changed the law. Both mattered. The idea spread. On May 26, 1956, two Florida A&M University students, Wilhelmina Jakes and Carrie Patterson, were arrested in Tallahassee, Florida, after they would not move to the back of a bus. Students organized a boycott, and the Reverend C. K. Steele formed the Inter-Civic Council to lead it. People shared cars, and the bus company lost so much money that it stopped service for a month. By the summer of 1957, Tallahassee's buses were integrated. The leaders practiced nonviolence: they would not hit back, even when attacked.

🤝 Let's Try Together: Florida's Harry T. and Harriette Moore

Raquel, a fourth grader in Brevard County, Florida, is writing a report about Harry T. Moore, who once taught school in her county. Let's help her. Moore started the Brevard County branch of the NAACP in 1934. In 1946, he and his wife, Harriette, also a teacher, were fired from their teaching jobs after years of civil rights work. After a 1944 Supreme Court ruling said Black voters could not be shut out of primary elections, Moore organized the Progressive Voters' League. Over the next six years, more than 116,000 Black Floridians registered to vote. Now a hard truth, told carefully: on Christmas night in 1951, people who hated the Moores' work set off a bomb under their home in Mims. Harry died on the way to the hospital, and Harriette died nine days later. The crime was never solved. Let's weigh it: the Moores were working for equal rights years before Montgomery. Raquel asks: why is it important to remember people like the Moores?

✊ Marching and Voting: 1963 to 1965

On August 28, 1963, about 250,000 people gathered at the Lincoln Memorial in Washington, D.C., for the March on Washington for Jobs and Freedom. A. Philip Randolph had the idea, and Bayard Rustin planned the day. Dr. King gave his famous "I Have a Dream" speech, picturing a future when his children would be judged by their character instead of their skin color. The next year, President Lyndon B. Johnson signed the Civil Rights Act of 1964 on July 2, 1964. It outlawed segregation in places such as hotels, restaurants and theaters, and it banned job discrimination because of race, color, religion, sex or national origin. But voting was still blocked in much of the South. Officials used literacy tests and threats to stop Black citizens from registering. On August 6, 1965, President Johnson signed the Voting Rights Act of 1965. It stopped literacy tests in many places and sent federal officials to help register voters.

👁 Watch Me Think

I notice that new laws came after people risked a great deal. On March 7, 1965, about 600 marchers, led by John Lewis, Hosea Williams and others, set out from Selma, Alabama, to walk to the state capital, Montgomery, to demand the right to vote. On the Edmund Pettus Bridge, state troopers and local officers attacked them with clubs and tear gas, and at least 50 marchers needed hospital care. People called that day Bloody Sunday, and Americans saw it on television. Two weeks later, on March 21, marchers set out again, this time protected. They walked 54 miles and reached Montgomery on March 25, about 25,000 strong. Less than five months later, the Voting Rights Act became law. I ask: how did ordinary people's courage push Congress to act?

🤝 Let's Try Together: Fannie Lou Hamer and the Right to Vote

Rania, a fifth grader, is making a voting rights timeline for her family's history night. Let's add Fannie Lou Hamer. She worked for 18 years as a sharecropper and timekeeper on a Mississippi plantation. On August 31, 1962, she and 17 other people rode a bus 26 miles to the courthouse in Indianola to register to vote. Only two of them were allowed to take the literacy test that day. When the plantation owner found out, he told her to take back her registration or leave, and she left that same night. She kept working to register voters, and in 1964 she told a committee at a national political convention in Atlantic City, New Jersey, what Black citizens in Mississippi faced when they tried to vote. Let's do the math with Rania: from 1962, when Hamer tried to register, to 1965, when the Voting Rights Act passed, is 3 years. Rania asks: what does it tell us about those times that a person could lose her home for trying to vote?

🔍 Source Detective: A Court, a President and a Law

Here are three short primary sources: a Supreme Court opinion from 1954, a president's speech to the nation from 1957 and a law passed by Congress in 1964. Read each part, then compare them.

Primary Sources: 1954, 1957 and 1964

Part 1 (Chief Justice Earl Warren, Brown v. Board of Education, May 17, 1954): "We conclude that, in the field of public education, the doctrine of 'separate but equal' has no place. Separate educational facilities are inherently unequal."

Part 2 (President Dwight D. Eisenhower, speech to the nation about Little Rock, September 24, 1957): "Mob rule cannot be allowed to override the decisions of our courts."

Part 3 (Civil Rights Act of 1964, Title II): "All persons shall be entitled to the full and equal enjoyment of the goods, services, facilities, privileges, advantages, and accommodations of any place of public accommodation, as defined in this section, without discrimination or segregation on the ground of race, color, religion, or national origin."

Word help: doctrine = a rule or idea a court follows; inherently = by its very nature; facilities = buildings and places; mob rule = an angry crowd taking control instead of the law; override = to cancel or overpower; entitled = having a right to; public accommodation = a place that serves the public, like a hotel, restaurant or theater; on the ground of = because of.

Part 1: Brown v. Board of Education of Topeka, Opinion of the Court, May 17, 1954 (National Archives). Part 2: President Eisenhower's radio and television address on the situation in Little Rock, September 24, 1957 (Eisenhower Presidential Library; History Matters, George Mason University). Part 3: Civil Rights Act of 1964, Public Law 88-352, Title II, Section 201(a) (National Archives; United States Code, Title 42, Section 2000a). Real wording; short parts.

👁 Think Like a Historian

First I check who wrote each source and what power they had. Part 1 comes from the Supreme Court, which decides what the Constitution means. Part 2 comes from the president, who must make sure laws and court rulings are carried out. Part 3 comes from Congress, which writes laws. Then I compare: in 1954 the Court said school segregation was wrong, in 1957 the president used soldiers to enforce that ruling, and in 1964 Congress outlawed segregation in hotels, restaurants and theaters too. All three branches of government acted, but each one acted after ordinary people pushed. How do we know? Look at the dates: Mendez came before Brown, the Little Rock Nine came before Eisenhower's speech, and the bus boycotts and the March on Washington came before the 1964 law.

✊ People Who Changed the Rules

Some were children, some were teachers and some were students. Read each card and ask: what risk did this person take, and what changed because of it?

Sylvia Mendez

Born 1936

At age 8, she was turned away from the 17th Street School in Westminster, California. Her family's case, Mendez v. Westminster, helped end school segregation in California in 1947. She later became a nurse and has spent years telling her story to students. In 2011 she received the Presidential Medal of Freedom.

Claudette Colvin

1939–2026

At 15, on March 2, 1955, she refused to give up her seat on a Montgomery bus and was arrested. She became one of the four plaintiffs in Browder v. Gayle, the case that ended bus segregation in Montgomery. For many years her story was rarely told. She died in January 2026 at age 86.

Harry T. Moore

1905–1951

Born in Houston, Florida, in Suwannee County, he was a teacher who started the Brevard County NAACP in 1934 and led the Florida State Conference of the NAACP. He helped more than 116,000 Black Floridians register to vote. He and his wife, Harriette, were killed in 1951 by people who opposed their work.

Ruby Bridges

Born 1954

Born in Tylertown, Mississippi, she was 6 when four federal marshals walked her into William Frantz Elementary School in New Orleans on November 14, 1960. She was the only student in her class that year. In 1999 she started the Ruby Bridges Foundation.

John Lewis

1940–2020

A young leader from Alabama, he helped lead the Selma march on Bloody Sunday, March 7, 1965, when he was 25, and he was hurt on the Edmund Pettus Bridge. He later served Georgia in the U.S. House of Representatives for more than 30 years.
