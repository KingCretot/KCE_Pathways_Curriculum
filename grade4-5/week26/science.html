🌍 Gravity Pulls Toward the Center

Gravity is a pull between all objects that have mass. NASA explains that anything with mass has gravity, objects with more mass have more gravity, and gravity gets weaker with distance. Earth has an enormous amount of mass, so its pull is strong enough to hold you on the ground, keep the oceans in their basins and hold down the atmosphere, the air you need to breathe. Earth's gravity pulls every object toward Earth's center. That means "down" is not one single direction for the whole planet. In Cleveland, Ohio, down points toward the center of Earth, and in Sydney, Australia, down points toward the center too, so nobody falls off the "bottom" of the world. Gravity pulls both ways: you pull on Earth just as hard as Earth pulls on you, but Earth has so much more mass that your pull does not noticeably move it. The Moon's gravity reaches all the way to Earth. It tugs on the oceans and helps cause tides, the slow rise and fall of the sea. The National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration (NOAA) explains that many coastal places get two high tides and two low tides every 24 hours and 50 minutes. The Sun is about 27 million times more massive than the Moon, but it is about 390 times farther away, so its tide-making pull is only about half as strong as the Moon's. Distance matters! When the Sun, Moon and Earth line up at new moon and full moon, their pulls work together and make extra-large tides called spring tides.

👁 Watch Me Think

Padma lives in Cleveland, Ohio. She video-calls her cousin Pablo in Sydney, Australia, and jokes, "You must be hanging upside down!" Here is how I reason about it. Gravity pulls toward Earth's center. If I draw Earth as a circle, put Padma near the top and Pablo near the bottom, and draw each person's "down" arrow, both arrows point to the middle of the circle. Each cousin is pulled toward the ground under his or her own feet. Neither one is upside down, because "down" means toward the center wherever you stand. I also check mass: Earth has far more mass than either cousin, so Earth's pull on each of them is far stronger than the pull between the two cousins.

⚖️ Mass Versus Weight

Mass is the amount of matter in an object, measured in grams and kilograms. Your mass is the same everywhere: on Earth, on the Moon or floating in space. Weight is different. Weight is the pull of gravity on an object's mass, so it changes when gravity changes. A NASA guide puts it simply: the mass of an object does not depend on the location, but the weight does. Scientists measure weight, a force, in newtons. On Earth's surface, gravity pulls on each kilogram with about 9.8 newtons, so you find an object's weight by multiplying its mass in kilograms by 9.8. A pan balance compares masses, so it gives the same answer on the Moon as on Earth. A spring scale measures the pull of gravity, so it would read less on the Moon. NASA lists the Moon's surface gravity at about 0.166 of Earth's, or about one-sixth, and Mars at about 0.38. A student who weighs 100 pounds on Earth would weigh only about 17 pounds on the Moon, but would have exactly the same mass. Even on Earth, weight changes a tiny bit. Earth bulges at the equator and spins fastest there, so a person who weighs 200 pounds at the North Pole would weigh one or two pounds less at the equator. What about astronauts? The International Space Station orbits between 200 and 250 miles up, where NASA says Earth's gravity is still about 90 percent as strong as on the ground. Astronauts float because they and the station are in free fall, falling around Earth together. NASA calls this floating condition microgravity.

👁 Watch Me Think

Pablo says, "Astronauts float because there is no gravity in space." Let me check that with numbers. NASA says the space station orbits 200 to 250 miles up, and gravity there is about 90 percent of what it is on the ground. NASA even gives an example: a person who weighs 100 pounds on the ground would weigh about 90 pounds at the top of a ladder that tall. So gravity is definitely there! The real reason astronauts float is free fall. The station and everyone inside are falling toward Earth, but they are also moving forward so fast that they keep falling around Earth instead of hitting it. Everything falls together, so nothing presses down on the floor.

🤝 Let's Try Together: Weigh It on Mars

Let's weigh Padma's backpack on two worlds. Its mass is 5 kilograms. On Earth, gravity pulls each kilogram with about 9.8 newtons, so 5 times 9.8 is 49 newtons. On Mars, gravity pulls each kilogram with only about 3.7 newtons, so 5 times 3.7 is 18.5 newtons. And its mass on Mars? Still 5 kilograms! Try It at Home: hang a small zip-top bag of rice from a rubber band taped to the edge of a table, and use a ruler to measure how far the band stretches. Then add a second bag with the same amount of rice. The stretch grows because gravity pulls harder on more mass. That is how a spring scale works.

🪂 Falling Faster and Faster

Drop a ball and it does not fall at one steady speed. It speeds up the whole way down. When something changes speed, scientists call it acceleration. NASA explains that on Earth's surface, gravity's acceleration is 9.8 meters per second squared. In plain words: with no air to slow it, a falling object gains about 9.8 meters per second of speed for every second it falls. After 1 second it moves about 9.8 meters per second, after 2 seconds about 19.6, and after 3 seconds about 29.4. Because it keeps speeding up, it covers more distance each second than the second before: about 4.9 meters in the first second, 19.6 meters in total after 2 seconds and 44.1 meters in total after 3 seconds. NASA also explains that in a vacuum, a space with no air, all objects fall with the same acceleration, whatever their size, shape or weight. In air, wide or fast objects get pushed back by the air, which is why parachutes work. Engineers at NASA's Glenn Research Center in Cleveland, Ohio, use this physics. In the Zero Gravity Research Facility, working since 1966, experiments fall 432 feet (132 meters) inside a giant steel vacuum chamber. The fall lasts 5.18 seconds, and during that time the experiment is in microgravity, just like on the space station. At the bottom, the experiment is moving about 113 miles per hour, and it is stopped in a cart filled with tiny foam plastic beads. A shorter tower at Glenn gives 2.2 seconds of free fall in a 79-foot drop.

👁 Watch Me Think

Pablo watches a video of a ball dropped from a tall tower and wonders why it seems to zoom faster near the end. Here is how I reason with numbers. Gravity adds about 9.8 meters per second of speed every second. So the ball is not falling at one speed; its speed keeps climbing: 9.8, then 19.6, then 29.4 meters per second. Faster motion means more distance each second. The first second covers about 4.9 meters, and by the end of 2 seconds the total is 19.6 meters: four times as far in twice the time! A real ball also meets air pushing back, so it falls a little slower than these perfect numbers, especially if it is light and wide.

🤝 Let's Try Together: Find the Pattern

Look at the speed column: 9.8, 19.6, 29.4. Each second adds 9.8, so the speed grows by the same amount every second. Now look at the distance column: 4.9, 19.6, 44.1. Divide each one by 4.9 and you get 1, 4 and 9. Those are 1 times 1, 2 times 2 and 3 times 3! So after 4 seconds, the total should be 4.9 times 16, which is 78.4 meters. 👁 Think Like a Planetary Scientist

A planetary scientist spots two surprises in this table. Mercury and Mars have almost the same gravity, 3.7, even though Mars has about twice Mercury's mass. And Saturn has about 95 times Earth's mass, yet its gravity at the cloud level is a little weaker than Earth's. Mass is not the whole story! Distance matters too. Mars and Saturn are much wider, so their surfaces or cloud levels are farther from their centers, and gravity weakens with distance.

🧑‍🔬 Scientists Who Measured the Pull

People from many countries and backgrounds helped us understand, measure and use gravity.

Émilie du Châtelet

1706–1749

Émilie du Châtelet was born in Paris, France. She repeated and shared an experiment first designed by the Dutch scientist Willem 's Gravesande, in which lead balls were dropped into soft clay. Balls hitting the clay at twice the speed sank in four times as deep, evidence about the energy of moving objects. Her greatest work was translating Isaac Newton's famous book about motion and gravity, the Principia, into French. She added her own notes, examples and explanations to make Newton's hard writing clearer. She finished it just before she died in 1749, and the complete edition was published ten years later. It is still the only complete translation of the Principia into French.

Henry Cavendish

1731–1810

Henry Cavendish was an English scientist who was extremely shy. He used a torsion balance, a rod with small lead balls on its ends hanging from a thin wire. The geologist John Michell designed it but died before he could use it, so the equipment passed to Cavendish, who rebuilt it. Big lead balls placed nearby pulled on the small balls and twisted the wire a tiny bit. That twist showed gravity pulling between ordinary objects! He stayed outside the closed room and watched through holes in the wall with telescopes, so air currents and temperature changes would not disturb the balls. In June 1798 he reported that Earth's average density is 5.48 times the density of water. People often describe his work as "weighing the world."

Gladys West

1930–2026

Gladys West was an African American mathematician born in rural Dinwiddie County, Virginia. After earning a mathematics degree from Virginia State College, she began work in 1956 at the Naval Proving Ground in Dahlgren, Virginia, and stayed more than 40 years. She programmed early computers, including an IBM 7030 "Stretch," to process satellite data about Earth's exact shape, using math that accounts for gravity, tides and other forces that distort the planet's shape. She also managed a project for Seasat, one of the first satellites that could study the oceans from space. Her work helped make the Global Positioning System (GPS) accurate. She entered the Air Force Space and Missile Pioneers Hall of Fame in 2018 and died in January 2026 at age 95.

Nergis Mavalvala

LIGO physicist

Nergis Mavalvala was born in Lahore, Pakistan, and grew up in Karachi. She studied physics and astronomy at Wellesley College in Massachusetts, then earned her doctorate at the Massachusetts Institute of Technology (MIT), where she helped build an early test version of a gravitational-wave detector. Gravitational waves are tiny ripples in space made by huge events far away. She joined the Laser Interferometer Gravitational-Wave Observatory (LIGO), with twin detectors in Livingston, Louisiana, and Hanford, Washington. On September 14, 2015, both detectors caught waves from two black holes merging about 1.3 billion years ago, confirming a prediction of Albert Einstein's 1915 theory of gravity. She won a MacArthur Fellowship in 2010 and became dean of MIT's School of Science in 2020.
