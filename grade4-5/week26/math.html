🚗 Why Robot Teams Need Expressions

Nonfiction Connection
📖

Real Talk: Robots in the Motor City

Detroit is nicknamed the “Motor City” because it grew into a world center for building cars in the early 1900s. Today the city is also a home for young engineers. In 2018 and 2019, Detroit hosted the FIRST Championship, a giant student robotics competition, at its convention center on the Detroit River, which was then called Cobo Center and is now called Huntington Place. FIRST stands for “For Inspiration and Recognition of Science and Technology.” At a robot competition, judges add up points for every task a robot finishes and take away points for penalties. A judge’s score sheet is really a numerical expression: numbers joined by operation symbols like +, −, ×, and ÷.

🖼️ Picture It: The Order of Operations

👁

One Expression, One Value

A numerical expression is a group of numbers and operation symbols that names one value, such as 10 + 4 × 5. To evaluate an expression means to find its value. Everyone must get the same value, so mathematicians agree on the order of operations: the rules that tell which step comes first.

10 + 4 × 5 = 10 + 20 = 30
Kavya’s robot parks for 10 points and places 4 cones worth 5 points each. The score is 10 + 4 × 5. Multiply first: 4 × 5 = 20. Then add: 10 + 20 = 30 points. Working straight left to right would give 14 × 5 = 70, which is wrong.
Multiply and divide are partners: do them in order from left to right. 24 ÷ 4 × 2 = 6 × 2 = 12, not 24 ÷ 8 = 3.
Add and subtract are partners, too: 20 − 6 + 4 = 14 + 4 = 18, not 20 − 10 = 10.

📝 Writing Expressions from Words

🧰

Coach Kasimir’s Plans

Coach Kasimir never says “20.” He says things like “add 3 and 7, then multiply by 2.” Parentheses tell the reader to do that step first, so the expression is 2 × (3 + 7). Kavya’s job is to turn his words into symbols.

add 3 and 7, then multiply by 22 × (3 + 7)
subtract 5 from 40, then divide by 7(40 − 5) ÷ 7
multiply 6 by 4, then add 96 × 4 + 9
three times as large as 18 + 9323 × (18 + 932)
half of the difference of 90 and 30(90 − 30) ÷ 2
💡

Word Clues

The sum is the answer to an addition, the difference is the answer to a subtraction, the product is the answer to a multiplication, and the quotient is the answer to a division. Watch the order in “subtract 5 from 40”: you start with 40, so write 40 − 5. And “three times as large as 18 + 932” does not need to be computed at all. It means the whole sum is multiplied by 3, so the parentheses go around 18 + 932.

🧱 Brackets, Braces, and Moving Parentheses

🔧

Expressions Inside Expressions

Sometimes a group sits inside another group. Brackets [ ] and braces { } are grouping symbols that work like bigger parentheses. Work from the inside out: parentheses first, then brackets, then braces.

2 × [5 + (9 − 3)]
Inside the parentheses: 9 − 3 = 6, so the expression becomes 2 × [5 + 6].
Inside the brackets: 5 + 6 = 11, so it becomes 2 × 11.
Last step: 2 × 11 = 22.
Same numbers, different values
No parentheses: 6 + 4 × 5 = 6 + 20 = 26.
With parentheses: (6 + 4) × 5 = 10 × 5 = 50.
Puzzle: where do parentheses go so that 24 − 4 × 3 = 60? Try (24 − 4) × 3 = 20 × 3 = 60. It works! Moving the parentheses changes which step comes first, and that changes the value.

⚖️ Comparing Without Computing

🏆

Ms. Kalinda’s Shortcut

Ms. Kalinda doesn’t always compute. She reads the structure of an expression. In 4 × (387 + 29), the parentheses hold one group, and that whole group is multiplied by 4. So 4 × (387 + 29) is four times as large as 387 + 29, and nobody has to add!

5 × (63 + 19)5 times as large as 63 + 19
(906 − 14) ÷ 2half as large as 906 − 14
6 × (450 + 25) vs. 3 × (450 + 25)the first is twice as large
10 × (78 − 6) vs. 10 × 78 − 6not the same: only the first multiplies the whole difference
💡

Look for the Same Group

When two expressions share the same group in parentheses, compare only the numbers that multiply or divide the group. If the parentheses are gone, the group is broken apart, and the comparison changes.
