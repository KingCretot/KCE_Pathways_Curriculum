🧊 What Was the Cold War?

During World War II, the United States and the Soviet Union fought on the same side. After World War II, the United States and the Soviet Union were the world's two superpowers, and they became rivals. The United States was a democracy, where voters choose leaders from more than one party. The Soviet Union was ruled by the Communist Party, which allowed no other parties and controlled businesses; this system is called communism. Each side feared the other's system would spread. After the war, Germany was divided into zones, and so was its capital, Berlin, which sat deep inside the Soviet-controlled east. On June 24, 1948, Soviet forces began a blockade of the roads, railroads and rivers into West Berlin. The United States and the United Kingdom answered with the Berlin Airlift, flying food and fuel into the city until the Soviets lifted the blockade on May 12, 1949. Historians call this rivalry the Cold War because the two superpowers never fought each other directly in a full war.

👁 Watch Me Think

I notice the word cold. A cold war is not peaceful, but the two superpowers did not fight each other directly. Instead they built nuclear weapons, competed for allies around the world, and raced to be first in science and space. The scariest moment came in October 1962, the Cuban Missile Crisis. The Soviet Union had placed nuclear missiles in Cuba, an island nation close to Florida. On October 22, President John F. Kennedy ordered a naval quarantine, a ring of ships around Cuba. Leaders on both sides sent letters and messages, some of them secret. On October 28, Soviet leader Nikita Khrushchev announced that the missiles would be taken apart and removed. So I ask: whose story is this? It is not only the story of two governments. Families in Berlin, in Cuba, in Florida and in many other countries lived through the Cold War too.

🚀 Sputnik and the Space Race

On October 4, 1957, the Soviet Union launched Sputnik 1, the world's first artificial satellite. It was about the size of a beach ball, weighed about 184 pounds, and circled Earth about every 98 minutes. Many Americans were alarmed: if the Soviets could launch a satellite, what else could their rockets do? The United States launched its first satellite, Explorer 1, on January 31, 1958. On July 29, 1958, President Dwight D. Eisenhower signed the law that created NASA, the National Aeronautics and Space Administration, which began work on October 1, 1958. On April 12, 1961, Soviet pilot Yuri Gagarin became the first human to orbit Earth. On May 5, 1961, Alan Shepard became the first American in space, on a 15-minute flight. Three weeks later, President Kennedy asked Congress to send Americans to the Moon before 1970. On February 20, 1962, John Glenn became the first American to orbit Earth, circling it three times. On July 20, 1969, Apollo 11 landed on the Moon.

👁 Watch Me Think

I notice that the astronauts got the headlines, but thousands of people did the work. At NASA's Langley Research Center in Hampton, Virginia, Black women mathematicians worked in a segregated office called West Area Computing. Before John Glenn's flight, computers had calculated his path around Earth, but the astronauts did not fully trust the new machines. Glenn asked engineers to have Katherine Johnson check the numbers by hand. NASA says Johnson remembered him saying, "If she says they're good, then I'm ready to go." Mary Jackson, who began in the same office under supervisor Dorothy Vaughan, became NASA's first Black female engineer in 1958. Then I check how the race ended. In July 1975, an American Apollo spacecraft docked with a Soviet Soyuz spacecraft in orbit, and the crews opened the hatches and shook hands. Rivals became partners.

🧱 The Berlin Wall and the End of the Cold War

After World War II, Germany split into two countries: democratic West Germany and communist East Germany. Berlin was split too, and West Berlin sat like an island inside East Germany. Before 1961, nearly 3 million people left East Germany, most of them through West Berlin. On August 13, 1961, East Germany began building the Berlin Wall to stop them. The barrier grew to about 155 kilometers (about 96 miles) around West Berlin, and for 28 years it split families and friends apart. The Berlin Wall Foundation reports that at least 140 people were killed at the Wall or lost their lives because of the border system. In March 1985, Mikhail Gorbachev became the leader of the Soviet Union and began a policy called glasnost, which means openness. In 1989, people in Leipzig, East Berlin and other East German cities held peaceful demonstrations. On the evening of November 9, 1989, the Berlin Wall opened, and crowds crossed and celebrated. On October 3, 1990, East and West Germany joined together again in reunification. On December 25, 1991, Gorbachev resigned, and the Soviet Union came to an end.

👁 Watch Me Think

I notice that the Wall did not fall because of one speech or one leader. Leaders mattered: President Ronald Reagan challenged Gorbachev at the Wall in 1987, and Gorbachev's policy of openness changed the Soviet Union. But ordinary people mattered too. In 1989, demonstrators in Leipzig, East Berlin and other East German cities marched peacefully. On the night of November 9, crowds grew so large at the Bornholmer Strasse checkpoint that the officer in charge, without official orders, finally raised the barrier and let them through. So I ask: how do we know? Historians use speeches, photographs, newspapers and the memories of people who were there. The Cold War ended the way it began: without the two superpowers fighting each other directly.

🔍 Source Detective: Words from 1958, 1961 and 1987

Here are three short primary sources: a law, a speech to Congress and a speech in Berlin. All three are records of the United States government. Read each part slowly.

Primary Sources: 1958, 1961 and 1987

Part 1 (National Aeronautics and Space Act of 1958, signed July 29, 1958): "The Congress hereby declares that it is the policy of the United States that activities in space should be devoted to peaceful purposes for the benefit of all mankind."

Part 2 (President John F. Kennedy, speech to Congress, May 25, 1961): "First, I believe that this nation should commit itself to achieving the goal, before this decade is out, of landing a man on the moon and returning him safely to the earth."

Part 3 (President Ronald Reagan, speech at the Brandenburg Gate, West Berlin, June 12, 1987): "Mr. Gorbachev, open this gate! Mr. Gorbachev, tear down this wall!"

Word help: hereby declares = officially announces; policy = an official plan; devoted to = used for; mankind = all people; commit itself = promise to work hard; before this decade is out = before the 1960s end; Mr. Gorbachev = Mikhail Gorbachev, the leader of the Soviet Union.

Part 1: National Aeronautics and Space Act of 1958, Public Law 85-568, Section 102(a) (NASA History Office; National Archives). Part 2: Special Message to the Congress on Urgent National Needs, May 25, 1961 (John F. Kennedy Presidential Library; NASA). Part 3: Remarks on East-West Relations at the Brandenburg Gate in West Berlin, June 12, 1987 (Ronald Reagan Presidential Library). Real wording; short parts.

👁 Think Like a Historian

First I check the date and the speaker of each part. Part 1 is a law, so it states an official policy. Parts 2 and 3 are speeches, so they try to persuade. Next I compare. Part 1 says space should be used for peaceful purposes, for all people. Part 2 sets a deadline: the Moon before 1970. Apollo 11 landed in July 1969, so the goal was met. Part 3 is a challenge to the leader of the rival superpower; two and a half years later, the Wall opened. Then I ask: does a speech prove what caused an event? No. A speech shows what a leader wanted. To learn what caused an event, historians need more evidence.

🌟 People of the Space Race

They flew, calculated and built on both sides of the rivalry. Read each card and ask: what barrier did this person face, and what door did they open?

Katherine Johnson

1918–2020

Born in White Sulphur Springs, West Virginia, she was one of three Black students chosen to integrate West Virginia's graduate schools. At Langley from 1953, she calculated flight paths, and in 1962 John Glenn asked her to check his orbit by hand.

Mary Jackson

1921–2005

Hired in 1951 into the segregated West Area Computing office, she needed special permission to take engineering classes at a then-segregated high school. In 1958 she became NASA's first Black female engineer, and later she worked to help women at NASA get hired and promoted.

Yuri Gagarin

1934–1968

A young Soviet air force pilot, he was chosen with 20 others for cosmonaut training in 1960. On April 12, 1961, he became the first human to orbit Earth, in a flight of 108 minutes, and he became a hero around the world.

Sally Ride

1951–2012

Born in Los Angeles, California, she earned a doctorate in physics. On June 18, 1983, aboard the space shuttle Challenger, she became the first American woman in space. Later she became a physics professor and worked to inspire young scientists.

Guion "Guy" Bluford

First flight: 1983

A Philadelphia native, he earned a degree in aerospace engineering at Penn State in 1964 and became an Air Force pilot. On August 30, 1983, aboard Challenger, he became the first African American in space, on the shuttle program's first night launch.
