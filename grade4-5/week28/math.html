🏔️ Why Runners Count Decimals

Nonfiction Connection
📖

Real Talk: Running in Boulder

Boulder, Colorado, sits at an altitude of 5,430 feet (1,655 meters), with the rocky Flatirons rising just west of town. The air up high has less oxygen, so visitors from sea level often run more slowly for the first few days. Elite runners from around the world come to Boulder to train, and the city hosts the BOLDERBoulder, one of the largest 10-kilometer road races in the United States. Cross-country races are run on grass and trails, and their distances are measured in kilometers. Race times, distances, and paces almost never come out to whole numbers, so runners and coaches multiply and divide decimals all the time.

🖼️ Picture It: Tenths Times Tenths

👁

A Part of a Part

Coach Larkin’s warm-up loop is 0.4 kilometer, and Lavinia jogs 0.3 of it. To find 0.3 × 0.4, use a big square that stands for 1 whole, cut into 100 small squares. Each small square is one hundredth. A tenth of a tenth is a hundredth, so 0.3 × 0.4 = 0.12.

0.3 × 0.4 = 0.12
Shade the factors: 3 of the 10 columns show 0.3, and 4 of the 10 rows show 0.4.
Count the overlap: 3 × 4 = 12 small squares are shaded both ways. Each one is a hundredth, so the product is 12 hundredths, or 0.12.
Fraction check: 3/10 × 4/10 = 12/100 = 0.12.
Watch out: 0.3 × 0.4 is NOT 1.2. Taking part of a part gives a smaller amount, and 0.12 is smaller than both 0.3 and 0.4.

✖️ Multiplying Larger Decimals

🏃

Coach Larkin’s Hill Workout

Coach Larkin’s flat path is 2.4 kilometers long, and his hill path is 1.5 times as long. To find 2.4 × 1.5, Lavinia splits each factor into ones and tenths. Each small rectangle in the area model holds a partial product, and the partial products add up to the whole product.

1.2 × 0.30.36
2.5 × 1.43.5
4.5 × 0.62.7
3.2 × 2.58
2.4 × 1.5 = 3.6
Estimate first: 2.4 is about 2 and 1.5 is about 2, so the product is about 4. An estimate tells you about how big the answer should be, so you know where the decimal point belongs.
Multiply as whole numbers: 24 × 15 = 360.
Place the decimal point: Count the decimal places in both factors: 2.4 has one and 1.5 has one, so the product has two. 360 becomes 3.60, which is 3.6.
Why it works: 2.4 is 24 tenths and 1.5 is 15 tenths. Tenths times tenths make hundredths, so 24 × 15 = 360 hundredths = 3.60.

➗ Dividing by a Decimal

⏱️

Ms. Leighton Marks the Course

Ms. Leighton has a 2.8-kilometer path and wants a water stop every 0.4 kilometer. How many 0.4-kilometer sections fit? That is 2.8 ÷ 0.4. Dividing by a decimal asks the same question as any division: how many groups of this size fit?

1.8 ÷ 0.3 = 18 ÷ 36
0.36 ÷ 0.04 = 36 ÷ 49
7.5 ÷ 2.5 = 75 ÷ 253
6.3 ÷ 1.4 = 63 ÷ 144.5
2.8 ÷ 0.4 = 28 ÷ 4 = 7
Use last week’s trick: Multiply the dividend and the divisor by the same power of 10 so the divisor becomes a whole number. Here, multiply both by 10: 2.8 becomes 28 and 0.4 becomes 4.
Why it is fair: The quotient does not change, because both numbers grew by the same factor. It is like 6 ÷ 2 = 3 and 60 ÷ 20 = 3.
Hundredths: For 0.36 ÷ 0.04, multiply both by 102, or 100, to get 36 ÷ 4 = 9.
Sense check: 0.4 is less than 1, so more than 2.8 of them fit. A quotient of 7 makes sense.

🔎 Bigger or Smaller? Predict Before You Compute

🏁

Lavinia’s Prediction Rule

Before she computes, Lavinia asks, “Should the answer be bigger or smaller than the number I started with?” Multiplying by a decimal less than 1 takes only part of a number, so the product is smaller. Dividing by a decimal less than 1 asks how many small pieces fit, so the quotient is bigger.

8 × 0.5 (less than 1)4: smaller than 8
8 × 1.5 (more than 1)12: bigger than 8
8 ÷ 0.5 (less than 1)16: bigger than 8
8 ÷ 2 (more than 1)4: smaller than 8
💡

On the Course

Half a lap of a 1.6-kilometer loop is 1.6 × 0.5 = 0.8 kilometer, which is less than one lap. But the number of 0.5-kilometer pieces in that same 1.6-kilometer loop is 1.6 ÷ 0.5 = 3.2, which is more than 1.6. Prediction catches a misplaced decimal point before it costs you.
