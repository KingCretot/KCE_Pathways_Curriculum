🌡️ Temperature, Thermal Energy and Heat

Everything around you is made of tiny particles that are always moving. The faster they move, the hotter something is. Temperature measures how fast, on average, the particles in something are moving. NASA explains that in a hot gas, the molecules move faster than in a cold gas. We measure temperature with a thermometer. On the Fahrenheit scale, pure water freezes at 32 degrees and boils at 212 degrees. On the Celsius scale, which scientists around the world use, pure water freezes at 0 degrees and boils at 100 degrees. Thermal energy is the total energy of all the moving particles in something. A full bathtub and a mug filled from the same faucet have the same temperature, but the bathtub holds much more thermal energy because it has many more moving particles. Heat is thermal energy moving from one thing to another. The U.S. Department of Energy says heat flows from warmer to cooler areas until there is no longer a temperature difference. When two things reach the same temperature and heat stops flowing between them, scientists say they are in thermal equilibrium. Cold is not something that flows. When an ice cube makes your hand feel cold, heat is leaving your hand and flowing into the ice.

👁 Watch Me Think

Pieter, in Anchorage, Alaska, pours a mug of hot cocoa and sets a glass of ice water beside it on the kitchen table. Here is how I predict what happens. The cocoa is warmer than the room, so heat flows out of the cocoa into the air and the table, and the cocoa cools. The ice water is cooler than the room, so heat flows from the room into the glass, and the water warms. Nothing "cold" flows anywhere. Each drink keeps changing until it reaches the room's temperature. Then both drinks are in thermal equilibrium with the room, and the heat flow stops. One rule explains both drinks: heat always flows from warmer to cooler.

🤝 Let's Try Together: Convert a Temperature

Let's turn 20 degrees Celsius into Fahrenheit. Step 1: each Celsius degree is 180 divided by 100, or 1.8 Fahrenheit degrees, so 20 Celsius degrees is 20 times 1.8, which is 36 Fahrenheit degrees above freezing. Step 2: freezing is 32 degrees Fahrenheit, so add 32. 36 plus 32 is 68 degrees Fahrenheit, a comfortable room. Try It at Home, with a grown-up: fill one clear cup with cold tap water and one with warm tap water (never boiling). Add one drop of food coloring to each at the same moment. The color spreads faster in the warm water, because its particles are moving faster.

🔥 Three Ways Heat Moves

Heat can move in three ways. Conduction is heat moving through matter that is touching, from particle to particle. The National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration (NOAA) gives a classic example: leave a metal spoon in a pot of soup on the stove, and after a short time its handle becomes hot. Conduction works very well in metals, but air conducts heat poorly. Convection is heat carried by a moving fluid, which means a liquid or a gas. Warm fluid rises, and cooler, denser fluid moves in to take its place, making a loop called a convection current. You can see convection in the swirling of a pot of boiling water, and NOAA says convection currents in the air cause many weather patterns. Radiation is energy traveling as waves, and it can even cross empty space. Sunlight travels about 93 million miles and reaches Earth in about 8 minutes and 20 seconds, says the Smithsonian's National Air and Space Museum. NOAA explains that if you stand near a campfire, the side of your body nearest the fire warms while your other side does not. Infrared radiation produces that warm feeling. Dark objects, like asphalt, absorb radiant energy faster than light-colored objects.

👁 Watch Me Think

Pinar is at Daytona Beach, Florida, on a sunny afternoon, and a cool breeze blows from the ocean onto the sand. Here is how I explain it with all three ways heat moves. Radiation: sunlight warms both the land and the water, but a University of Wisconsin weather lesson explains that the land heats much more quickly than the water. Conduction: the hot sand warms the air touching it. Convection: that warm air rises, and air from over the water flows toward the land to take its place. That flow is the sea breeze. At night the land cools faster than the water, so the process reverses and the wind blows from the land out to sea. That is a land breeze.

🤝 Let's Try Together: Sort a Campfire

At a campfire with a grown-up, your face feels warm even though the night air is cool. That is radiation, traveling as infrared waves. Hot air and smoke rise straight up above the flames. That is convection. If someone left a metal grill grate over the fire, its edges would soon be too hot to touch. That is conduction through the metal. Try It at Home, with a grown-up: have a grown-up fill a mug with hot tap water. Hold your hand a few inches to the SIDE of the mug, then a few inches ABOVE it, never touching. Above, you feel rising warm air plus radiation. To the side, you mostly feel radiation, so it feels less warm.

🧤 Conductors, Insulators and Keeping Heat In

Some materials let heat flow through them quickly. They are good thermal conductors. Other materials slow heat down. They are insulators. Scientists compare materials with a number called thermal conductivity: the bigger the number, the faster heat flows through. In a table on HyperPhysics, a physics website from Georgia State University, copper scores 385, aluminum 205 and steel 50.2, while glass scores 0.8, wood 0.04 to 0.12, polystyrene foam 0.033 and air just 0.024. Air is such a poor conductor that most insulation works by trapping it. Cooperative Extension experts explain that fiberglass insulation creates millions of tiny air pockets that slow the movement of heat. Engineers can fight all three ways heat moves at once. In 1892 the Scottish scientist James Dewar created a vacuum flask: one glass flask inside another, with a partial vacuum between them. With almost no matter in the gap, conduction and convection nearly stop, and a shiny silvered coating on the glass cuts the heat lost by radiation. Today's vacuum bottles use the same idea. For houses, the U.S. Department of Energy rates insulation by its R-value, and it says the higher the R-value, the greater the insulating effectiveness. Insulation keeps heat inside in winter, and in summer it slows the heat flowing in from outdoors.

👁 Watch Me Think

Piper waits for the school bus on a January morning in Minneapolis, Minnesota. She touches a metal railing and a wooden bench that have both been outside all night. The railing feels much colder! Here is how I think it through. Both have been out in the same air all night, so they are the same temperature: they are in thermal equilibrium with the air. The difference is how fast they pull heat out of her warm hand. Steel scores 50.2 on the HyperPhysics table, and wood only 0.04 to 0.12, so heat travels through steel hundreds of times more easily. The steel quickly carries heat away from her fingers, while the wood barely does. Her skin senses that fast heat loss as "colder." The railing is not colder; it is a better conductor.

🤝 Let's Try Together: How Many Times Faster?

Let's compare copper and steel for the bottom of a frying pan. Copper: 385. Steel: 50.2, which rounds to 50. 385 divided by 50 is 7.7, so heat flows through copper about 8 times as fast. That is why some pans have copper bottoms: they spread heat quickly to the food. Now compare glass with polystyrene foam: 0.8 and 0.033, which rounds to about 0.03. 0.8 divided by 0.03 is between 26 and 27, so heat flows through glass more than 25 times as fast as through foam. A foam cup keeps cocoa hot much longer than a glass cup with walls just as thick.

👁 Think Like a Scientist

First I check that the test is fair: only the wrap changed, so the wrap is the variable being tested. Then I look for patterns. Every mug cooled toward the 70-degree room, just as heat always flows from warmer to cooler. The bare mug cooled fastest at the start, when it was much warmer than the room, and more slowly later. The lid made a big difference, so a lot of heat must escape out of an open top, carried away by rising warm air and evaporating water. Joseph Fourier would recognize that pattern: a bigger temperature difference means faster heat flow.

🧑‍🔬 People Who Studied and Tamed Heat

Scientists and inventors from different backgrounds measured heat, explained how it flows and built machines to control it.

Eunice Newton Foote

1819–1888

Eunice Newton Foote grew up in Bloomfield, New York. She and her husband signed the 1848 Declaration of Sentiments at the Seneca Falls Convention for women's rights. In 1856 she put thermometers inside glass cylinders filled with different gases and set them in sunlight. On her thermometers, common air reached 106 degrees, but carbon dioxide reached 125. Joseph Henry of the Smithsonian Institution presented her paper that year at a science meeting in Albany, New York. She wrote: "An atmosphere of that gas would give to our earth a high temperature."

Joseph Fourier

1768–1830

Joseph Fourier was born in Auxerre, France, and was orphaned at age nine. In 1798 he traveled with Napoleon's expedition to Egypt. His 1822 book, The Analytical Theory of Heat, showed how heat conduction in solid objects can be described with mathematics, using series that are now named after him. In the 1820s he also suggested that Earth's atmosphere might act like an insulator, which is widely recognized as the first proposal of what we now call the greenhouse effect.

Alice H. Parker

born 1895

Alice H. Parker was born in Morristown, New Jersey, and attended classes at Howard University in Washington, D.C. On December 23, 1919, she received U.S. Patent 1,325,905 for a heating furnace that burned natural gas instead of wood or coal, with several burners that could each be controlled separately. With it, no one would need to chop firewood or keep a fire burning overnight. Her exact design was never built, but the Lemelson-MIT Program calls it a forerunner of today's heating zones and thermostats.

Frederick McKinley Jones

1893–1961

Frederick McKinley Jones was born in Cincinnati, Ohio. While working as a mechanic on a farm in Minnesota, he earned an engineering license. He invented the first successful system for mobile refrigeration and, with Joseph Numero, founded Thermo King Corporation to build refrigerated trucks. During World War II, his portable cooling units helped keep blood, medicine and food cold for soldiers. He earned more than 60 patents, and in 1991 he became the first Black American to receive the National Medal of Technology, awarded after his death.
