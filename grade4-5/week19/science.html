📚 Reading the Layers: The Law of Superposition

Sand, mud and tiny shells settle in layers at the bottom of seas, lakes and rivers, and wind piles sand into dunes. Over a very long time, new layers press down on older ones, and the sediment hardens into sedimentary rock. In 1669, a Danish scientist named Nicolaus Steno wrote down two big ideas. First, layers of sediment form flat, or nearly flat. Second, in layers that have not been disturbed, the oldest layer is on the bottom and the youngest layer is on top. This idea is called the law of superposition. It gives relative age: it tells which layer is older or younger, not exactly how many years old. Layers can be disturbed, though. Moving rock can tilt, fold or even flip layers, so geologists check for clues before they use superposition. Sometimes layers are missing, too, because erosion wore rock away before new layers formed on top. Geologists call a gap like that an unconformity.

👁 Watch Me Think

Imagine the laundry basket in your house. All week, people drop clothes on top. On Saturday, which shirt went in first? The one at the very bottom, because each new shirt landed on top of the ones already there. Rock layers work the same way. But what if someone dumped the basket upside down and then picked it back up? Now the oldest shirt could be on top. That is why I look for clues first. Are the layers flat? Are the ripple marks and footprints right side up? If the layers look undisturbed, I can say the bottom layer is the oldest.

🤝 Let's Try Together: Read a Rock Column

Let's read a rock column together. From the top down, a cliff shows: Layer A, sandstone; Layer B, limestone with seashells; Layer C, shale; Layer D, sandstone with ripple marks. The layers are flat and the ripple marks point up, so they look undisturbed. Using superposition, D is the oldest, then C, then B, and A is the youngest. Try It at Home, with a grown-up's help: put a cup of gravel, a cup of sand and a cup of garden soil in a clear plastic jar, fill it most of the way with water, put the lid on tightly and shake it. Let it sit overnight without moving it. You will see layers settle, with the heaviest pieces usually at the bottom. Do not drink the water, and wash your hands afterward.

🐚 Fossils: Clues to Past Environments

A fossil is the preserved remains or trace of a living thing from long ago. Most fossils are found in sedimentary rock, because sediment can bury a plant or animal gently. The heat and pressure that form other kinds of rock usually destroy remains. Body fossils are parts of the living thing itself, such as bones, teeth, shells or leaves, often turned to stone as minerals slowly fill them. A mold is a hollow print left in rock, and a cast forms when minerals fill a mold. Trace fossils show what an animal did: footprints, tracks, burrows and nests. Fossils are clues to past environments. Fossil seashells and corals mean a sea was once there, even if the land is a desert today. Fossil footprints in old sand dunes mean there was once a desert. In Florida, shark teeth are the most common fossils, and the Peace River near Arcadia is a popular place to look for them. Florida has fossils of both sea and land animals because sea level rose and fell many times in its history.

👁 Watch Me Think

A fossil is a clue, but I have to read it carefully. Suppose I find a fossil fish in rock high on a hill. Did the fish climb the hill? No. A fish needs water, so the rock must have formed under water. Later, the land rose or the water went away. I also ask what the rest of the rock says. Fine mud with fish and shells means a calm sea or lake. Sand with slanted layers and footprints but no shells means wind-blown dunes. I put the fossil and the rock together before I decide what the past environment was.

🤝 Let's Try Together: Sort the Fossils

Let's sort some fossils together. A footprint of a reptile-like animal in sandstone: that is a trace fossil, because it shows what the animal did. A brachiopod shell turned to stone: that is a body fossil, and it tells us the place was a sea. A hollow print of a fern leaf in shale: that is a mold. If minerals later filled that hollow and made a fern-shaped rock, that would be a cast. Now the big question: what does each one tell us about the past environment? Shells and corals point to a sea. Ferns and other plants point to land with water nearby. Footprints in dune sand point to a desert.

🗺️ Index Fossils and Changing Landscapes

How can geologists match layers in two cliffs that are miles apart? One tool is the index fossil. A good index fossil comes from a living thing that spread over a wide area but lived for only a short span of Earth's history, and it is easy to recognize. Certain kinds of trilobites and ammonites are famous index fossils. If the same index fossil appears in a layer in two places, those layers likely formed at about the same time. In the early 1800s, an English surveyor named William Smith noticed that fossils in rock layers always appeared in the same order from bottom to top, even in places far apart. He used that pattern to make the first geologic map of a whole country, published in 1815. Fossils and layers also show that landscapes change over time. The rocks of the Grand Canyon formed in seas, deserts and river plains over hundreds of millions of years. Much later, the land was pushed up, and in the last 5 to 6 million years the Colorado River carved the canyon, about a mile deep. The rocks are far older than the canyon itself.

👁 Watch Me Think

Here is how I match two cliffs. In Cliff 1, a layer holds a trilobite with a special three-part head shape that lived only a short time. In Cliff 2, ten miles away, I find the same kind of trilobite in a layer of a different color. Should I match them by color? No. Rock color can change from place to place, because the same sea might drop mud in one spot and sand in another. But the same short-lived index fossil means those two layers formed at about the same time. So I match the layers by the index fossil, then use superposition to say which layers are older or younger in each cliff.

🤝 Let's Try Together: Pick the Best Index Fossil

Let's test which fossil would make the BEST index fossil. Fossil 1: a kind of shark tooth found in rocks from many different time periods. Fossil 2: a trilobite found all over the world, but only in rocks from one short span of time. Fossil 3: a snail found in just one small valley. Fossil 2 wins. It is widespread, so we can find it in many places, and it lived for only a short time, so it points to one narrow slice of Earth's history. Fossil 1 lived too long, and Fossil 3 is found in too few places. Remember the checklist: widespread, short time, easy to recognize.

A paleontologist studies ancient life. She reads each layer like a page: the rock tells how it formed, and the fossils tell what lived there. Then she tells the story from the bottom up, oldest to youngest, the way it happened: sea, then rivers and dunes, then sea again.

🦴 Scientists Who Read Earth's History

From the 1600s to today, these scientists learned to read rock layers and fossils as clues to Earth's past.

Nicolaus Steno

1638–1686

Nicolaus Steno was born Niels Stensen in Copenhagen, Denmark, and studied medicine. In 1666, while working in Florence, Italy, he studied the head of a huge shark. He saw that its teeth matched the strange "tongue stones" people found in rock, and argued that tongue stones were really fossil shark teeth. In 1669 he described how rock layers form flat, with the oldest on the bottom and the youngest on top unless something disturbs them. That idea, the law of superposition, is still used by geologists today.

William Smith

1769–1839

William Smith grew up in a family of small farmers in England and had little formal schooling, but he taught himself geometry and surveying. While he worked as a surveyor and oversaw the digging of a canal, he studied the rock layers he saw. He noticed that each layer held its own kinds of fossils, always in the same order. Using that pattern, he made the first geologic map of a whole country, published in 1815. Few scientists paid attention at first, but in 1831 he received the first Wollaston Medal, the Geological Society of London's highest honor.

Xu Xing

Chinese paleontologist

Xu Xing is a paleontologist at the Institute of Vertebrate Paleontology and Paleoanthropology in Beijing, China. He has named dozens of new kinds of dinosaurs, about 80 by his own count, more than almost any other living scientist. Some of his most famous finds come from rock layers in Liaoning Province that formed in ancient lakes about 125 million years ago, where volcanic ash buried animals and preserved even their feathers. In 2003 his team described a Microraptor fossil, a small dinosaur with feathered wings on its arms and legs. His work helps show that birds are living relatives of dinosaurs.

Lisa White

micropaleontologist

Lisa White is a micropaleontologist, a scientist who studies fossils so tiny you need a microscope to see them. She studies fossil diatoms, tiny one-celled living things that are preserved in rock and help scientists date rocks and learn about past ocean climates. She taught geology at San Francisco State University, and since 2012 she has led education and outreach at the University of California Museum of Paleontology. She has led programs that took high school students into the field to study real rocks, to welcome more young people of all backgrounds into Earth science.
