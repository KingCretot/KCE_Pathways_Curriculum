🔁 Warm-Up: Week 18 Review

Remember These?

Last week you studied opinion and argument. A claim is the writer’s main opinion. Reasons tell why. Evidence (facts, numbers, examples, quotes) proves each reason. A counterclaim is what the other side believes, which a strong writer answers. You also put a comma after an introductory word like However, and between items in a series. Today’s writers have a point to make too, but in stories and poems they usually show it instead of arguing it. That point is called the theme.

🖼️ Picture It: Topic or Theme?

The topic of a text is what it is about, in a word or two: horses, a river, the weather. The theme is the message about life that the text shows. A theme is written as a full sentence, and it is true outside the story too. Two texts can have completely different topics and still share the same theme. Read this short fable and look for its message.

Model Text • FableThe Wind and the Sun

An Aesop fable, retold from Joseph Jacobs’s 1894 version

The Wind and the Sun were arguing about which of them was stronger. Just then they saw a traveler walking down the road in a heavy cloak. “Here is a test,” said the Sun. “Whichever of us can make that traveler take off his cloak is the stronger one. You go first.”

So the Sun hid behind a cloud, and the Wind blew as hard as it could. But the harder the Wind blew, the more tightly the traveler wrapped his cloak around himself. At last the Wind gave up.

Then the Sun came out and shone warmly on the traveler. Soon he grew so hot that he took off his cloak and rested in the shade.

Moral: Kindness gets more done than force.

Topic (a word or two)Theme (a full sentence about life)
The wind and the sunPatience and gentleness can accomplish more than force.
❌ “Kindness” (only one word, so it is still a topic)✅ “Being kind often works better than being harsh.”
❌ “Be nice.” (a command, not a statement)✅ “People respond better to gentleness than to force.”

How Each Genre Shows a Theme

GenreHow the theme appearsWhere to look
FableUsually stated as a moral at the endThe last line
Realistic storyUsually shown through how a character changes and what the character learnsThe problem, the turning point, and the ending
PoemShown through images and comparisons; sometimes the speaker states it near the endRepeated ideas, contrasts, and the last stanza
💡

Four Theme Clues

Ask: (1) What problem does the main character face? (2) What does the character try first, and does it work? (3) What changes, and what is learned? (4) What would this lesson mean for anyone, not only this character? Turn your answer into one sentence.

📖 Text A: The Realistic Story

A realistic story has characters, a setting, and a problem that could happen in real life. Watch what the main character tries first, what changes, and what she learns. Notice the paragraph numbers; you will use them.

Text A • Realistic StoryJuniper in the Tall Grass

An original story

1On a horse farm outside Ocala, Florida, a gray filly named Juniper had spent three days refusing to be caught. Every morning, Wren Adebayo marched into the pasture with a halter in one hand and a rope in the other, determined to lead the young horse to the barn for her first checkup.

2On the fourth morning, Wren tried running. She sprinted across the wet grass, waving the rope and calling, “Juniper! Come here right now!” The filly flicked her ears, spun on her back hooves, and galloped to the far fence. The faster Wren chased, the farther Juniper ran.

3Wren stomped back to the gate, where her uncle Desmond was leaning on a post with a mug of coffee. “She’s impossible,” Wren said, breathing hard. “She won’t listen.”

4“Oh, she’s listening,” Uncle Desmond said. “She hears a loud girl with a rope running straight at her. To a horse, that looks like a mountain lion. Try being something else.”

5So Wren tried something else. She walked to the middle of the pasture, sat down in the tall grass, and turned her back to the filly. She picked a few clover blossoms and hummed. Ten minutes passed. Twenty. Her legs began to tingle. Out of the corner of her eye, she watched Juniper take one step closer, then two, then stop to graze.

6After almost an hour, Wren felt warm breath on her neck. Juniper lowered her soft gray nose and sniffed the clover in Wren’s hand. Slowly, without standing up, Wren raised the halter, and the filly let it slide over her ears. That afternoon, Juniper walked to the barn beside Wren, the rope hanging loose between them.

🌊 Text B: The Poem

A poem uses lines and stanzas, images, and sound. The speaker is the voice talking in the poem. Watch the two things this poem compares, and read the last stanza slowly.

Text B • PoemWhat the River Knows

An original poem

1The hammer strikes the limestone hard
with one tremendous crack.
It leaves a jagged, broken shard,
then cannot put it back.

2The river never strikes at all.
It hums along its bed,
and grain by grain, and fall by fall,
it smooths the stone instead.

3A thousand years of quiet flow
have carved a bowl of stone
where turtles rest and lilies grow,
the river’s work alone.

4So when you want to move a heart
or change a stubborn mind,
remember how the river’s art
is patient, slow, and kind.

📝 Summarize Like a Pro

🪜

Four Steps to a Strong Summary

  1. Who or what? Name the main character (or, for a poem, the main things the speaker describes).
  2. Problem or big idea: What does the character want, and what gets in the way?
  3. Key events in order: beginning, turning point, ending. For a poem, give the main idea of each stanza.
  4. Cut and check: leave out tiny details, dialogue, and your opinions. Use your own words, and don’t copy 4 or more words in a row.
🧪

Worked Example: Summarizing the Fable

❌ Weak: I loved this fable! The Sun hid behind a cloud and the Wind blew really hard and it was so funny.
It gives an opinion (“I loved,” “so funny”), keeps a small detail (the cloud), and leaves out the test and the ending.

✅ Strong: The Wind and the Sun argue about who is stronger, so they test which one can get a traveler to remove his cloak. The Wind’s hard blowing only makes him hold it tighter, but when the Sun shines warmly, the traveler takes it off himself.
Main characters, the problem, and the key events in order, all in new words and with no opinions.

🎯

Summary or Theme?

A summary tells what happens. A theme tells what it means. “Wren sits quietly until Juniper comes to her” is part of a summary. “Patience can accomplish more than force” is the theme.

📝 Writer’s Turn: Write a Summary

🔄

Your Summary Is Checked by Three Fair Rules

Your summary must (1) have enough words to cover the important events, (2) not copy 4 or more words in a row from the text (names can stay), and (3) include every key idea listed. Leave out tiny details and opinions. Write one or two sentences. If a rule is missed, the feedback tells you which one.

+6

Summarize paragraphs 1 and 2 of the story in one or two sentences, in your own words.

Text A, paragraphs 1–2: On a horse farm outside Ocala, Florida, a gray filly named Juniper had spent three days refusing to be caught. Every morning, Wren Adebayo marched into the pasture… determined to lead the young horse to the barn for her first checkup. On the fourth morning, Wren tried running… The faster Wren chased, the farther Juniper ran.
  • Use at least 15 words.
  • Do not copy 4 or more words in a row from the text. (Names like Wren, Juniper, and Ocala are fine to keep.)
  • Name the main character: include Wren.
  • Name the horse: include Juniper (or the filly or horse).
  • Keep the key event: say that Wren chased or ran after her.
  • Keep the result: say that the filly ran away or would not be caught.
