A large meeting of people with a shared goal. The first women's rights convention met in Seneca Falls, New York, in 1848.

To officially approve. Three-fourths of the states must ratify an amendment before it joins the Constitution.
picket
To stand outside a place holding signs as a protest. Suffragists picketed the White House starting in 1917.
procession
A parade or march, often for a cause. Thousands of women marched in the 1913 suffrage procession.
naturalization
The process an immigrant goes through to become a citizen. For many years, laws barred Asian immigrants from it.
disenfranchise
To take away or block someone's right to vote. Poll taxes and reading tests disenfranchised many Black voters.

📜 From Seneca Falls to the Polls in Rochester

In 1848, women in the United States could not vote, and the law often gave a married woman's property, even her wages, to her husband. On July 19 and 20, 1848, about 300 people met at the Wesleyan Chapel in Seneca Falls, New York, for the first women's rights convention. Elizabeth Cady Stanton and the M'Clintock family wrote a Declaration of Sentiments, and 68 women and 32 men signed it, including Frederick Douglass. On November 5, 1872, Susan B. Anthony and 14 other women voted in Rochester, New York. They argued that the 14th Amendment made them citizens, and citizens could vote. Anthony was arrested, put on trial and fined $100.

👁 Watch Me Think

I notice the convention borrowed the words of the Declaration of Independence on purpose, adding two words: "all men and women are created equal." Borrowing famous words is a strategy. It says: you already believe this, so live up to it. Then I look at Anthony's plan. She used the 14th Amendment from our Reconstruction lesson as her argument. When she lost in court, suffragists saw they needed a new amendment of their own.

🤝 Let's Try Together: The Trial of Susan B. Anthony

Anthony was the only one of the 15 women brought to trial, in June 1873. The judge told the jury to find her guilty without discussing it. When he fined her $100, she said, "I shall never pay a dollar of your unjust penalty," and she never did. Let's weigh it: she lost the case, so did she fail? A trial puts an argument in front of the public. Anthony kept speaking for 33 more years. She died in 1906, but the 19th Amendment was later nicknamed the Susan B. Anthony Amendment in her honor.

🤝 Many Women, One Fight, and a Hard Split

The 15th Amendment (1870) protected Black men's votes but not women's, and the movement split in 1869. Stanton and Anthony refused to support an amendment that left women out and formed the National Woman Suffrage Association. Frances Ellen Watkins Harper, a Black poet and speaker, supported the 15th Amendment along with Frederick Douglass, and she helped form the American Woman Suffrage Association. The fight went on, state by state. Wyoming Territory let women vote in 1869, and other Western states followed. Women of many backgrounds led. Mabel Ping-Hua Lee, a Chinese immigrant teenager in New York City, helped lead a 1912 suffrage parade on horseback. In New Mexico, Adelina Otero-Warren insisted that suffrage writings be printed in Spanish as well as English.

👁 Watch Me Think

I have to tell this part honestly. During the split, Stanton argued that educated white women deserved the vote more than Black men, and she used insulting, racist words. Frederick Douglass said her language was offensive. Harper reminded white suffragists that Black women faced racism and sexism at the same time. So I ask: whose movement was this? It belonged to many women, but its leaders did not always treat each other as equals.

🤝 Let's Try Together: The 1913 Parade

On March 3, 1913, the day before President Woodrow Wilson's inauguration, thousands of women marched in Washington, D.C. Alice Paul and Lucy Burns organized it. Inez Milholland rode a white horse at the front. As the Illinois group lined up, white leaders asked Ida B. Wells-Barnett not to march with them and to go to the back. She refused. She waited along the route, then stepped into the Illinois group with two white allies and marched. Students from Howard University's Delta Sigma Theta sorority marched too, and so did Mary Church Terrell. Let's weigh it: the parade was meant to show unity, yet its leaders tried to push Black women out of sight. Black women made sure they were seen.

🗳️ Silent Sentinels, the 19th Amendment and Who Was Left Out

Alice Paul, a Quaker from New Jersey, wanted one national amendment instead of a slow state-by-state fight. Starting on January 10, 1917, her group, later called the National Woman's Party, picketed the White House with banners. People called them the Silent Sentinels. Beginning that summer, police arrested many of them. In jail, Paul, Lucy Burns and others refused to eat in protest, and news of their harsh treatment won the cause sympathy. In January 1918, President Wilson said he supported the amendment. Congress passed the 19th Amendment on June 4, 1919. On August 18, 1920, Tennessee became the 36th state to ratify it, after 24-year-old lawmaker Harry Burn voted yes, with a letter from his mother urging him to support suffrage in his pocket.

👁 Watch Me Think

Now I look for the gap, the way we did with the 15th Amendment in our Reconstruction lesson. The 19th Amendment says states cannot deny the vote "on account of sex." But states could still block voters for other reasons, like poll taxes, reading tests, or not being a citizen. So I ask: which women were still blocked by those other rules?

🤝 Let's Try Together: Reading the Left-Out Table

Let's do the date math. A Black woman in Mississippi kept from voting in 1920 by a poll tax and a reading test might wait until the Voting Rights Act of 1965: that is 45 years. Mabel Ping-Hua Lee marched for the vote in 1912, but Chinese immigrants could not become citizens until 1943, so the 19th Amendment did not give her the vote. Historians are not sure she ever voted. Remember Wong Kim Ark from Week 17: Asian American women born in the United States were citizens, so the amendment covered them. Native American women who were not yet citizens had to wait for the 1924 law, and in Arizona and New Mexico until 1948. A historian asks: was 1920 the end of the story, or a giant step on a longer road?

🔍 Source Detective: Three Documents, Seventy-Two Years

Here are three short primary sources: a declaration from 1848, a speech from 1866 and a law from 1920. Read each part, then compare them.

Primary Sources: 1848, 1866 and 1920

Part 1 (Declaration of Sentiments, 1848): "We hold these truths to be self-evident; that all men and women are created equal." It complains that man "has compelled her to submit to laws, in the formation of which she had no voice."

Part 2 (Frances Ellen Watkins Harper, 1866): "We are all bound up together in one great bundle of humanity, and society cannot trample on the weakest and feeblest of its members without receiving the curse in its own soul."

Part 3 (19th Amendment, 1920): "The right of citizens of the United States to vote shall not be denied or abridged by the United States or by any State on account of sex."

Word help: self-evident = clearly true; compelled = forced; submit = obey; formation = making; bound up = tied; trample = stomp on; feeblest = weakest; abridged = cut short or limited; on account of = because of.

Part 1: Declaration of Sentiments, Seneca Falls, New York, July 1848 (text from the National Park Service, Women's Rights National Historical Park). Part 2: Frances Ellen Watkins Harper, speech at the Eleventh National Woman's Rights Convention, New York City, May 1866. Part 3: 19th Amendment to the U.S. Constitution, passed by Congress June 4, 1919, ratified August 18, 1920 (National Archives). Real wording; short parts.

👁 Think Like a Historian

First I check the type of each source: a declaration, a speech and a law. Then I trace change over time. In 1848, women complained they had no voice in making laws. In 1920, a law finally said sex could not be used to deny the vote. Then I read the law closely, word by word, and I notice one important word: citizens. Who was not a citizen in 1920?

🗳️ People Who Fought for the Vote

Some spoke, some marched, some went to court or to jail. Read each card and ask: how did she fight, and what did she have to push against?

Susan B. Anthony

1820–1906

Born in Adams, Massachusetts, she was a teacher and abolitionist before she led the fight for women's votes. She voted in 1872, was arrested and never paid her $100 fine. With Stanton, she formed the National Woman Suffrage Association in 1869 and opposed the 15th Amendment because it left women out. She died 14 years before the 19th Amendment, which was nicknamed for her.

Elizabeth Cady Stanton

1815–1902

Born in Johnstown, New York, she helped plan the Seneca Falls convention and wrote the Declaration of Sentiments in 1848. She led the movement for more than 50 years. Told honestly: during the 1869 split, she argued that educated white women deserved the vote more than Black men and used racist words, which Frederick Douglass criticized.

Frances Ellen Watkins Harper

1825–1911

Born free in Baltimore, Maryland, and orphaned by age three, she became a famous poet, speaker and abolitionist, and the first African American woman to publish a short story. In 1866 she told a national convention that "we are all bound up together." She supported the 15th Amendment and helped found the National Association of Colored Women's Clubs.

Mabel Ping-Hua Lee

1896–1966

Born in Guangzhou, China, she moved to New York City in 1905. As a teenager, she helped lead a 1912 suffrage parade on horseback. She became the first Chinese woman to earn a PhD in economics at Columbia University. Because Chinese immigrants could not become citizens until 1943, the 19th Amendment did not give her the vote. She later led the First Chinese Baptist Church in New York's Chinatown.

Adelina "Nina" Otero-Warren

1881–1965

Born near Los Lunas, New Mexico, to a Hispano family, she led New Mexico's branch of Alice Paul's suffrage group starting in 1917. She pushed lawmakers to ratify the 19th Amendment, which New Mexico did on February 21, 1920. She served as Santa Fe's school superintendent, and in 1922 she became the first Hispanic woman to run for Congress.
