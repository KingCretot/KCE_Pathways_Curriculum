solstice
One of two moments each year when the Sun reaches its farthest point north or south, giving the longest or shortest day. Example: the December solstice is around December 21.
axial tilt
The lean of Earth's axis, about 23.5 degrees. The tilt, not Earth's distance from the Sun, causes the seasons.
hemisphere
Half of Earth. The equator divides Earth into the Northern and Southern Hemispheres.
polar night
A period when the Sun does not rise for a full day or more. It happens near the poles in winter.
lunisolar calendar
A calendar whose months follow the Moon but which adds extra months to stay in step with the seasons. The Hebrew and Chinese calendars are lunisolar.
tradition
A custom or belief passed down from one generation to the next.
principle
A basic idea or value that guides how people act. Kwanzaa has seven principles.

🌍 The Science of the Longest Night

Earth's axis is tilted about 23.5 degrees, and that axial tilt, not Earth's distance from the Sun, causes the seasons. At the December solstice, which comes on December 21 in 2026, the Northern Hemisphere is tilted farthest from the Sun, so the Sun stays low in the sky and northern days are at their shortest. At the same moment, the Southern Hemisphere is tilted toward the Sun, so places like Australia, Argentina and South Africa have their longest day and are starting summer. Far north, inside the Arctic Circle, the Sun does not rise at all for a time, which is called polar night.

👁 Watch Me Think

A common idea is that winter happens when Earth is far from the Sun. Let me test it with evidence. Earth is actually closest to the Sun in early January, during northern winter! And if distance caused winter, both hemispheres would have winter at the same time, but Australia has summer in December. So distance can't be the cause. The tilt explains both facts, because it changes how directly sunlight hits each hemisphere and how many hours the Sun is up.

🤝 Let's Try Together: Polar Night in Utqiaġvik, Alaska

Utqiaġvik is a town at the northern tip of Alaska, and most of its people are Iñupiat. It sits far inside the Arctic Circle. In 2021, the Sun set there on November 18 and did not rise again until January 22, 2022, a stretch of 65 days. It was not pitch-black the whole time: around midday the Sun is just below the horizon, so there are a few hours of twilight. Now reason it out: at the June solstice, the same town has the opposite, weeks when the Sun never sets.

🕎 Calendars and Festivals of Light

Many Jewish families celebrate Hanukkah for eight nights, lighting one more candle each night on a menorah that holds nine candles, including a helper candle called the shamash. Hanukkah remembers the rededication of the Temple in Jerusalem in 164 BCE, and it begins on the 25th of Kislev in the Hebrew calendar, a lunisolar calendar; in 2026, that is the evening of December 4. Many Christian families celebrate Christmas, which marks the birth of Jesus, on December 25, while many Orthodox Christians, such as in Ethiopia, Egypt, Russia and Serbia, celebrate on January 7. Christians celebrate in many local ways, from summer beach gatherings in Australia to Las Posadas in Mexico.

👁 Watch Me Think

Why would Christmas have two dates? I check the calendars. Many Orthodox churches still use the older Julian calendar for church holidays, and today the Julian calendar runs 13 days behind the Gregorian calendar that most of the world uses. So December 25 on the Julian calendar lands on January 7 on ours. Hanukkah moves for a different reason: the Hebrew calendar's months follow the Moon, and it adds a leap month in 7 of every 19 years to stay near the same season. Different calendars, not different facts, explain the moving dates.

🤝 Let's Try Together: Las Posadas, Step by Step

In Mexico and in many Mexican American communities, Las Posadas runs for nine nights, December 16 to 24. Posada means inn. Each night, one group plays the travelers, Mary and Joseph, outside, and another group plays the innkeepers inside; they sing verses back and forth until the travelers are let in. Then come tamales, warm ponche (a fruit punch with cinnamon and guava) and a piñata. Compare it with Hanukkah: both repeat a ritual on several nights in a row, and both retell a story from long ago, but they come from different religions and follow different calendars.

🕯️ Kwanzaa, Yalda Night and Dongzhi

Kwanzaa, created in 1966 by Maulana Karenga in California, is a cultural celebration of African American family, community and African heritage that runs from December 26 to January 1. Its name comes from the Swahili phrase matunda ya kwanza, "first fruits," and each day honors one of seven principles, such as umoja (unity) and kuumba (creativity), as a candle is lit on the kinara. On the longest night itself, many families in Iran and nearby countries celebrate Yalda Night, and many families in China and Chinese communities around the world celebrate Dongzhi.

👁 Watch Me Think

Kwanzaa is not tied to the solstice, so why is it in this lesson? I look at its symbols: three red, three green and one black candle, plus crops and ears of corn on a mat. "First fruits" connects it to harvest festivals, and lighting one more candle each night connects it to other winter festivals of light. It is also young, from 1966, which shows that people still create traditions today. Kwanzaa is cultural, not religious, so families of any faith can celebrate it.

🤝 Let's Try Together: What's in a Name?

Names hold history. Yalda comes from a Syriac word meaning "birth." The night is also called Shab-e Chelleh, "the night of forty," because it opens a forty-day stretch of deep winter; in 2022, UNESCO added Yalda/Chelleh to its list of the world's important cultural traditions. Dongzhi means "winter's arrival." It is the 22nd of the 24 solar terms in the traditional Chinese calendar, and some elders still count themselves a year older at Dongzhi. Red pomegranates stand for the glow of dawn, and round tangyuan stand for a family together.

👁 Think Like a Geographer

A geographer reads data two ways. Down a column, I compare places on the same day: in December, daylight grows as I move south. Across a row, I compare one place in two seasons: Sydney's long December day becomes a short June day. I also look for surprises. New York and Beijing are on different continents, but their daylight is almost the same because they are at almost the same latitude. That tells me latitude, not country, controls day length.

🌟 People Behind the Stories

Meet five people whose stories, art or science are part of winter celebrations today. For each one, ask: how do we know about this person, and what kind of source tells us?

Judah Maccabee

died 160 BCE

A Jewish leader in Judea who, with his father Mattathias and his brothers, led a revolt against the Seleucid king Antiochus IV, who had banned Jewish religious practice and taken over the Temple in Jerusalem. In 164 BCE, the Maccabees rededicated the Temple; Hanukkah, which means "dedication," remembers that event. The ancient books called 1 and 2 Maccabees tell the story. A much later tradition, written in the Talmud, tells of one day's oil burning for eight days.

Francis of Assisi

about 1181–1226

An Italian Christian friar known for living simply and caring for the poor and for animals. In December 1223, in a cave near Greccio, Italy, he set up a manger with hay, an ox and a donkey so people could picture the birth of Jesus, and townspeople came with candles and torches. It is called the first living nativity scene. We know about it from his biographer, Thomas of Celano.

King Gebre Meskel Lalibela

ruled about 1181–1221

A king of Ethiopia's Zagwe dynasty. Ethiopian tradition credits him with 11 churches carved out of solid rock in the town now named Lalibela, a UNESCO World Heritage Site since 1978. Historians think the churches may have been built in several stages. Each January 7, large crowds of Ethiopian Orthodox Christians gather there for Genna, Ethiopian Christmas.

Hafez

early 1300s–1390

A poet from Shiraz, in what is now Iran, who wrote short lyric poems called ghazals. His collected poems, the Divan, are found in many Iranian homes. On Yalda Night, many families open the Divan to a random page and read the poem aloud, a custom called fal-e Hafez. Visitors still gather at his tomb in Shiraz and recite his poems.

Guo Shoujing

1231–about 1316

A Chinese astronomer and engineer. In 1276, he and Wang Xun designed the Gaocheng observatory, where a tall tower casts a shadow onto a stone ruler about 31 meters long; the noon shadow is longest at the winter solstice. Using careful measurements, his Shoushi calendar, finished in 1280, set the year at 365.2425 days, within about 26 seconds of modern measurements. It was used for about 363 years.
