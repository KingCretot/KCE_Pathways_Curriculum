🌕 Full-Moon Festivals and Sukkot

In Korea, Chuseok falls on the 15th day of the 8th month of the lunisolar calendar, the night of the harvest full moon. In China and Vietnam, the Mid-Autumn Festival is held on that very same night. Many Jewish families celebrate Sukkot, a week-long harvest festival that begins on the 15th day of the Hebrew month of Tishrei. Because these calendars follow the moon, the dates move each year, landing in September or October. Not everyone celebrates these days, and families who do celebrate them in different ways.

👁 Watch Me Think

When I compare festivals, I sort facts into same and different. Same: all three come at harvest time and bring families together around food. Different: in Korea, many families travel to their hometowns, make half-moon rice cakes called songpyeon, and honor their ancestors. In China, families share round mooncakes, a symbol of being together; in Vietnam, the festival is a children's celebration with lantern parades. For Sukkot, families eat meals in a sukkah, a hut whose roof of branches is loose enough to see the stars. Each festival celebrates the same season in its own way.

🧩 Picture It: Three Autumn Harvest Festivals

Chuseok (Korea)

  • 15th day of the 8th lunar month; three days off in South Korea
  • Songpyeon steamed on pine needles; visits to family graves; honoring ancestors

Mid-Autumn Festival (China, Vietnam and other places)

  • Same full moon as Chuseok; called Zhongqiu Jie in China and Tết Trung Thu in Vietnam
  • Mooncakes, moon watching, lanterns; in Vietnam, children's lantern parades and lion dances

Sukkot (Jewish communities worldwide)

  • Seven days, starting on the 15th of Tishrei; also called the Festival of Booths and the Festival of Ingathering
  • Meals in a sukkah; the four species: a palm branch (lulav), a citron (etrog), myrtle and willow

🤝 Let's Try Together: Reading a Calendar Clue

In 2026, Chuseok's main day and the Mid-Autumn Festival both fell on September 25, and Sukkot began at sunset that same evening. In 2027, the Mid-Autumn Festival falls on September 15. Why did the date move? Twelve cycles of the moon add up to about 354 days, about 11 days shorter than a year of seasons. So these calendars add an extra month every few years to keep their festivals in autumn.

🍚 Pongal, Homowo and Two Thanksgivings

Pongal is a harvest festival celebrated by many Tamil families in Tamil Nadu, India, in Sri Lanka and in Tamil communities around the world. It begins around January 14 and is tied to the sun's yearly path, so its date barely changes. Homowo, the harvest festival of the Ga people of Accra, Ghana, is usually held in August or September. In North America, Canada has held Thanksgiving on the second Monday of October since 1957, and U.S. Thanksgiving falls on the fourth Thursday of November.

👁 Watch Me Think

A festival's name can hold its history. Pongal means "boiling over" in Tamil: families cook new rice with milk and jaggery, a type of unrefined cane sugar, until the pot overflows, then shout "Pongalo Pongal!" because overflowing means plenty. Homowo means "hooting at hunger." Ga oral history tells of a famine long ago, before a good harvest finally came. So both names point to thankfulness for having enough food, but Homowo also remembers a hard time.

🧩 Picture It: Four Harvest Celebrations

  • Pongal (Tamil people): about January 14, for 3–4 days. Pongal rice boiled over; kolam designs drawn with rice flour; Mattu Pongal honors cattle.
  • Homowo (Ga people, Ghana): August or September. Chiefs sprinkle kpokpoi, made from corn and palm oil; drumming and processions.
  • Thanksgiving in Canada: second Monday of October. Family harvest meals; a holiday in most provinces.
  • Thanksgiving in the U.S.: fourth Thursday of November. Family meals, parades and football.

🤝 Let's Try Together: Sun or Moon?

Pongal stays near January 14 every year, but Chuseok can land anywhere from early September to early October. Which calendar does each follow? Pongal is tied to the sun's path, so it barely moves. Chuseok follows the moon, so it shifts by weeks. Clue: if a holiday's date jumps around by weeks from year to year, it is probably set by the moon.

🦃 1621 and the National Day of Mourning

The Wampanoag, whose name means "People of the First Light," have lived in what is now Massachusetts and Rhode Island for more than 12,000 years. From about 1616 to 1619, diseases carried by European sailors killed a huge share of their people. In 1620, the Mayflower colonists settled at Patuxet, a village emptied by that sickness, and renamed it Plymouth. In March 1621, the sachem Ousamequin (Massasoit) formed an alliance with the English. That fall, about 90 of his men joined about 50 colonists for a three-day harvest gathering.

👁 Watch Me Think

I ask: who benefited, and who paid the cost? In 1621, both sides gained. The English needed food and protection, and Ousamequin wanted allies against rival nations such as the Narragansett after disease had weakened his people. But in the following decades, thousands more colonists arrived and took more Wampanoag land. In 1675, Ousamequin's son Metacom led a war to defend Wampanoag homelands; many Wampanoag people were killed, enslaved or forced from their land. So the 1621 meal really happened, but it is not the whole story.

🧩 Picture It: Timeline: Before and After 1621

🤝 Let's Try Together: Three True Stories

On the fourth Thursday of November, many families share a meal and give thanks. On that same day, since 1970, Indigenous people and their allies gather on Cole's Hill in Plymouth for the National Day of Mourning, to remember lost lives and land and to speak about Indigenous rights today. And on the second Tuesday of October, the Aquinnah Wampanoag of Martha's Vineyard hold their own harvest celebration, Cranberry Day. Which one is the real story? All three are true, and together they give a fuller picture.

🕵️ Source Detective: Two Voices, 350 Years Apart

Historians compare sources from different times and points of view. Source 1 is the main eyewitness account of the 1621 harvest gathering. Source 2 comes from a speech written in 1970 by Wamsutta Frank James, an Aquinnah Wampanoag elder, for the 350th anniversary of the Pilgrims' arrival.

Primary Sources: A Letter from 1621 and a Speech from 1970

Source 1: Edward Winslow, English colonist, letter of December 1621 (today's spelling; words in [brackets] are our edits: [Wampanoag] replaces the word Winslow used for Indigenous people; ... marks words left out)

"Our harvest being gotten in, our governor sent four men on fowling, that so we might after a special manner rejoice together, after we had gathered the fruits of our labors... many of the [Wampanoag] coming amongst us, and amongst the rest their greatest king Massasoit, with some ninety men, whom for three days we entertained and feasted, and they went out and killed five deer, which they brought to the plantation and bestowed on our governor... we often wish you partakers of our plenty."

Source 2: Wamsutta Frank James, Aquinnah Wampanoag, speech written in 1970 (exact words, in order; ... marks words left out)

"This is a time of celebration for you - celebrating an anniversary of a beginning for the white man in America... It is with a heavy heart that I look back upon what happened to my People... Massasoit, the great Sachem of the Wampanoag, knew these facts, yet he and his People welcomed and befriended the settlers of the Plymouth Plantation... What has happened cannot be changed, but today we must work towards a more humane America..."

Word help: fowling = hunting birds. bestowed on = gave to. partakers of our plenty = sharing in our rich supply. humane = kind and caring. these facts = earlier in his speech, James lists wrongs from before and after 1620, such as explorers kidnapping Wampanoag people and the Pilgrims taking the Wampanoag's stored corn and beans.

Edward Winslow, letter of December 1621, printed in Mourt's Relation (London, 1622), modern spelling as published by Pilgrim Hall Museum, Plymouth, Massachusetts; words in [brackets] are our edits. Wamsutta Frank James, speech prepared for the 350th anniversary of the Pilgrims' landing (1970), text as published by UAINE, the group James founded (uaine.org).

👁 Think Like a Historian

First I source each document: who wrote it, when, and for whom? Winslow was an English colonist writing to a friend in England in a letter that was printed for English readers, and he stresses the colony's plenty, perhaps to encourage support. James was a Wampanoag man looking back 350 years, writing for a celebration that he felt left out his people's losses. Neither source alone tells the whole story; together, they show two points of view on the same events.

🧑‍🤝‍🧑 Voices of the Thanksgiving Story

These people shaped how the harvest of 1621 and the holiday of Thanksgiving are remembered. As you read each card, ask: whose point of view does this person add?

Ousamequin (Massasoit)

died about 1661

Ousamequin was the sachem of the Pokanoket Wampanoag; "Massasoit" was a title meaning Great Sachem. After epidemics weakened his people, he made an alliance with Plymouth in March 1621, hoping for help against rivals such as the Narragansett. He kept the peace with the English for about 40 years, but colonists kept pressing onto Wampanoag land. A statue of him now stands on Cole's Hill in Plymouth.

Tisquantum (Squanto)

died 1622

A Patuxet Wampanoag man, Tisquantum was kidnapped in 1614 by English captain Thomas Hunt and taken to Spain to be sold. He made his way to England, learned English and returned home in 1619, only to find Patuxet emptied by disease. He became an interpreter between Ousamequin and the colonists and showed them how to plant corn with fish to enrich the soil.

Sarah Josepha Hale

1788–1879

Hale edited Godey's Lady's Book, one of the most popular magazines of her time, and wrote "Mary Had a Little Lamb." Starting in 1846, she wrote letters to presidents and governors urging a national Thanksgiving. In 1863, during the Civil War, President Abraham Lincoln proclaimed a national day of Thanksgiving for the last Thursday in November.

Wamsutta Frank James

1923–2001

An Aquinnah Wampanoag elder, James was invited in 1970 to speak at a Massachusetts celebration of the 350th anniversary of the Pilgrims' landing. After reading his speech, organizers would not let him give it as written. On Thanksgiving Day 1970, he and others gathered on Cole's Hill in Plymouth, beside the statue of Ousamequin, for the first National Day of Mourning. The group he founded, known as UAINE, still holds it every year.

Beverly Wright

Aquinnah Wampanoag leader

A former chairwoman of the Wampanoag Tribe of Gay Head (Aquinnah), Wright wrote a 2017 essay about Cranberry Day for What It Means to Be American, a project of the Smithsonian and Zócalo Public Square. She described tribal children being excused from school, families picking berries in bogs that only tribal members may harvest, and a potluck dinner with drumming, singing and stories from Elders. Her essay shows a Wampanoag harvest tradition that continues today.
