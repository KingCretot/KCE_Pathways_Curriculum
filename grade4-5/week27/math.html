🌌 Why Astronomers Love Powers of 10

Nonfiction Connection
📖

Real Talk: The Adler Planetarium

The Adler Planetarium opened in Chicago in 1930. It was the first planetarium in the Western Hemisphere, and it stands at the end of a peninsula on the shore of Lake Michigan. Inside a planetarium, a dome becomes a night sky full of stars and planets. Space numbers are huge. Earth is about 93,000,000 miles from the Sun, the Moon is about 239,000 miles from Earth on average, and light travels about 186,000 miles in one second. Writing all those zeros is slow, and it is easy to drop one, so scientists use powers of 10 and exponents to keep track.

🖼️ Picture It: Exponents Count the Tens

👁

A Short Way to Write Many Tens

A power of 10 is a number like 10, 100, or 1,000 that you get by multiplying tens together. In 103, the 10 is the base and the 3 is the exponent. The exponent tells how many times the base is used as a factor: 103 = 10 × 10 × 10 = 1,000.

The exponent = the number of zeros
Pattern: 104 = 10,000 has 4 zeros, and 106 = 1,000,000 has 6 zeros. Each extra factor of 10 adds one more zero.
Standard form: A number written with digits, like 1,000, is in standard form. 103 is the same number in exponent form.
Watch out: 103 does NOT mean 10 × 3. 10 × 3 = 30, but 103 = 1,000.
With another factor: 4 × 103 = 4 × 1,000 = 4,000.

✖️ Multiplying by Powers of 10

🔭

Dr. Kostas’s Place-Value Slide

On a place-value chart, each place is 10 times the value of the place to its right. So when you multiply by 10, every digit moves one place to the left and becomes 10 times as large. Multiplying by 103 moves every digit three places to the left.

45 × 10345,000
3.6 × 102360
0.47 × 103470
2.05 × 1020.5
3.6 × 102 = 360
Move the digits: 102 means two tens, so each digit moves 2 places to the left. The 3 ones become 3 hundreds, and the 6 tenths become 6 tens.
Fill the empty place: the ones place is now empty, so write a 0. A placeholder zero holds an empty place so the other digits stay in the right places.
Shortcut check: it looks like the decimal point jumps 2 places to the right: 3.6 → 36. → 360. The exponent tells how many places.
Whole numbers: for 45 × 103, just write 3 zeros after 45 to get 45,000. Do not do that with decimals: 3.6 × 100 is not 3.600.

➗ Dividing by Powers of 10

🪐

Mr. Koji Shrinks the Numbers

Dividing undoes multiplying. When you divide by 10, every digit moves one place to the right and becomes 1/10 as large. Dividing by 102 moves every digit two places to the right, and dividing by 103 moves every digit three places.

4,500 ÷ 10245
3.8 ÷ 100.38
72 ÷ 1030.072
960 ÷ 1040.096
72 ÷ 103 = 0.072
Move the digits: each digit moves 3 places to the right. The 7 tens become 7 hundredths, and the 2 ones become 2 thousandths.
Fill the empty place: nothing is left in the tenths place, so a placeholder zero goes there: 0.072. Without it, 0.72 would be ten times too large.
Shortcut check: it looks like the decimal point jumps 3 places to the left: 72. → 7.2 → 0.72 → 0.072.
Sense check: dividing by a power of 10 always makes a whole number or decimal smaller, so the quotient must be less than 72.

🌟 Vocabulary Round-Up

Flip each card to review today’s words. Each card shows the sentence from today’s lesson where the word appeared.

power of 10
A number made by multiplying tens, such as 100 or 1,000. “A power of 10 is a number like 10, 100, or 1,000 that you get by multiplying tens together.”
base
The number being multiplied in a power. “In 103, the 10 is the base and the 3 is the exponent.”
exponent
The small raised number that tells how many factors. “The exponent tells how many times the base is used as a factor: 103 = 10 × 10 × 10 = 1,000.”
standard form
A number written with digits, like 4,000. “A number written with digits, like 1,000, is in standard form.”
place value
The value a digit has because of its place. “On a place-value chart, each place is 10 times the value of the place to its right.”
placeholder zero
A zero that holds an empty place. “A placeholder zero holds an empty place so the other digits stay in the right places.”
factor
A number that is multiplied. “The exponent tells how many times the base is used as a factor: 103 = 10 × 10 × 10 = 1,000.”
1/10 as large
What one move to the right does to a digit. “When you divide by 10, every digit moves one place to the right and becomes 1/10 as large.”
