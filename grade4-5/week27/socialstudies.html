🍇 Farmworkers and the Delano Grape Strike

Farmworkers picked the fruits and vegetables Americans ate, but many earned low pay, lived in poor housing and worked near harmful pesticides. In 1962, Dolores Huerta and César Chávez founded the National Farm Workers Association, a group of mostly Latino farmworkers. Filipino American grape pickers had their own group, the Agricultural Workers Organizing Committee, led by Larry Itliong. Earlier in 1965, growers had offered Filipino workers lower wages than Mexican guest workers. On September 8, 1965, about 1,500 Filipino American workers walked out of the vineyards near Delano, California, in a strike. Itliong asked the National Farm Workers Association to join, and on September 16, its members voted to join the Filipino picket lines. In 1966 the two groups joined into one union, which became the United Farm Workers. Across the country, many shoppers stopped buying grapes in a boycott led by Dolores Huerta. The strike lasted five years and ended in 1970 with union contracts.

👁 Watch Me Think

I notice that this strike is often told as one man's story, but the evidence shows a team. Filipino American workers led by Larry Itliong walked out first, and the Latino workers of Huerta and Chávez joined them eight days later. Dolores Huerta led the grape boycott and was the lead negotiator on the final contracts. In 1972, when an Arizona bill tried to limit farmworker unions, she coined the rallying cry "Sí, se puede," which means "Yes, it is possible." The idea of working across groups grew. In 1968, students at the University of California, Berkeley, met at the home of graduate students Yuji Ichioka and Emma Gee and formed the Asian American Political Alliance. They chose the name Asian American so that people with Chinese, Japanese, Filipino and other roots could work together. Student strikes at Berkeley and San Francisco State led to the first ethnic studies departments, where students could study the histories of their own communities.

🤝 Let's Try Together: How a Strike and a Boycott Work Together

Renata, a fourth grader whose family lives near Delano, California, is learning how the grape strike worked. Let's help her. In a strike, workers stop working until employers agree to changes. Strikes are hard, because workers on strike do not get paid by the employer. In a boycott, shoppers stop buying a product. The farmworkers used both at once: no one picked the grapes, and fewer people bought them. That put pressure on the growers from two sides. Let's do the math: the strike began in September 1965 and ended with contracts in 1970, about 5 years. Renata asks her grandmother: what would make you keep going for 5 years without giving up?

🪶 Indigenous Nations and Self-Determination

In the 1950s and 1960s, the federal government tried a policy called termination: it ended its official relationship with some tribal nations, and those nations lost land and services the government had promised in treaties. Indigenous people organized to protect their rights. In the Pacific Northwest, Billy Frank Jr. of the Nisqually Tribe and other Native fishers held fish-ins, fishing in their rivers as the 1854 Medicine Creek Treaty allowed, even when they were arrested. On November 20, 1969, 89 Indigenous people calling themselves Indians of All Tribes, many of them college students, began an occupation of Alcatraz Island in San Francisco Bay. Richard Oakes, an Akwesasne Mohawk student, was one of their leaders. They stayed for 19 months to call attention to broken promises. On July 8, 1970, President Richard Nixon told Congress the government should support self-determination, the right of tribal nations to govern themselves. In 1975, Congress passed the Indian Self-Determination and Education Assistance Act.

👁 Watch Me Think

I notice the word self-determination. It means Native nations make their own decisions, instead of the federal government deciding for them. In his 1970 message, President Nixon wrote: "Self-determination among the Indian people can and must be encouraged without the threat of eventual termination." Why did that matter? Remember Week 18, when the government sent Native children to boarding schools and broke treaties. Then I look for results I can check. During the Alcatraz occupation, the government returned Blue Lake, a sacred place, and 48,000 acres of land to Taos Pueblo in New Mexico. In 1973, Ada Deer and other Menominee leaders in Wisconsin won back their nation's federal recognition, which termination had taken away. So I ask: how do we know protests made a difference? We look for changes in laws and in land.

🤝 Let's Try Together: The Fish Wars on the Nisqually River

Reuben, a fifth grader in Olympia, Washington, is visiting the Billy Frank Jr. Nisqually National Wildlife Refuge with his class. Let's help him read the story of the fish wars like a historian. In 1945, when Billy Frank Jr. was 14, he was arrested for fishing for salmon at Frank's Landing on the Nisqually River, on his own family's land. Over the years he was arrested more than 50 times. He and other Native fishers said the 1854 treaty protected their right to fish in their usual places. On February 12, 1974, Judge George Boldt agreed. He ruled that treaty tribes could catch 50 percent of the harvestable salmon. Let's do the math: from 1945 to 1974 is 29 years of standing up for a treaty right. Reuben asks: why would someone keep getting arrested for the same thing for so long?

♿ Title IX and the Section 504 Sit-In

Before 1972, schools and colleges could legally treat girls and women unfairly. Patsy Takemoto Mink of Hawaii was turned down by several medical schools because she was a woman. In 1964 she became the first woman of color elected to Congress. In the 1971 to 1972 school year, only 294,015 girls played high school sports, compared with nearly 3.7 million boys. Mink worked with Representative Edith Green and Senator Birch Bayh on a new law. On June 23, 1972, President Nixon signed Title IX, which bans sex discrimination in schools and colleges that receive federal money. People with disabilities were shut out too. Many children with disabilities were kept out of public schools, and many buildings had no ramps. In 1973, Congress passed Section 504 of the Rehabilitation Act, which banned discrimination against people with disabilities in programs that receive federal money. But for four years, the government did not sign the rules needed to enforce it.

👁 Watch Me Think

I notice that a law is not enough if no one enforces it. On April 5, 1977, people with disabilities began sit-ins at federal offices in several cities. In San Francisco, more than 120 protesters stayed inside the federal building for almost four weeks. Judy Heumann and Kitty Cone were among the leaders. Brad Lomax, a member of the Black Panther Party who used a wheelchair, connected the protesters with the Black Panthers, who brought them a hot meal every day. On April 28, 1977, Joseph Califano, the Secretary of Health, Education, and Welfare, signed the rules. So I ask: how did different movements help each other? The Black Panthers fed the protesters, and the words of Section 504 were copied from the Civil Rights Act of 1964.

🤝 Let's Try Together: How Much Did Title IX Change?

Renzo, a fifth grader who plays soccer at his school, wants to know how much Title IX changed things. Let's do the math with him. In 1971–72, 294,015 girls played high school sports. By 2010–11, about 3.2 million girls did. 3.2 million is 3,200,000. If we divide 3,200,000 by 294,015, we get about 11. So roughly 11 times as many girls were playing. Here is a surprise: the main sentence of Title IX has only 37 words, and none of them is "sports." It bans sex discrimination in every education program that gets federal money, and school sports are one of those programs. Renzo asks: why might a short law change so much?

🔍 Source Detective: Three Laws, One Pattern

Here are three short primary sources: parts of three laws from 1964, 1972 and 1973. Read each part slowly, then look for the pattern.

Primary Sources: 1964, 1972 and 1973

Part 1 (Civil Rights Act of 1964, Title VI, signed July 2, 1964): "No person in the United States shall, on the ground of race, color, or national origin, be excluded from participation in, be denied the benefits of, or be subjected to discrimination under any program or activity receiving Federal financial assistance."

Part 2 (Title IX of the Education Amendments of 1972, signed June 23, 1972): "No person in the United States shall, on the basis of sex, be excluded from participation in, be denied the benefits of, or be subjected to discrimination under any education program or activity receiving Federal financial assistance . . ."

Part 3 (Section 504 of the Rehabilitation Act of 1973, as the law reads today): "No otherwise qualified individual with a disability in the United States . . . shall, solely by reason of her or his disability, be excluded from the participation in, be denied the benefits of, or be subjected to discrimination under any program or activity receiving Federal financial assistance . . ."

Word help: on the ground of and on the basis of = because of; excluded = kept out; denied = refused; subjected to = made to suffer; discrimination = unfair treatment of a group; Federal financial assistance = money from the national government; otherwise qualified = able to meet the program's other requirements; solely by reason of = only because of.

Part 1: Civil Rights Act of 1964, Public Law 88-352, Title VI, Section 601 (National Archives; United States Code, Title 42, Section 2000d). Part 2: Education Amendments of 1972, Title IX, Section 901(a) (United States Code, Title 20, Section 1681). Part 3: Rehabilitation Act of 1973, Section 504, signed September 26, 1973; the words shown are today's wording, because Congress later updated the law's language about disability (United States Code, Title 29, Section 794). Real wording; short parts.

👁 Think Like a Historian

First I read the three parts side by side. They are almost the same sentence! Part 1 protects people from unfair treatment because of race, color or national origin. Part 2 copies the pattern for sex, but only in education. Part 3 copies it again for disability. That gives me evidence for something historians say: the movements of the 1970s borrowed the tools of the civil rights movement, even its words. Then I ask: who is protected in each part, and who is not named yet? Laws grow when people who were left out organize.

🌟 People Who Raised Their Voices

They worked in fields, on rivers, in Congress and in a federal building. Read each card and ask: what was this person shut out of, and how did they respond?

Dolores Huerta

1930–2026

Born in New Mexico, she co-founded the National Farm Workers Association in 1962, led the national grape boycott and coined "Sí, se puede." She received the Presidential Medal of Freedom in 2012. She died on October 28, 2026, at age 96.

Larry Itliong

1913–1977

Born in the Philippines, he came to the United States as a teenager and worked for years on farms. As leader of the Agricultural Workers Organizing Committee, he led about 1,500 Filipino American workers out of the Delano vineyards on September 8, 1965.

Billy Frank Jr.

1931–2014

A member of the Nisqually Tribe in Washington State, he was first arrested at 14 for fishing on his family's land and was arrested more than 50 times. He spent his life protecting salmon and treaty rights. In 2015 he was honored with the Presidential Medal of Freedom.

Patsy Takemoto Mink

1927–2002

Born on Maui, Hawaii, she was turned down by medical schools because she was a woman, so she became a lawyer. In 1964 she became the first woman of color elected to Congress. In 2002, after she died, Title IX was renamed the Patsy T. Mink Equal Opportunity in Education Act.

Judy Heumann

1947–2023

She had polio as a baby and used a wheelchair. New York City at first would not let her attend public school, and later it refused her a teaching license. She sued at age 22 and received her license. In 1977 she helped lead the Section 504 sit-in in San Francisco.
