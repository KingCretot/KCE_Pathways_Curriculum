💧 Counting Earth's Water

How much water does Earth have? Scientists measure huge amounts of water in cubic miles. One cubic mile is a cube of water one mile long, one mile wide and one mile deep, and the U.S. Geological Survey (USGS) says it holds more than 1.1 trillion gallons. Earth has about 332.5 million cubic miles of water in its oceans, ice, ground, lakes, rivers, air and living things. If you could gather all of it into one ball, the USGS says the ball would be only about 860 miles across, a bit more than the distance from Salt Lake City, Utah, to Topeka, Kansas. Most of that water is saline, or salty. The oceans, seas and bays alone hold about 321 million cubic miles, more than 96 percent of all Earth's water. Fresh water is only about 2.5 percent, and most of it is hard to reach. About 68.7 percent of all fresh water is frozen in ice caps, glaciers and permanent snow, mainly near the poles and in Greenland. About 30.1 percent is fresh groundwater, stored in soil and rock. Rivers, where people get most of the fresh surface water they use, hold only about 509 cubic miles, about 0.0002 percent of Earth's water: a tiny fraction of one percent. These estimates come from the Russian hydrologist Igor Shiklomanov, published in 1993, and the USGS still shares them in its Water Science School today.

👁 Watch Me Think

Penelope lives in Milwaukee, Wisconsin, right on the shore of Lake Michigan. She says, "There is so much fresh water here that Earth can never run short!" Here is how I check her idea with the USGS numbers. Fresh lakes hold about 21,830 cubic miles. Fresh groundwater holds about 2,526,000 cubic miles, more than 100 times as much. Ice holds about 5,773,000 cubic miles, but most of it is frozen far away. So lakes, even giant ones, are a tiny slice of Earth's water. The Environmental Protection Agency says the Great Lakes hold about 21 percent of the world's supply of surface fresh water. That is a lot to protect, but it is not endless.

🤝 Let's Try Together: Compare Huge Numbers

Let's compare ice and fresh groundwater. Ice: about 5,773,000 cubic miles. Groundwater: about 2,526,000. Rounded to the nearest million, that is about 6 million and about 3 million, so roughly twice as much fresh water is frozen as is underground. Now compare the oceans with ALL fresh water. The oceans hold more than 96 percent, and fresh water is about 2.5 percent. 96 divided by 2.5 is about 38, so the oceans hold roughly 38 times as much water as all the fresh water combined. Try It at Home, with a grown-up: fill a 1-liter bottle with water to stand for all of Earth's water. Use a measuring spoon or cup to pour 25 milliliters into a small cup: that is all the fresh water. About 17 milliliters of it stands for ice and about 8 for groundwater. The fresh lakes and rivers would be less than one-tenth of a milliliter!

📊 Graphing the Data

Numbers this big and this different are hard to picture, so scientists graph them. The trick is choosing the right graph. A circle graph, also called a pie chart, shows parts of a whole that add up to 100 percent. It works well for salt water versus fresh water, but tiny parts, like rivers at about 0.0002 percent of all water, become slivers too thin to see. A bar graph compares amounts side by side. Every bar should start at zero and use an even scale, such as 1 square for every 10 billion gallons. Otherwise a small difference can look huge, and the graph misleads its reader. A line graph shows how one amount changes over time. Scientists also zoom in: first they graph all water, then they make a second graph of just fresh water, then a third of just the liquid fresh water people can reach. Each zoom makes the small parts easier to see. Here is real data to graph. The USGS estimated that people in the United States withdrew, or took out from rivers, lakes and the ground, about 322 billion gallons of water per day in 2015. The two biggest uses were thermoelectric power plants, which use heat to make electricity, at about 133 billion gallons per day, and irrigation for farms, at about 118 billion. Public water supply, the water that flows to homes and businesses, was about 39 billion gallons per day.

🌱 Why Fresh Water Matters

Fresh water is precious because people, farms, factories, plants and animals all depend on the small share we can reach. The Environmental Protection Agency (EPA) says less than 1 percent of Earth's water is available for human use; the rest is salty, frozen or too hard to reach. Each American uses an average of about 82 gallons of water a day at home, and the average American family uses more than 300 gallons a day at home. Roughly 70 percent of home water use happens indoors, and about 30 percent happens outdoors. Groundwater is a big part of the story: the USGS says about 26 percent of the water used in the United States in 2015 came from the ground. When people pump groundwater out faster than rain and melting snow can refill it, the water stored underground shrinks. How can scientists see water underground? They use gravity! From 2002 to 2017, NASA and Germany flew the twin GRACE satellites (Gravity Recovery and Climate Experiment), and in 2018 they launched the twin GRACE Follow-On satellites. Where water collects, there is more mass, and more mass means slightly stronger gravity. As the twin satellites fly over, that changing pull makes the distance between them change by tiny amounts, measured to within the width of a red blood cell. Their monthly gravity maps show where groundwater is shrinking or growing and how much ice Greenland and Antarctica are losing. Small actions help too. The EPA says household leaks can waste about 9,400 gallons a year for an average family, and turning off the tap while brushing your teeth can save 8 gallons a day.

👁 Watch Me Think

Pema notices a faucet dripping in her library's restroom. "It's just a few drops," her friend says. Here is how I reason with the EPA's numbers. Household leaks can waste about 180 gallons a week for an average family, which adds up to about 9,400 gallons a year. That is clean, treated water going down the drain. Fixing leaks is one of the easiest ways to protect the small share of water we can use. So Pema tells a librarian, who reports the drip to the building staff.

🧑‍🔬 People Who Counted and Protected Water

Scientists and community leaders from around the world measured Earth's water and fought to keep it clean.

Ellen Swallow Richards

1842–1911

Ellen Swallow Richards was admitted to the Massachusetts Institute of Technology (MIT) in 1870, the first woman admitted there, and earned a chemistry degree in 1873. In 1887, the Massachusetts State Board of Health asked for a survey of the state's waters. Her two-year survey analyzed more than 20,000 water samples. It led Massachusetts to set the first state water-quality standards in the nation and to build the first modern city sewage treatment plant, in Lowell, Massachusetts. She is also remembered as a founder of home economics in the United States.

Ruth Patrick

1907–2013

Ruth Patrick was born in Topeka, Kansas. When she was 7, her father gave her a microscope of her own, and she peered at drops of water they had collected. She became an expert on diatoms, tiny one-celled living things in water, and was the first to notice that different kinds of diatoms live in different conditions, so they reveal the quality of the water. In 1947 she started the Department of Limnology, the study of fresh water, at the Academy of Natural Sciences in Philadelphia. Her "ecosystem approach," which checks a river's chemistry plus the plants, insects and fish living in it, became the standard way to monitor rivers. She received the National Medal of Science in 1996. She was fond of repeating her father's advice: "Leave the world a better place for having passed through it."

Igor Shiklomanov

1939–2010

Igor Shiklomanov was born in 1939 in the Tver Region of Russia and in 1981 became director of the State Hydrological Institute in St. Petersburg. He served as a leading scientist for collecting and analyzing water data from around the whole world, starting with a 1974 report for the United Nations Educational, Scientific and Cultural Organization (UNESCO). The USGS table of where Earth's water is comes from his 1993 chapter, "World fresh water resources." In 2001 he received the International Hydrology Prize, and in 2006 the Tyler Prize for Environmental Achievement.

Rajendra Singh

Water Man of India

Rajendra Singh moved to rural Rajasthan, a dry state in India, in the mid-1980s, planning to open health clinics. Villagers told him water was the greater need. With his group, Tarun Bharat Sangh, he helped villagers build johads, traditional earthen dams that store rainwater. Water came back to more than 1,000 villages, and five rivers flowed again. In 2015 he won the Stockholm Water Prize. The prize committee said that "today's water problems cannot be solved by science or technology alone."
