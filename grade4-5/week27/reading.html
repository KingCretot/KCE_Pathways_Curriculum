🖼️ Picture It: How a Mystery Works

Every mystery has a puzzle to solve, a detective who solves it, and suspects who might be responsible. The author hides clues in the story. Some clues point toward the truth. Others, called red herrings, are planted to point the wrong way. A good detective, and a good reader, makes inferences from ALL the clues before deciding.

Model TextThe Empty Cookie Jar

An original story

When Mina got home, the cookie jar was empty. Her little brother, Monty, had a brown smear across his shirt. “Caught you!” Mina said. Monty looked down. “That’s paint from art class,” he said. Mina looked again. The smear smelled like paint, not chocolate. And the cookie jar sat on the top shelf, far above Monty’s reach, right next to Dad’s car keys.

ClueReal clue or red herring?Why
Brown smear on Monty’s shirtRed herringIt looks like chocolate, but it is paint from art class.
Jar on the top shelf, beyond Monty’s reachReal clueMonty could not reach it, so someone tall probably emptied it.
Dad’s car keys beside the jarReal clueDad was at that shelf recently. Inference: Dad may have taken the cookies.
💡

Loud Clues and Quiet Clues

Red herrings are often “loud”: easy to notice and exciting. Real clues are often “quiet”: small details that only matter when you think about them. Before you name a suspect, ask: does EVERY clue fit my answer?

🌟 Vocabulary Round-Up

Flip each card to review today’s words. The last two come straight from today’s story.

mystery
A story about a puzzle or a problem that a character must figure out using clues.
clue
A detail that helps solve a mystery.
red herring
A clue that seems important but points readers toward the wrong answer.
suspect
A character who might be responsible for the problem in a mystery.
alibi
Proof that a suspect was somewhere else or could not have done it.
conclusion
The decision you reach after thinking about all the evidence.
draped
Hung loosely over something. “Live oaks draped in Spanish moss shaded the square”
startled
Suddenly surprised or a little frightened. “Mikael looked up, startled.”

📖 Text A: Mystery 1

This mystery is told by a narrator who is not in the story (third person). As you read, list every clue you notice. Which ones point to the truth, and which are red herrings? Notice the paragraph numbers; you will use them.

Text A • Mystery StoryThe Case of the Missing Compass

An original story

1Every Saturday, Mitsuko helped out at Ms. Moira’s bookshop, which faced Wright Square in Savannah, Georgia. Live oaks draped in Spanish moss shaded the square, and Ms. Moira liked to remind visitors that Tomochichi, a Creek leader who helped James Oglethorpe found the Georgia colony in 1733, is buried there.

2This Saturday, something was wrong. The brass compass that was the prize for the shop’s summer reading contest was gone from its glass case. “Someone stole it!” Ms. Moira cried. Mitsuko leaned close to the case. The glass was not cracked, and the little lock was still clicked shut.

3Her friend Montrell pointed at the floor. “Look! Spanish moss, right under the case. The thief must have come in from the square.” Mitsuko picked up the gray strand and glanced at the door. Every time it opened, the breeze blew bits of moss across the floor. She had already swept up three piles that morning.

4“What about that tall man with the huge backpack?” Montrell whispered. “He left in a hurry.” Ms. Moira shook her head. “Mr. Mohan? He buys a stack of books every Saturday and runs to catch the bus.” She held up his receipt: six paperbacks, paid in full.

5Mitsuko thought about the lock. “Who has a key to the case?” she asked. “Only me,” said Ms. Moira, “and my nephew, Mikael. He opened the shop this morning.” From the back room came the scratch of a pencil. Mitsuko tiptoed to the doorway. Mikael was bent over a big poster for the reading contest, sketching a circle with a pointed needle inside it.

6Beside his elbow, half buried under sheets of poster paper, lay the brass compass. Mikael looked up, startled. “I borrowed it so I could draw it for the poster,” he said. “I meant to put it back before anyone noticed.” Ms. Moira laughed with relief. Montrell’s face turned pink. “I was sure it was the man with the backpack,” he admitted. Mitsuko smiled. “The backpack was the loudest clue. The lock was the quiet one.”

Text B • Mystery Told by a CharacterThe Case of the Vanishing Hand Pies

An original story

1I thought I had learned my lesson at the bookshop. I was wrong.

2On Sunday, Grandma Mimi set up a table near the big white fountain in Forsyth Park to sell peach hand pies for the neighborhood food pantry. When I came back from filling our water jug, one whole tray of pies was gone. Under the next table sat Moxie, Mrs. Mitra’s beagle, licking her lips.

3“Moxie ate them!” I shouted. Mrs. Mitra, who was selling honey at the next table, looked hurt. “Montrell, Moxie has been tied to my chair all morning,” she said. “Her leash only reaches halfway to your table.”

4I looked at the leash. She was right. Then I noticed something quieter: a line of white powdered-sugar dots running across the grass from our table to the cooler in the shade of a live oak. I lifted the lid. There was the tray, with every pie still on it.

5“Oh, I moved those,” Grandma Mimi said, walking up with a stack of napkins. “That sun was melting the glaze.” And Moxie? Mrs. Mitra had just given her a dog biscuit.

6I said sorry to Mrs. Mitra and scratched Moxie behind the ears. Next time, I am going to look for the quiet clues first.

📝 Two Mysteries, One Theme

🪜

Four Steps to Compare Two Mysteries

  1. Puzzle: what is missing in each story, and who is blamed first?
  2. Clues: which clue is the red herring in each, and which quiet clue solves it?
  3. Telling: who tells each story? A narrator (third person) or a character (first person)?
  4. Theme: what lesson do BOTH stories teach? Write it as a full sentence about life.
Text A: The Missing CompassText B: The Vanishing Hand Pies
Who tells itA third-person narratorMontrell, in the first person
First blamedThe man with the backpackMoxie the beagle
Who solves itMitsuko, a careful detectiveMontrell, after his own mistake
The truthMikael borrowed the compassGrandma Mimi moved the pies
🎯

Same Theme, Different Path

Both stories suggest a theme like: “Before you blame someone, look at all the evidence, because the most obvious clue can be misleading.” In Text A, Mitsuko shows this lesson by being careful. In Text B, Montrell learns it the hard way, by making the mistake himself.
