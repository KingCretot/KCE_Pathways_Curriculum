🏚️ Why a New Plan? The Articles of Confederation

The first plan for the national government, the Articles of Confederation, took effect in 1781. Leaders feared a powerful king, so they made the national government weak: Congress could not collect taxes, there was no president to carry out laws, and there were no national courts. Changing the Articles needed all 13 states to agree. In 1786 and 1787, farmers in debt in western Massachusetts rose up in Shays' Rebellion, and Congress had no army or money to respond, so in May 1787, delegates met in Philadelphia to fix the government.

👁 Watch Me Think

I look at the problem from the leaders' side. They had just fought a king, so they were afraid of strong government. But a government that cannot collect taxes cannot pay soldiers or debts. So the real question was balance: how do you make a government strong enough to work, but not so strong that it takes away freedom? Every choice in 1787 tries to answer that question.

🤝 Let's Try Together: Who Was in the Room?

From May to September 1787, 55 delegates from 12 states met in the Pennsylvania State House in Philadelphia; Rhode Island did not send anyone. George Washington led the meeting, and James Madison took careful notes every day. Let's ask a historian's question: who was in the room? All the delegates were white men, and many were wealthy enslavers. No women, no Black Americans and no Indigenous nations had a seat. On September 17, 1787, 39 delegates signed the Constitution, which begins "We the People."

⚖️ Three Branches and Checks and Balances

The Constitution splits power into three branches. The legislative branch, Congress, makes the laws; it has two parts, the Senate and the House of Representatives. The executive branch, led by the president, carries out the laws. The judicial branch, led by the Supreme Court, decides what laws mean in court cases. With checks and balances, each branch can limit the others: the president can veto a bill, Congress can override a veto with a two-thirds vote, and the Senate must approve the judges the president chooses.

👁 Watch Me Think

I picture a three-legged stool. If one leg grows too long, the stool tips over. The writers wanted no single branch to grow too powerful, so they gave each branch a way to push back on the others. When I read about any check, I ask two questions: which branch is doing the checking, and which branch is being checked?

🤝 Let's Try Together: The Great Compromise

Big states wanted seats in Congress based on population. Small states wanted every state to have an equal vote. Roger Sherman of Connecticut helped broker a deal. Let's check it: in the Senate, every state gets two senators, which pleases small states. In the House, seats depend on population, which pleases big states. Each side gave up something, so this was a compromise. It is often called the Great Compromise.

🗳️ Compromises, Rights and Who Could Vote

Delegates from states with many enslaved people wanted them counted to win more House seats, even though enslaved people could not vote. They agreed to the Three-Fifths Clause: every five enslaved people counted as three people for seats and some taxes. This gave enslavers' states extra power. Many people would not support the Constitution without a list of rights, so James Madison wrote amendments, and in 1791 the first ten, the Bill of Rights, became law, protecting freedom of speech, religion and the press. But in 1789, each state set its own voting rules, and mostly white men who owned property could vote.

👁 Watch Me Think

I want to say the Three-Fifths Clause clearly. It did not give enslaved people any rights or votes. It gave extra seats to the states that enslaved them, so enslavers gained power from the people they held in slavery. The Constitution never used the word "slave," but it protected slavery. Then I ask: who wrote this rule, and who paid the cost? The delegates wrote it. Enslaved people paid the cost.

🤝 Let's Try Together: Doing the Three-Fifths Math

Imagine a state with 100,000 free people and 50,000 enslaved people. Under the Three-Fifths Clause, every 5 enslaved people count as 3. So 50,000 enslaved people count as 30,000. The state's total for House seats is 100,000 + 30,000 = 130,000. The 50,000 enslaved people could not vote at all, but their count gave the white voters of that state more seats in Congress. Later amendments changed this: the 13th ended slavery in 1865, and the 14th, in 1868, ended the three-fifths count.

🔍 Source Detective: Benjamin Banneker Writes to Jefferson

In August 1791, Benjamin Banneker, a free Black man from Maryland who taught himself advanced mathematics, wrote a long letter to Thomas Jefferson, then the U.S. Secretary of State. Read two short parts of it.

Primary Source: Benjamin Banneker's letter to Thomas Jefferson, August 19, 1791

Part 1: "Sir, suffer me to recall to your mind that time in which the arms and tyranny of the British Crown were exerted with every powerful effort in order to reduce you to a state of servitude..."

Part 2: "...how pitiable is it to reflect, that although you were so fully convinced of the benevolence of the Father of mankind, and of his equal and impartial distribution of those rights and privileges which he had conferred upon them, that you should at the same time counteract his mercies, in detaining by fraud and violence so numerous a part of my brethren under groaning captivity and cruel oppression..."

Word help: suffer me = allow me; tyranny = cruel, unfair rule; servitude = being forced to serve; pitiable = sad and shameful; benevolence = kindness; impartial = fair to all; counteract his mercies = work against God's kindness; brethren = brothers, his people; captivity = being held prisoner.

Benjamin Banneker to Thomas Jefferson, August 19, 1791. Founders Online, National Archives. Spelling modernized and shortened.

👁 Think Like a Historian

First I check who is writing to whom. Banneker is a free Black man writing to one of the most powerful leaders in the country, a man who wrote "all men are created equal" and still enslaved people. Next I look at his strategy. He reminds Jefferson of a time when Jefferson himself felt threatened with servitude. Writers do this to make a reader see the problem from the other side.

🧑‍🤝‍🧑 People Who Shaped the Constitution

Some people shaped the Constitution from inside the convention room. Others shaped the debate from outside it. Read each card and ask: what did this person do, and what did they leave undone?

James Madison

1751–1836

A Virginia delegate called the "Father of the Constitution." He drafted the plan for three branches, took detailed notes at the convention, and wrote the amendments that became the Bill of Rights. He later became the fourth president. He enslaved people at Montpelier, his plantation, and did not free them in his will.

Benjamin Franklin

1706–1790

A printer, scientist and diplomat who was 81 at the convention, the oldest delegate. Earlier in life he enslaved people, and his newspaper printed ads for selling enslaved people. Late in life he changed: in 1787 he became president of the Pennsylvania Abolition Society, and in 1790 he signed its petition asking Congress to end slavery.

Benjamin Banneker

1731–1806

A free Black man from Maryland who taught himself mathematics and astronomy. In 1791 he sent Thomas Jefferson a handwritten copy of his almanac along with a long letter challenging slavery. Jefferson replied politely and sent the almanac to a scientist in France, but he kept enslaving people.

Mercy Otis Warren

1728–1814

A Massachusetts writer who wrote plays and poems supporting the Patriots. In 1788 she argued that the new Constitution needed a bill of rights to protect the people. In 1805 she published one of the first histories of the American Revolution.

Canassatego

died 1750

An Onondaga leader of the Haudenosaunee. At a 1744 treaty meeting in Lancaster, Pennsylvania, he urged the quarreling colonies to unite as the Six Nations had. Franklin printed his words. Historians still debate how much Haudenosaunee ideas shaped the Constitution.
