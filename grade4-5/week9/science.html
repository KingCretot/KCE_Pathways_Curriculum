🪐 The Solar System and Gravity

Our solar system is the Sun and everything that orbits it: eight planets and their moons, dwarf planets such as Pluto, and many asteroids and comets. The four inner planets, Mercury, Venus, Earth and Mars, are small and rocky; the four outer planets are giants: Jupiter and Saturn are gas giants, and Uranus and Neptune are ice giants. Gravity is a pull between all objects that have mass, and the Sun holds more than 99% of the solar system's mass, so its pull is by far the strongest. Gravity pulls each planet toward the Sun while the planet's forward motion carries it ahead, and together they bend its path into an orbit. Planets far from the Sun feel a weaker pull, move more slowly and travel a much longer path, so their years are longer.

👁 Watch Me Think

Imagine whirling a ball on a string around your head. The string keeps pulling the ball toward your hand, but the ball keeps trying to go straight, so it moves in a circle. What happens if the string snaps? The ball flies off in a straight line. The Sun's gravity is like an invisible string. Without it, a planet would not stop or fall in; it would keep moving in a straight line out into space. Comparing a new idea to something I can test with my hands helps me reason about it.

⭐ Stars, and the Sun Is One of Them

A star is a giant ball of hot, glowing gas that makes its own light and heat, deep in its center. The Sun is a star, a medium-sized one. It looks far bigger and brighter than other stars only because it is much closer: sunlight takes about 8 minutes to reach Earth, but light from the next-nearest star, Proxima Centauri, takes more than 4 years. Distances to stars are so huge that astronomers measure them in light-years. A star's color tells its temperature: blue-white stars are the hottest, red stars are the coolest, and our yellow-white Sun is in between. Planets and moons make no light; they shine by reflecting sunlight.

👁 Watch Me Think

On a clear night some stars look bright and some look dim. Does that mean the dim ones are small? Not necessarily! Picture two identical flashlights: one across the room and one a mile away. The far one looks dim, even though it is just as bright. So when a star looks dim, there are two possible reasons: it might truly give off less light, or it might be very far away. A scientist does not jump to one conclusion when two explanations fit the evidence.

🤝 Let's Try Together: Why Can't We See Stars in the Daytime?

The stars are still in the sky during the day. So why can't we see them? Let's reason it out. At night, the dark sky lets faint starlight stand out. In the daytime, sunlight bounces around in our air and makes the whole sky bright blue. The faint stars are still shining, but the bright sky outshines them, like trying to see a flashlight beam at noon. Evidence: during a total solar eclipse, when the Moon blocks the Sun, the sky darkens and bright stars and planets appear in the middle of the day.

🧭 Reading the Sky: Constellations and Wayfinding

A constellation is a pattern of stars that people have named, and astronomers today divide the sky into 88 official constellations. The stars in a pattern look close together, but they are often very different distances from Earth. Because Earth rotates, the stars appear to wheel across the sky each night, and because Earth revolves around the Sun, we see different constellations in different seasons. Polaris, the North Star, sits almost directly above Earth's North Pole, so it barely moves and always marks north. For thousands of years, people around the world have read the same sky in their own ways, to keep calendars, tell stories and find their way.

👁 Watch Me Think

How could Polynesian navigators sail thousands of miles of open ocean with no compass or map? Let me reason like a navigator. A star rises at the same spot on the horizon every night, and it sets at the same spot too. If I memorize where many stars rise and set, the horizon becomes a compass. As one star climbs too high, I steer by the next one rising in the same place. Then I check my thinking with other evidence: the direction of ocean swells, the wind and birds that fly out to sea and back to land each day.

🤝 Let's Try Together: Find the North Star

On a clear night, face north and find the Big Dipper: four stars make the bowl and three make the handle. Look at the two stars on the outer edge of the bowl, away from the handle. These are the pointer stars. Draw an imaginary line from the bottom pointer star through the top one, and keep going about five times that distance. You reach Polaris, the North Star. It is not the brightest star in the sky, but it stays almost still while the others circle around it. Now you know which way is north.

👁 Think Like a Astronomer

An astronomer reads down each column to find a pattern, such as "the farther out, the longer the year." Then she checks every row to see if any planet breaks the pattern. When one does, she does not ignore it. She asks what else could explain it, and looks for more evidence.

🚀 People Who Reached for the Sky

Mathematicians, astronauts, navigators and astronomers have all helped us understand space and find our way by the stars.

Katherine Johnson

1918–2020

Johnson grew up in West Virginia and was so strong in math that she skipped several grades. In 1953 she joined NACA, the agency that became NASA, as a "human computer" in a segregated group of Black women mathematicians at Langley, Virginia. She calculated the trajectory for Alan Shepard's 1961 flight, the first American in space. In 1962, before John Glenn orbited Earth in Friendship 7, he asked that she check the new electronic computer's numbers by hand. She also helped calculate the path for Apollo 11, the 1969 Moon landing, and received the Presidential Medal of Freedom in 2015.

Mae Jemison

born 1956

Born in Alabama and raised in Chicago, Jemison dreamed of space as a child. She earned a degree in chemical engineering from Stanford University, became a medical doctor, and served as a Peace Corps doctor in Sierra Leone and Liberia. NASA chose her as an astronaut in 1987. In September 1992 she flew on the space shuttle Endeavour, becoming the first African American woman in space. During about eight days in orbit, she ran science experiments, including studies of how the human body responds to weightlessness.

Mau Piailug

1932–2010

Mau Piailug was a master navigator from Satawal, a small island in Micronesia, trained in wayfinding from childhood by his grandfather. In 1976 he guided Hōkūleʻa, a Hawaiian double-hulled voyaging canoe, from Hawaiʻi to Tahiti, more than 2,000 miles, with no compass, maps or instruments. He read the stars, ocean swells, winds and birds. The voyage showed that ancient Polynesians could have crossed the Pacific on purpose. He later taught Hawaiian navigators such as Nainoa Thompson, helping bring traditional wayfinding back to life.

Annie Jump Cannon

1863–1941

Annie Jump Cannon worked at the Harvard College Observatory, studying starlight captured on glass photographic plates. She sorted stars into groups by temperature using the letters O, B, A, F, G, K and M, from hottest to coolest, and astronomers still use her system today. Our Sun is a G star. She classified hundreds of thousands of stars, and she did this work while mostly deaf.
