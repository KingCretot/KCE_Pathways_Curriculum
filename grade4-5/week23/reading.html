🖼️ Picture It: Literal or Figurative?

Literal language means exactly what the words say: “The practice cabin was very hot.” Figurative language uses words in an unusual way to create a picture or a feeling: “The practice cabin was an oven.” The cabin is not really an oven, but now you can feel the heat. Writers use figurative language to show how a character feels and to set the mood, the feeling a text creates in the reader.

Kind and what it doesExample from camp → what it really means
Simile
Compares two unlike things using like or as		“Her bow skipped across the strings like a stone across a pond.” → She played quickly and lightly.
Metaphor
Says one thing is another, with no like or as		“The practice cabin was an oven.” → The cabin was very hot.
Personification
Gives human actions or feelings to something that is not human		“The old piano groaned when Aldo sat down.” → The piano made a low, creaky sound.
Hyperbole
A huge exaggeration that no one is meant to believe		“My fiddle case weighs a ton.” → The case feels very heavy.
Idiom
A common saying whose meaning is different from its words; you have to learn it		“Break a leg tonight!” → Good luck in your show!

💡

Three Figurative Language Questions

Ask: (1) Could this be literally true? If not, it is figurative. (2) What is being compared, exaggerated, or brought to life? (3) What does it show about the character’s feelings or the mood? A simile always has like or as; a metaphor never does.

📖 Text A: The Story

This realistic story is packed with figurative language. As you read, ask: is this literally true? If not, what does it really mean, and how does Adisa feel? Notice the paragraph numbers; you will use them.

Text A • Realistic StoryButterflies in Music City

An original story

1On the last morning of fiddle camp in Nashville, Tennessee, Adisa woke up with a stomach full of butterflies. In six hours she would play her first solo, all alone, on the camp’s small stage beside the Cumberland River. Her fiddle case sat by the door like a patient dog waiting for a walk.

2At breakfast, her bunkmate Aedan slid a plate of pancakes toward her. “Eat something,” he said. “You look as pale as a sheet of paper.” Adisa pushed the plate away. “I’ve practiced this song a million times,” she groaned, “and my fingers still forget the middle part.”

3In the practice cabin, the air was an oven, and the old wooden floor creaked complaints under every step. Adisa lifted her bow. The first notes came out thin and shaky, like a kitten mewing in the rain. She stopped and stared at the strings as if they had betrayed her.

4Her teacher, Mr. Aldo, leaned in the doorway. “You’re trying to play every note perfectly,” he said. “Stop counting and start telling the story. Music is a conversation, not a test.” Then he winked. “And don’t worry. Everybody gets cold feet before a show.”

5That evening, the sun melted into the river, and the stage lights blinked awake one by one. When Adisa’s name was called, her knees were made of jelly. She walked to the microphone, closed her eyes, and pictured her grandmother humming this same tune on the porch back home.

6Her bow began to dance. The melody climbed, swooped, and soared over the crowd like a bird that had finally found the sky. When the last note faded, the audience roared to its feet. Adisa grinned so wide that her face nearly split in two. She was still flying high when Aedan wrapped her in a hug.

🎙️ Text B: The Poem

In this poem, an old Nashville concert hall is the main character. The speaker is the voice talking in the poem. Watch for personification and metaphors, and notice how the mood changes by the last stanza.

Text B • PoemWhat the Old Hall Hears

An original poem

1When all the crowds have wandered home
and every seat is bare,
the old hall stretches wooden arms
and breathes the midnight air.

2Its pews are pockets full of songs
from summers long ago;
its windows are a hundred eyes
that watched each nervous show.

3The stage remembers trembling hands,
a singer’s shaky start,
and how one brave and honest note
can calm a racing heart.

4So when it’s your turn in the lights,
don’t fear the waiting crowd.
The hall has heard a million songs
and still wants yours, sung loud.

Authors do not use figurative language just to sound fancy. In Text A, it shows Adisa’s feelings changing, and it sets the mood of each scene. In Text B, personification turns a building into a wise, encouraging character.

📝 Explain It Like a Pro

🪜

Four Steps to Explain Figurative Language

  1. Name it: simile, metaphor, personification, hyperbole, or idiom.
  2. Say what it literally says: what would happen if the words were true?
  3. Say what it really means in plain words.
  4. Say what it shows: a character’s feelings, or the mood of the scene.
🧪

Worked Example: “Adisa grinned so wide that her face nearly split in two.”

❌ Weak: This is a simile and it is funny.
It names the wrong kind (there is no like or as) and never says what the line means or shows.

✅ Strong: This is hyperbole. Her face did not really split, but the exaggeration shows that she is smiling as big as she can because she is thrilled and proud.
It names the kind, gives the real meaning, and explains what it shows about Adisa.

🎯

Word Choice and Connotation

Words with almost the same meaning can carry different feelings. This is called connotation. “The audience roared” feels loud and thrilled; “the audience clapped” is calmer. When you explain word choice, tell what feeling the exact word adds.
