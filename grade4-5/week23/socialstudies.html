📉 The Crash, the Banks and the Dust Bowl

On October 29, 1929, a day later called Black Tuesday, prices on the New York stock market crashed. People who had borrowed money to buy stocks lost fortunes. Over the next few years, businesses closed, and about 9,000 banks failed between 1930 and 1933, wiping out many families' savings. By 1933, about one out of every four workers had no job. Families who lost their homes built shacks from scrap wood and tin in camps they called Hoovervilles, after President Herbert Hoover. Then nature struck the Great Plains. Farmers had plowed up millions of acres of native grassland to grow wheat, and when a long drought began in 1931, the bare soil blew away in giant dust storms. The worst, on Black Sunday, April 14, 1935, turned day into night. Roughly 2.5 million people left the Plains states during the 1930s, and many traveled west to California looking for farm work.

👁 Watch Me Think

I notice two kinds of causes here. Some were human choices: borrowing money to buy stocks, and plowing up the grass whose roots held the soil in place. One was nature: the drought. Historians call this multiple causes. A disaster rarely has just one cause. I also notice the name Hooverville. People named the camps after the president to blame him. A name can be a kind of argument, so I ask: was it fair to blame one person for the whole Depression?

🤝 Let's Try Together: Building a Dust Bowl Cause-and-Effect Chain

Elsbeth, a fifth grader in Kansas, is building a cause-and-effect chain for a history fair. Let's help her. Cause: farmers plowed up the deep-rooted prairie grass to plant wheat. Cause: the rain stopped in 1931. Effect: the dry, loose soil blew away in storms. Effect: crops died, and many families lost their farms. Effect: roughly 2.5 million people moved away. Notice that each effect can become the next cause. On April 27, 1935, less than two weeks after Black Sunday, the government created the Soil Conservation Service to teach farmers ways to plow and plant that hold the soil in place. Let's weigh it: the dust storms were partly a natural disaster and partly a human-made one.

🦅 Franklin and Eleanor Roosevelt and the New Deal

In 1932, voters elected Franklin D. Roosevelt, often called FDR, as president. He took office on March 4, 1933, and told the nation, "the only thing we have to fear is fear itself." Eight days later he gave the first of his radio talks, called fireside chats, to explain the banking crisis in plain words. Polio had paralyzed FDR's legs in 1921, and he used a wheelchair in private. He promised a New Deal: new government programs to bring relief, recovery and reform. Congress created the Civilian Conservation Corps (CCC), which hired young men ages 18 to 25 to plant trees, fight forest fires and build trails in parks. They earned $30 a month, and most of it, usually $25, was sent home to their families. The Works Progress Administration (WPA), started in 1935, hired more than 8.5 million people to build roads, bridges and schools, and even paid artists and writers. Frances Perkins, the first woman in a president's Cabinet, helped plan the Social Security Act of 1935, which created payments for older Americans.

👁 Watch Me Think

I notice that FDR did not work alone. Eleanor Roosevelt made the first lady's job bigger than ever. She traveled the country to see the hard times for herself and reported back, and at the end of 1935 she began a newspaper column called "My Day." In 1939, the Daughters of the American Revolution would not let Marian Anderson, a famous Black singer, perform in their hall in Washington, D.C. Eleanor Roosevelt quit the group in protest, and on April 9, 1939, Anderson sang on the steps of the Lincoln Memorial for about 75,000 people. So I ask: how can one person use her position to speak up?

🤝 Let's Try Together: Following a CCC Paycheck

Let's follow the money the way a CCC family would. A young man earned $30 a month. If $25 went home, he kept $5 for himself. That money paid for food and rent for his parents, brothers and sisters. More than 2.5 million young men served in the CCC from 1933 to 1942. Close to home, CCC workers built the trails, bridges, cabins and pavilions of Myakka River State Park near Sarasota, Florida, from 1934 until it opened in 1941. Now let's ask who was missing. The CCC hired only men. Black young men served in segregated camps, and the director limited Black enrollment to about 10 percent. More than 80,000 Native Americans worked in a special Indian Division, mostly on their own reservations. Let's weigh it: the CCC helped millions of families, but it did not treat everyone the same.

⚖️ Mary McLeod Bethune, the Mexican Repatriation and Who Was Left Out

Mary McLeod Bethune was born in 1875 in Mayesville, South Carolina, one of 17 children of parents who had been enslaved. In 1904, with just $1.50, she opened a school for Black girls in Daytona Beach, Florida. It grew into Bethune-Cookman University. In 1935 she founded the National Council of Negro Women, and in 1936 President Roosevelt named her director of the Division of Negro Affairs of the National Youth Administration, the highest government job an African American woman had held at that time. She was a leader of a group of Black advisers people called the Black Cabinet, and she pushed the government to include Black Americans in New Deal programs. At the same time, Mexican Americans faced a hard truth. During the 1930s, local, state and federal officials pressured or forced people of Mexican descent to leave the United States in what is called the Mexican Repatriation. Estimates range from about 400,000 to as many as 2 million people, and many of them, perhaps more than half, were U.S. citizens, including children born here.

👁 Watch Me Think

Now I look for who was left out, the way we did with the 19th Amendment. The Social Security Act did not cover farm workers or people who cooked, cleaned or cared for children in private homes. Many Black, Mexican American and Asian American workers did exactly those jobs, so many of them were left out. Some local officials also paid Black workers less or turned them away from relief. So I ask: a program can look fair on paper, but who could actually use it?

🤝 Let's Try Together: One Girl's Story: Emilia Castañeda

Emilia Castañeda was born in Los Angeles in 1926, so she was a U.S. citizen. During the Depression, when she was about nine, Los Angeles County paid for her family to travel by train to Mexico. Officials told many families on relief that they would be better off in Mexico. There, Emilia's family stayed with relatives and sometimes had to sleep outdoors. Emilia came back to the United States when she was 17, after losing much of her English. Her father never returned. Let's weigh it: officials said they were saving relief money, but they sent away American children like Emilia. In 2005, California passed a law apologizing for the state's part in the Mexican Repatriation. How do we know Emilia's story? Partly from Emilia herself, who later told it in interviews.

🔍 Source Detective: A Speech, a Law and a Photograph Caption

Here are three short primary sources from the 1930s: words from a president's speech, part of a law, and a caption written for a famous photograph. Read each part, then compare them.

Primary Sources: 1933, 1935 and 1936

Part 1 (Franklin D. Roosevelt, first inaugural address, March 4, 1933): "This great Nation will endure as it has endured, will revive and will prosper." Later he said, "Our greatest primary task is to put people to work."

Part 2 (Social Security Act, August 14, 1935): "The term 'employment' means any service, of whatever nature, performed within the United States by an employee for his employer, except— (1) Agricultural labor; (2) Domestic service in a private home;"

Part 3 (Dorothea Lange, photograph caption, Nipomo, California, March 1936): "Destitute pea pickers in California. Mother of seven children. Age thirty-two." This photograph became famous as "Migrant Mother." The mother was Florence Owens Thompson, a Native American woman born in Indian Territory, now Oklahoma.

Word help: endure = last through hard times; revive = come back to life; prosper = do well; primary = most important; employment = paid work; agricultural labor = farm work; domestic service = cooking, cleaning or caring for children in someone else's home; destitute = without money or food; pea pickers = farm workers who harvest peas.

Part 1: Franklin D. Roosevelt, First Inaugural Address, March 4, 1933 (text from The American Presidency Project, University of California, Santa Barbara). Part 2: Social Security Act, Title II, Section 210(b), approved August 14, 1935. Part 3: Dorothea Lange, Resettlement Administration, caption for "Migrant Mother," Nipomo, California, March 1936 (Library of Congress). Real wording; short parts.

👁 Think Like a Historian

First I check who made each source and why. A president giving a speech wants to calm a scared nation. A law is written by Congress, and it decides who gets help. A government photographer's caption was meant to show the public why families needed help. Then I look for gaps. Years later, Florence Owens Thompson said she had been told the photographs would not be published, and some captions about her family were not exact. A primary source is strong evidence, but it can still be incomplete. Whose voice is missing from the caption?

🌾 People of the New Deal Years

Some led the government, some pushed it to do better, and some lived through its gaps. Read each card and ask: what power did this person have, and how did they use it?

Franklin D. Roosevelt

1882–1945

Born in Hyde Park, New York, he was elected president four times, more than any other president. Polio paralyzed his legs in 1921, but he kept working in politics. He launched the New Deal and spoke to Americans by radio in his fireside chats. Told honestly: to pass his laws, he needed votes from Southern lawmakers who supported segregation, and many programs treated Black Americans unfairly.

Eleanor Roosevelt

1884–1962

Born in New York City, she turned the first lady's role into a job of action. She traveled the country, wrote her "My Day" newspaper column starting at the end of 1935, and pushed for fair treatment of Black Americans. In 1939 she quit the Daughters of the American Revolution after it refused to let Marian Anderson sing in its hall.

Mary McLeod Bethune

1875–1955

Born in Mayesville, South Carolina, to parents who had been enslaved, she founded a school in Daytona Beach, Florida, in 1904 with $1.50. She founded the National Council of Negro Women in 1935 and led the National Youth Administration's Division of Negro Affairs. In 2022, Florida chose her for National Statuary Hall in the U.S. Capitol, the first Black American a state has chosen for the collection.

Frances Perkins

Secretary of Labor, 1933–1945

She was the first woman to serve in a president's Cabinet. As Secretary of Labor for more than 12 years, longer than anyone else, she was a main planner of the Social Security Act and fought for a minimum wage and limits on working hours.

Emilia Castañeda

Born 1926 in Los Angeles

A U.S. citizen by birth, she was sent to Mexico with her family when she was about nine, during the Mexican Repatriation. She came back to the United States at 17. Many years later she shared her story in interviews, helping Americans learn about the Mexican Repatriation.
