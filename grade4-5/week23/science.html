🍄 Meet the Decomposer Team

When a leaf falls or an animal dies, its body is still made of matter. Living things called decomposers break that dead matter down. The two main groups are fungi and bacteria. Fungi are not plants. They cannot make their own food from sunlight. Instead, a fungus grows a web of tiny threads called hyphae through a log or the soil. The threads give off chemicals called enzymes that break the dead material down outside the fungus, and then the threads soak up the food. The mushroom you see is only the fruiting body, the part that makes spores. Bacteria are single cells, far too small to see without a microscope, and huge numbers of them live in soil. Worms, millipedes, snails and pill bugs help, too. They eat bits of dead leaves and wood and break them into smaller pieces, so scientists sometimes call them detritivores, from the word detritus, which means bits of dead matter. A worm's droppings, called castings, are full of nutrients. Smaller pieces have more surface for fungi and bacteria to work on, so the whole team breaks matter down faster together.

👁 Watch Me Think

On a hike, Chioma finds an old fallen log. The wood is soft and crumbly, and shelf-shaped fungi grow on its side. When she gently rolls it with her grown-up, she sees white threads, pill bugs and a millipede. Here is how I think about it. The shelf fungi are only the fruiting bodies. The white threads are hyphae, and they run all through the wood, digesting it. The pill bugs and millipede chew the soft wood into smaller bits, which gives the fungi and bacteria more surface to work on. Over years, the log will shrink until it is gone. But where does its matter go? That is the question for our next card.

🤝 Let's Try Together: Sort the Team

Let's sort some living things together. White mold growing on an old strawberry: that is a fungus, a decomposer, because its threads digest the strawberry. An earthworm eating dead leaves: that is a detritivore, because it eats and shreds dead matter. Bacteria in the soil under a fallen leaf: decomposers. A mushroom on a stump: the fruiting body of a fungus. Notice that none of them can make food from sunlight the way plants can. Try It at Home, with a grown-up: wear gloves and gently lift a log, a flat rock or a pile of old leaves outdoors. Look for white fungus threads, worms, pill bugs and millipedes, then put everything back the way you found it. In Florida, watch for fire ants, spiders and snakes, and let your grown-up lift first. Never touch or taste wild mushrooms, because some are poisonous. Wash your hands with soap and water when you are done.

♻️ The Cycle of Matter

Matter is anything that takes up space and has mass. Living things are built from matter, and Earth does not get a new supply, so the same matter must be used again and again. Here is how. Plants take in carbon dioxide from the air and water from the soil, and they use energy from the Sun to build sugar, which they use to grow. Most of a plant's mass comes from that air and water, not from the soil. Animals eat plants, or eat animals that ate plants, and use that matter to grow. When plants and animals die, or drop leaves and wastes, decomposers break the matter down. As decomposers use the food, they give off carbon dioxide into the air, the same gas plants need. They also leave nutrients, such as nitrogen and phosphorus, in the soil, where plant roots can take them up again. That is the cycle of matter: from air, water and soil to plants, to animals, to decomposers, and back again. Energy is different. Energy comes from the Sun and flows through the food web, and at each step much of it leaves as heat. So scientists say: matter cycles, but energy flows. In Week 21 you learned that mass is conserved in a chemical change. The same is true here. When a log rots, its matter is not destroyed. It moves into the air, the soil and the bodies of the decomposers.

👁 Watch Me Think

Calixto asks a great question: "Trees drop leaves every fall. Why isn't the forest floor buried under a mountain of old leaves?" Here is how I think it through. Each year new leaves fall, but decomposers break the older ones down over months and years. Part of the leaf matter goes into the air as carbon dioxide, part becomes nutrients in the soil, and part becomes the bodies of fungi and bacteria. The trees then take up those nutrients and carbon dioxide to build new leaves. So the leaves do not pile up forever. Now imagine a forest with no decomposers. Dead matter would pile higher every year, and the nutrients would stay locked inside it, so new plants would run short.

🤝 Let's Try Together: Follow One Bit of Carbon

Let's follow one tiny bit of carbon together. It starts in the air as part of the gas carbon dioxide. A clover plant takes it in and uses the Sun's energy to build it into sugar, so now the carbon is part of a clover leaf. A rabbit eats the clover, and the carbon becomes part of the rabbit. The rabbit leaves droppings on the ground. Bacteria and fungi break the droppings down, use them for food and give off carbon dioxide. Our carbon is back in the air, ready for another plant. Which step needed decomposers? Step three, where the droppings were broken down. Without decomposers, that carbon would stay stuck in the droppings.

🪱 Composting: Decomposers at Work

Composting is a way people put decomposers to work on purpose. A compost pile or bin turns food scraps and yard waste into compost, a dark, crumbly material that feeds garden soil. The U.S. Environmental Protection Agency says all composting needs three basic ingredients. Browns are materials such as dead leaves, branches and twigs, and they provide carbon. Greens are materials such as grass clippings, vegetable waste, fruit scraps and coffee grounds, and they provide nitrogen. Water provides moisture. The pile should be damp, like a wrung-out sponge. It also needs air, because aerobic bacteria, the most important decomposers in a compost pile, need oxygen. A bad smell usually means the pile needs more air, so people turn it with a garden fork. In a busy pile, the decomposers work so hard that the center can heat up to about 130 to 160 degrees Fahrenheit. Some things should stay out: meat, fish, dairy products and oils cause odors and attract pests, and pet wastes can carry germs that make people sick. A worm bin is another kind of composting. It uses red wigglers, a kind of earthworm that lives near the surface, to turn fruit and vegetable scraps into worm castings.

👁 Watch Me Think

Chioma's family started a compost bin, but after two weeks it is soggy, slimy and smells bad. Let me think like a compost scientist. A bad smell usually means not enough air. Soggy and slimy means too much water and probably too many greens, like fruit scraps and grass. So the fix is to add browns, such as dry leaves or shredded cardboard, to soak up water and add carbon, and then turn the pile with a garden fork to let air in. Now think about the opposite problem. If a pile is dry and stays cool, the decomposers need more water and more greens for nitrogen.

🤝 Let's Try Together: Build a Mini Compost Jar

Let's plan a mini compost jar together. Try It at Home, with a grown-up's help: use a clear plastic jar, or a plastic bottle that a grown-up cuts open for you. Add a layer of garden soil, then a layer of dry leaves or torn paper, then a few small fruit or vegetable scraps, such as banana peel or apple core. No meat, dairy or oils. Sprinkle water until everything is damp, not dripping. Cover the top with a cloth held by a rubber band so air can get in, and keep the jar out of direct sun. Draw what you see once a week for a month. Wear gloves when you handle soil or compost, do not eat or touch your face while you work, and wash your hands with soap and water afterward. If you have mold allergies or asthma, just watch and let a grown-up do the handling. When you finish, a grown-up can add the jar to a garden or outdoor compost pile.

📊 Data Lab: What Could Decomposers Handle?

How much of what Americans throw away could decomposers break down? The U.S. Environmental Protection Agency, or EPA, measures the trash, called municipal solid waste, that homes, schools and businesses throw away. Read the real numbers for 2018.

Data Table: What Americans Threw Away in 2018 (percent of all waste, by weight)

MaterialPercent of all wasteCan decomposers break it down?
Paper and paperboard23.1%Yes, slowly
Food21.6%Yes, quickly
Plastics12.2%Not in a compost pile
Yard trimmings (leaves, grass, branches)12.1%Yes

Source: U.S. Environmental Protection Agency, National Overview: Facts and Figures on Materials, Wastes and Recycling (2018 data). In 2018, Americans made 292.4 million tons of this waste, about 4.9 pounds per person each day, and composted about 25 million tons. The "all other" row is 100 minus the four rows above.

👁 Think Like a Soil Scientist

A soil scientist reads a table by asking what each number means. Each percent tells how much of all the waste, by weight, was that material. Food and yard trimmings are exactly what decomposers in a compost pile love. Paper can break down too, but more slowly. Plastic is made to last, so decomposers in a compost pile cannot break it down. EPA says food scraps and yard waste together make up more than 30 percent of what we throw away, and that putting them in landfills takes up space and releases methane, a strong greenhouse gas. Composting them puts decomposers to work instead.

🔬 Scientists Who Study Life in the Soil

From a famous book about worms to Antarctica's frozen valleys, these scientists showed how much the tiny living things in soil matter.

Charles Darwin

1809–1882

Charles Darwin was an English naturalist, famous for his ideas about how living things change over time. His last scientific book, published in 1881, was all about earthworms. He showed that worms swallow soil, bring it up to the surface as castings, and slowly bury stones and old objects over many years. He concluded that worms have shaped the land far more than most people would expect. The book sold thousands of copies in its first few weeks.

Selman Waksman

1888–1973

Selman Waksman was born in the Russian Empire, in a village that is now in Ukraine, and came to the United States to study at Rutgers University in New Jersey. He spent his career studying microbes in soil, and his lab tested thousands of soil samples. He is credited with coining the word "antibiotic." His team discovered streptomycin, made by a soil bacterium, the first antibiotic that worked against the disease tuberculosis. He won the Nobel Prize in 1952.

Diana Wall

1943–2024

Diana Wall was a soil ecologist at Colorado State University. She studied nematodes, tiny roundworms that live in soil, and how soil life affects carbon and the health of ecosystems. Starting in 1989, she led research in the cold, dry valleys of Antarctica, one of the harshest places on Earth for life. A valley in Antarctica, Wall Valley, is named for her, and in 2013 she won the Tyler Prize for Environmental Achievement.

Rattan Lal

soil scientist

Rattan Lal grew up on a small farm in India and became a Distinguished University Professor of Soil Science at Ohio State University. For five decades, on four continents, he has shown farmers how to build healthy soil by not plowing it and by leaving crop remains on the ground after harvest, which helps build carbon in the soil. In 2020 he won the World Food Prize for this soil-centered way of growing more food.
