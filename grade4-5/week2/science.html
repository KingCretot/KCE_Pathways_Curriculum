🌟 Word Wrangler

Tap each card to flip it. Tap the 🔊 to hear the word and what it means.

ecosystem
All the living and nonliving things in an area, interacting together. Example: a pond with its fish, plants, water and rocks.
producer
A living thing that makes its own food using energy from sunlight. Example: grass, trees, algae.
consumer
A living thing that gets energy by eating other living things. Example: rabbits, owls, people.
decomposer
A living thing that breaks down dead plants and animals and returns nutrients to the soil. Example: mushrooms and bacteria.
food chain
One path that energy takes from living thing to living thing. Example: grass → rabbit → fox.
food web
Many food chains that connect and overlap in one ecosystem.
population
All the members of one kind of living thing in an area. Example: all the rabbits in a meadow.

☀️ Energy Starts with the Sun

An ecosystem is all the living and nonliving things in an area interacting together, like a pond, a forest or a coral reef. Almost all the energy in an ecosystem starts as sunlight. Producers, such as plants and algae, use sunlight to make their own food through photosynthesis. Consumers cannot make their own food, so they get energy by eating other living things. Herbivores eat plants, carnivores eat animals, and omnivores eat both.

👁 Watch Me Think

To classify a living thing, I ask: Can it make its own food from sunlight? A maple tree can, so it is a producer. A deer cannot; it eats plants, so it is a consumer, an herbivore. A red fox eats rabbits and also berries, so it is a consumer, an omnivore. For every living thing, I ask where its energy came from.

Only the algae are producers, and every consumer’s energy traces back to them, and to the Sun.

🕸️ Chains, Webs and Decomposers

A food chain shows one path energy takes, with arrows pointing from the living thing that is eaten to the one that eats it: grass → rabbit → fox. Real ecosystems have many chains that cross and connect, forming a food web. Decomposers, such as fungi and bacteria, break down dead plants and animals and return nutrients to the soil, so producers can use them again. Only a small part of the energy, about one-tenth, passes on to the next link; the rest is used for living or given off as heat.

👁 Watch Me Think

Arrows confuse many people, so here is my trick: the arrow means “gives energy to.” In grass → rabbit, the grass gives energy to the rabbit. If the arrow pointed rabbit → grass, that would mean the grass eats the rabbit, which makes no sense. Next I look for an animal with two or more arrows pointing in. That animal eats more than one thing, which is why a web is more realistic than a single chain.

🧩 Picture It: A Small Forest Food Web

  • Acorns → mice, deer
  • Grass → rabbits, deer
  • Mice → owls, foxes, snakes
  • Rabbits → foxes, owls
  • Snakes → owls
  • When any of them die, fungi and bacteria break them down.

🤝 Let's Try Together: Chain or Web?

Chain: acorns → mouse → snake → owl. That is 4 links.
Now look at the web. The owl also eats mice and rabbits directly. So if snakes became rare, would the owl starve? Probably not, because it could eat more mice and rabbits.
Webs show the backup paths that a single chain hides.

⚖️ Balance and Change

A population is all the members of one kind of living thing in an area, such as all the rabbits in a meadow. In a healthy ecosystem, populations rise and fall but stay in rough balance. When one population changes a lot, the change can ripple through the whole food web. For example, if a disease wipes out many rabbits, foxes have less food and their numbers may drop, while the grass may grow thicker. Scientists study these ripples by counting populations year after year.

👁 Watch Me Think

When one population changes, I follow the arrows both ways. Say the mice disappear. Up the web: owls and snakes that eat mice lose food, so their numbers may fall, or they may eat more of something else. Down the web: the acorns and seeds that mice ate are left alone, so more young oak trees might sprout. I say “may,” because a real web has backup paths, and I need data to know for sure.

🧩 Picture It: A Real Example: Wolves in Yellowstone

People, including park rangers, killed gray wolves in Yellowstone National Park until none were left by the late 1920s. In 1995 and 1996, scientists brought wolves from Canada back to the park. Over the next years, elk numbers fell, and in some places willow and aspen trees along streams grew back. Scientists still debate how much of this the wolves caused, because drought, hunting by people and bears changed too. That is why they keep collecting data.

🤝 Let's Try Together: Predict the Ripple

Pond food chain: algae → tadpoles → small fish → heron. Suppose a chemical kills most of the small fish.
Step 1: Who ate the small fish? Herons. They lose food, so the heron population may fall or move away.
Step 2: What did the small fish eat? Tadpoles. With fewer predators, the tadpole population may grow.
Step 3: More tadpoles eat more algae, so the algae may decrease.
One change rippled through three other populations.

🌎 Scientists Who Protect Ecosystems

These scientists studied how living things depend on each other, and they used evidence to protect ecosystems.

Rachel Carson

1907–1964

Carson was a marine biologist and writer who worked for the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service. In her 1962 book Silent Spring, she gathered evidence that pesticides like DDT, sprayed to kill insects, were also harming birds, fish and other wildlife as the poisons moved through food webs. Chemical companies attacked her work, but she spoke to Congress and stood by her evidence. Her book helped lead to the U.S. ban on most uses of DDT in 1972.

Robin Wall Kimmerer

born 1953

Kimmerer is a botanist (a plant scientist) and a member of the Citizen Potawatomi Nation. She studies mosses and other plants, and she shows how Indigenous knowledge, built over many generations, and Western science can work together to care for the land. Her book Braiding Sweetgrass shares these ideas.

Wangari Maathai

1940–2011

Maathai was a Kenyan scientist and the first woman in East and Central Africa to earn a doctorate. She saw that cutting down forests was drying up streams and hurting farms. In 1977 she started the Green Belt Movement, which helped women plant tens of millions of trees. In 2004 she became the first African woman to win the Nobel Peace Prize.
