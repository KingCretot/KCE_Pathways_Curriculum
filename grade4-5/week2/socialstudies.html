Nation

Confederacy
A group of nations that join together to make decisions and protect each other, while each keeps its own government.
Mound
A large hill built by people out of earth, often for homes of leaders, ceremonies, or burials.
Cliff dwelling
A home built of stone under an overhanging cliff, like the ones at Mesa Verde.
Trade network
Many paths and people connected by trading goods, so items travel far from where they began.
Oral tradition
Knowledge, laws, and history that a people pass down by speaking, from elders to young people.
Tree-ring dating
Finding the age of wood by matching its pattern of growth rings to a known timeline.

⛰️ Cahokia: A Great City on the Mississippi

Before 1492, millions of people lived in North America, in hundreds of nations that spoke hundreds of languages. The largest city north of Mexico was Cahokia, built by Mississippian people near present-day St. Louis. At its height, from about 1050 to 1200, it may have held 10,000 to 20,000 people. Its builders raised more than 100 earthen mounds. The largest, Monks Mound, is about 100 feet tall and was built by hand, one basket of soil at a time.

👁 Watch Me Think

How do we know about Cahokia if its people left no books? Step 1: The mounds are still standing, so I can measure them. Step 2: Archaeologists found house floors, tools, and corn, which show that many people lived and farmed there. Step 3: They found rings of post holes, called Woodhenge, that lined up with the sunrise on special days. Step 4: I also notice who is missing: we do not know what Cahokia's people called their own city.

🧩 Picture It: Map Description

Picture the Mississippi River running north to south. On its east bank, in present-day Illinois, a huge flat Grand Plaza lies in the middle of the city. At the north end of the plaza rises Monks Mound, with about 100 feet of earth stacked in giant steps. Smaller mounds and thousands of houses spread out around it. Across the river is where St. Louis stands today.

🤝 Let's Try Together: Whose Name Is It?

The name Cahokia comes from a nation that lived nearby in the 1600s, long after the city's builders. The name Monks Mound comes from French monks who lived near it in the early 1800s. So both names were given by later people! What does this tell us? The builders' own words for their city are missing from the record.

🏜️ Ancestral Puebloan Towns

In the Southwest, Ancestral Puebloans built towns of stone in a dry land. At Chaco Canyon in New Mexico, between about 850 and 1150, they built great houses. The biggest, Pueblo Bonito, had hundreds of rooms and stood at least four stories tall. Later, at Mesa Verde in Colorado, families built cliff dwellings under rock overhangs. Their descendants include today's Hopi, Zuni, and other Pueblo nations.

👁 Watch Me Think

How do we know when these towns were built? Step 1: The roof beams were made from trees. Step 2: Trees grow one ring each year, thick in wet years and thin in dry years. Step 3: Scientists match the ring pattern in a beam to a known timeline. Step 4: That tells the year the tree was cut. This is tree-ring dating, and it gives very exact dates.

🤝 Let's Try Together: Choosing the Right Name

Some older books call these people the “Anasazi.” That word comes from the Navajo language, not from the people's own descendants. Many Pueblo people today prefer Ancestral Puebloans, which links the builders to their living families. When we choose names, we ask: Whose word is this?

🌲 The Haudenosaunee and the Great Law of Peace

In what is now New York, five nations once fought each other: the Mohawk, Oneida, Onondaga, Cayuga, and Seneca. Haudenosaunee oral tradition tells that the Peacemaker and Hiawatha brought a message of peace. The nations joined in a confederacy under the Great Law of Peace, with a Grand Council of 50 chiefs. Clan mothers chose the chiefs and could remove them. In the early 1700s, the Tuscarora joined, making six nations.

👁 Watch Me Think

When did this happen? Step 1: The Great Law was kept by oral tradition and wampum, not by written dates. Step 2: Scholars use different clues and disagree, giving dates from about the 1100s to the 1500s. Step 3: Most estimates place it before Europeans began coming regularly to Haudenosaunee lands in the early 1600s. Step 4: So the honest answer is a range of dates, and we keep listening to Haudenosaunee keepers and the evidence.

🧩 Picture It: The Tree of Peace

The Peacemaker planted a Great White Pine. Its four white roots spread in four directions so other nations could follow them to peace. Weapons were buried under the tree. An eagle sits on top, watching for danger. The Hiawatha Belt, a wampum belt, shows the nations joined together, with a tree or heart in the center for the Onondaga, who keep the council fire.

🤝 Let's Try Together: Whose Story Is It?

In 1855, a poet named Henry Wadsworth Longfellow wrote The Song of Hiawatha. But his poem mixed up the Haudenosaunee leader with stories from a different nation, the Ojibwe. So which source tells the Haudenosaunee story best? The Haudenosaunee's own oral tradition and wampum, kept by their own people.

👁 Think Like a Archaeologist

An archaeologist asks: Where was this found? Where could it have come from? A seashell in Illinois did not walk there. If materials from far away show up again and again, people must have been trading along rivers and trails. Many finds from many places corroborate a trade network.

🌟 Builders and Peacemakers

Some of these people are known by name through oral tradition. Others, like Cahokia's builders, are known through what they built. All of them shaped North America long before 1492.

The Peacemaker (Deganawida)

Before European contact (date debated)

In Haudenosaunee tradition, he came from the Wendat (Huron) people and carried a message of peace to five warring nations. Out of respect, many Haudenosaunee people simply call him the Peacemaker. He planted the Tree of Peace and gave the Great Law.

Hiawatha (Ayenwatha)

Before European contact (date debated)

An Onondaga man in Haudenosaunee tradition who was deep in grief after losing his family. The Peacemaker comforted him with strings of wampum. Hiawatha became his partner and spoke for peace to the nations.

Jigonsaseh

Before European contact (date debated)

In Haudenosaunee tradition, she was the first person to accept the Peacemaker's message. She is honored as the Mother of Nations, and her example is linked to the power of clan mothers.

Cahokia's Builders

About 1050–1200 (height of the city)

Thousands of Mississippian farmers, engineers, and workers built a city of plazas, houses, and more than 100 mounds. They tracked the sun with circles of posts. Their names are not recorded, but their work still stands.

Ancestral Puebloan Builders

About 850–1300 (Chaco and Mesa Verde)

Stonemasons and farmers built great houses at Chaco Canyon, long straight roads, and cliff dwellings at Mesa Verde. They grew corn in a dry land. Their descendants are the Hopi, Zuni, and other Pueblo nations today.
