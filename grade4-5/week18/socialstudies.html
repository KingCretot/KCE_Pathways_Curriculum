🌟 Word Wrangler

Tap each card to flip it. Tap the 🔊 to hear the word and what it means.

sovereignty
A nation's right to govern itself. Indigenous nations signed treaties with the United States as sovereign nations.
treaty
A formal agreement between nations. The 1868 Fort Laramie Treaty set aside the Black Hills for the Lakota.
reservation
Land set aside for an Indigenous nation, usually much smaller than its homeland.
allotment
Cutting shared reservation land into small plots for single families. The Dawes Act of 1887 did this.
assimilation
Forcing or pressuring a group to give up its own culture and take on another. Boarding schools tried to force it.
boarding school
A school where students live away from home. Many Native children were sent far from their families to these schools.
Ghost Dance
A religious movement that spread among many Plains nations by 1890. Dancers prayed for a renewed world and the return of their ancestors.

🏔️ The Black Hills and the Little Bighorn

In the Fort Laramie Treaty of 1868, the United States agreed to a large reservation for the Lakota in the western half of today's South Dakota, including the Black Hills, which the Lakota call Paha Sapa and hold sacred. The treaty said no land could be given up unless three-fourths of the adult Lakota men agreed. In 1874, an army expedition led by George Armstrong Custer found gold in the Black Hills, and miners rushed in, breaking the treaty. The government ordered all Lakota onto the reservation by January 31, 1876. Bands led by Sitting Bull (Hunkpapa Lakota) and Crazy Horse (Oglala Lakota) refused. On June 25 and 26, 1876, Lakota, Northern Cheyenne and Arapaho warriors defeated Custer's 7th Cavalry at the Little Bighorn River in what is now Montana.

👁 Watch Me Think

I ask: who attacked whom? The soldiers rode toward the village. Historians estimate it held about 8,000 people, and its warriors defended their families. I also notice that Crow and Arikara scouts rode with Custer. Indigenous nations were not one group on one side; each nation made its own choices to protect its people. So I name the nation instead of saying "the Indians."

🤝 Let's Try Together: Who Tells the Story?

For many years, the battlefield was called Custer Battlefield National Monument, and paintings of "Custer's Last Stand" showed the battle from the soldiers' side. The Lakota and Cheyenne side was kept mostly in oral tradition. In 1991, Congress changed the name to Little Bighorn Battlefield National Monument and ordered an Indian Memorial, which was dedicated in 2003. It honors the Lakota, Cheyenne, Arapaho, Crow and Arikara people who fought there, under the theme "Peace Through Unity." Let's weigh it: a name on a map is evidence of whose story is being told. Changing the name added voices that had been left out.

🐎 The Nez Perce Journey, 1877

Remember Week 11? In 1805, the Nez Perce, who call themselves the Nimiipuu, fed the starving Lewis and Clark expedition. By the 1860s, settlers and gold miners wanted Nez Perce land. In 1863, some Nez Perce leaders were pressured into signing a treaty that cut their reservation by about 90 percent and left out the Wallowa Valley in Oregon, home of Chief Joseph's band. The bands that never signed were called "non-treaty" bands. In 1877, the army ordered them onto the small reservation in Idaho. After fighting broke out, about 800 Nez Perce, including children and elders, fled. Led by several chiefs, including Joseph, Looking Glass and White Bird, they traveled 126 days and more than 1,170 miles across four of today's states, hoping to reach safety in Canada.

👁 Watch Me Think

I compare 1805 and 1877. In 1805 the Nez Perce helped the newcomers. In 1877 the newcomers' government ordered them off their homeland. What changed? Gold, settlers, and a treaty that some leaders signed but other bands never agreed to. I notice a pattern from Week 11: a treaty signed by a few was used against everyone.

🤝 Let's Try Together: Reading a Reported Speech

Newspapers printed a speech said to be Chief Joseph's surrender, ending "From where the sun now stands, I will fight no more forever." Let's weigh it like historians. Joseph spoke in the Nez Perce language. An interpreter turned his words into English, army officers wrote them down, and the speech was printed in Harper's Weekly on November 17, 1877. So the English is a reported translation, and historians cannot be sure how close it is to his exact words. In 1879, Joseph went to Washington, D.C. His words, again put into English by an interpreter, were printed in a magazine: "Good words do not last long unless they amount to something." Both sources carry the same message: his people wanted to go home.

📜 Allotment, Boarding Schools and Wounded Knee

Next, the government tried to break up Indigenous nations from the inside. The Dawes Act of 1887 divided shared reservation land into allotments, such as 160 acres for the head of a family. Land left over was called "surplus," and much of it was sold or opened to white settlers. The government also sent Native children to boarding schools. The Carlisle Indian Industrial School in Pennsylvania, the first federal boarding school for Native children built far from a reservation, opened in 1879. From 1879 to 1918, about 7,800 children attended it. Staff cut boys' braids, gave children new English names and did not allow Native languages. In 1890, fear of the Ghost Dance led the army onto Lakota reservations. On December 29, 1890, at Wounded Knee Creek in South Dakota, U.S. soldiers killed hundreds of Lakota people, many of them women and children.

👁 Watch Me Think

I connect the two policies. Allotment took land. Boarding schools tried to take language and culture. Both had the same goal, called assimilation: to make Native people stop living as Native nations. Now I check the land table for evidence. Did the land Indigenous nations held go up or down after 1887?

🤝 Let's Try Together: Reading the Land Table

Let's do the math. 138 million minus 48 million is 90 million acres lost in 47 years. That is about two out of every three acres. The table cannot tell us why by itself, so we connect it to evidence: most of the loss came from "surplus" land sold or opened to settlers. A historian also asks what a table cannot show: the families who lost farms, and children who lost their languages. Here is a hard truth, told gently. A U.S. government investigation counted at least 973 Native children who died at federal boarding schools and said the true number is likely higher. On October 25, 2024, the president formally apologized for the boarding school system. Today, many Native nations teach their languages to children again.

🔍 Source Detective: Two Views of One School System

In 1892, Richard Henry Pratt, the army officer who founded the Carlisle school, gave a speech explaining his idea. In 1900, Zitkála-Šá, a Yankton Dakota writer, published her memory of arriving at a missionary boarding school in Indiana when she was eight years old. Compare the two.

Primary Sources: Richard Henry Pratt, 1892, and Zitkála-Šá, 1900

Part 1 (Pratt, 1892): "Kill the Indian in him, and save the man."

Part 2 (Zitkála-Šá, 1900): "Our mothers had taught us that only unskilled warriors who were captured had their hair shingled by the enemy." When she heard the plan to cut her hair, she said: "No, I will not submit! I will struggle first!"

Part 3 (Zitkála-Šá, 1900): "I cried aloud, shaking my head all the while until I felt the cold blades of the scissors against my neck, and heard them gnaw off one of my thick braids. Then I lost my spirit."

Word help: Kill the Indian in him = Pratt meant destroying the child's Native culture; shingled = cut short; submit = give in; gnaw = chew away at; spirit = inner strength and hope.

Part 1: Richard Henry Pratt, "The Advantages of Mingling Indians with Whites," speech to the National Conference of Charities and Correction, 1892. Parts 2 and 3: Zitkála-Šá, "The School Days of an Indian Girl," Atlantic Monthly, February 1900; reprinted in her book American Indian Stories (1921). Real wording; short parts.

👁 Think Like a Historian

First I check who is speaking. Pratt ran the schools. Zitkála-Šá was a child inside one. Both are primary sources, but they come from opposite sides. Then I compare purpose: Pratt is persuading other officials that his plan is good. Zitkála-Šá is telling readers what the plan felt like to a child. A historian needs both to understand the schools.

🪶 People Who Kept Their Nations Strong

Some resisted on the battlefield, some with pens, medicine, music or sports. Read each card and ask: how did this person help their people survive, and what did they have to push against?

Sitting Bull (Tȟatȟáŋka Íyotake)

about 1831–1890

A Hunkpapa Lakota holy man and leader. Before the Little Bighorn, he had a vision of soldiers falling into the Lakota camp. In 1877 he led his band into Canada rather than surrender, and in 1881 he returned and surrendered at Fort Buford. He toured with a Wild West show in 1885, then lived on the Standing Rock Reservation. In December 1890, he was killed when reservation police came to arrest him.

Zitkála-Šá (Red Bird)

1876–1938

A Yankton Dakota writer, violinist and activist, also known as Gertrude Simmons Bonnin. Sent to a missionary boarding school at age eight, she later taught at Carlisle, then wrote essays criticizing the schools. She co-wrote The Sun Dance Opera (1913). In 1926 she and her husband founded the National Council of American Indians and helped register Native voters.

Jim Thorpe (Wa-Tho-Huk)

1887–1953

A citizen of the Sac and Fox Nation who grew up in Indian Territory (now Oklahoma) and attended Carlisle. His Native name means "Bright Path." At the 1912 Olympics in Stockholm, Sweden, he won gold medals in the pentathlon and decathlon. In 1913 the medals were taken away because he had been paid to play minor league baseball. In 1982 his family received copies, and in 2022 the Olympic committee named him the sole champion again.

Dr. Susan La Flesche Picotte

1865–1915

Born on the Omaha Reservation in Nebraska, she graduated at the top of her class from the Woman's Medical College of Pennsylvania in 1889, becoming the first Native American woman to earn a medical degree in the United States. She cared for more than 1,200 Omaha people. In 1913 she opened a hospital in Walthill, Nebraska, the first privately funded hospital on a reservation.

Native Voters After 1924

1924–1948

On June 2, 1924, the Indian Citizenship Act made all Native Americans born in the United States citizens, without taking away their rights as members of their nations. About 125,000 Native people became citizens that day. But some states still blocked Native voters. In 1948, World War II veteran Frank Harrison and his nation's chairman, Harry Austin (both Yavapai), won in Arizona's courts, and Marine veteran Miguel Trujillo (Isleta Pueblo) won in New Mexico.
