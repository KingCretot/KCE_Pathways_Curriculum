🏛️ A City Planned with Lines and Angles

Nonfiction Connection
📖

Real Talk: Washington, D.C.

In 1791, a French-born engineer named Pierre L’Enfant drew a plan for the new capital city of the United States. He laid out a grid: numbered streets run north and south, and lettered streets run east and west, so most corners are right angles. Then he added wide diagonal avenues, many named after states, that cut across the grid. Where a diagonal avenue crosses a grid street, the corners are not right angles: some are narrow and some are wide. That same year, surveyors Andrew Ellicott and Benjamin Banneker, a self-taught Black astronomer and mathematician, helped measure the boundary lines of the new capital.

📏 Points, Lines, Segments, and Rays

👁

The Building Blocks of Geometry

A point is an exact location, shown with a dot and named with a capital letter. A line is perfectly straight and goes on forever in both directions, so we draw arrows on both ends. A line segment is the part of a line between two endpoints, so it has a length you can measure. A ray has one endpoint and goes on forever in one direction.

Line BCno endpoints, arrows on both ends
Line segment DE2 endpoints, has a length
Ray FG1 endpoint; name it endpoint first: FG, not GF

How Two Lines Can Meet (or Not)

🧩

On Imani’s Map

Two lettered streets run east and west side by side, so they are parallel: they never meet. A numbered street crosses them at square corners, so it is perpendicular to them. A diagonal avenue crosses the grid but not at square corners, so it is intersecting but not perpendicular. A small square drawn in a corner means “this is a right angle.”

📐 Angles and the Protractor

🧭

Mr. Adeyemi’s Corners

An angle is made of two rays that share the same endpoint. That shared endpoint is the vertex. Angles are measured in degrees, written with the symbol °. A full turn all the way around a circle is 360°, so a 1-degree angle turns through 1/360 of a circle.

acute: 50°
right: 90°
obtuse: 130°
straight: 180°
Acute angleless than 90°
Right angleexactly 90° (a square corner)
Obtuse anglemore than 90° but less than 180°
Straight angleexactly 180° (a straight line)

Reading a Protractor

The base ray passes through the red 0, so read the red numbers: 60°
Step 1: Put the center hole of the protractor exactly on the vertex.
Step 2: Line up the protractor’s base line with one ray.
Step 3: Find the 0 that this ray passes through. Use the numbers that start from that 0.
Step 4: Read the number where the other ray crosses the scale. Here it is 60 on the red scale (the blue scale shows 120, the wrong scale for this angle).
Check: This angle is smaller than a square corner, so it is acute. 60° makes sense; 120° does not.
🕒

Angles on a Clock

A clock face is a full turn: 360° split into 12 equal spaces. 360 ÷ 12 = 30, so the hands turn 30° from one number to the next. At 3:00 the hands are 3 spaces apart: 3 × 30 = 90°, a right angle.

➕ Angle Addition and Missing Angles

👁

Angles Add Up

When an angle is split into smaller angles that do not overlap, the measure of the whole angle is the sum of the parts. This is called angle addition. Below, ray BC splits angle ABD into two parts: 35° + 55° = 90°.

Parts of a right angleadd to 90°
Parts of a straight angleadd to 180°
Angles all the way around a pointadd to 360°
On a straight line: 130° + 50° = 180°
Around a point: 140° + 110° + 110° = 360°
Missing angle = whole − known parts
Example: A diagonal avenue meets a straight street. One angle on the north side of the street is 130°. The two angles on that side make a straight angle, so the other one is 180 − 130 = 50°.
Example: Three paths meet at a fountain. Two angles are 140° and 110°. All the way around is 360°: 140 + 110 = 250, and 360 − 250 = 110°.
Check: Add all the parts. They should equal the whole: 90°, 180°, or 360°.

✏️ Drawing Angles and Angles as Turns

🚲

Ms. Ruiz’s Bike Lane Plan

Ms. Ruiz needs a bike lane that leaves the curb at a 65° angle. Imani draws it on the plan with a protractor.

Drawing a 65° angle
Step 1: Draw a ray. Its endpoint will be the vertex.
Step 2: Put the protractor’s center hole on the endpoint and its base line along the ray.
Step 3: Find 65 on the scale that starts at 0 on your ray (here, the red scale). Make a small dot.
Step 4: Take the protractor away and draw a ray from the vertex through the dot.
Check: 65° is acute, so your angle should look smaller than a square corner. If it looks wide, you used the other scale and drew 115°.

Angles Are Turns

Picture a ray spinning around its endpoint like the hand of a clock. A full turn is 360°. Find a fraction of a turn by finding that fraction of 360.

1/12 turn360 ÷ 12 = 30°
1/8 turn360 ÷ 8 = 45°
1/4 turn (quarter turn)360 ÷ 4 = 90°
1/2 turn (half turn)360 ÷ 2 = 180°
3/4 turn3 × 90 = 270°
