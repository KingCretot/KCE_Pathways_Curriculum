🖼️ Picture It: The Parts of an Argument

An argument in writing is not a fight. It is a careful plan to persuade a reader. The writer states a claim, backs it up with reasons, proves each reason with evidence, and then answers the other side. Here is how one neighbor built her argument about the empty lot.

📣 CLAIM: the main opinion

The city should turn the lot on Linden Street into a community garden, not a parking lot.

💭 REASON: why the writer thinks so

First, a garden would give families fresh food.

🔍 EVIDENCE: proof for the reason

The nearest grocery store is two miles away, and many neighbors do not have cars.

↔️ COUNTERCLAIM and REBUTTAL: the other side, then an answer

Some neighbors argue that Linden Street needs more parking. However, a survey found that 84 of 120 shoppers walked or took the bus.
📣 claim💭 reason🔍 evidence↔️ counterclaim + rebuttal, comma after an introductory word
PartExample
1. A claim is an opinion that people could disagree with. State it clearly, early in your writing.The lot should become a community garden.
2. A reason tells WHY you believe the claim. Strong arguments give two or three.A garden would give families fresh food.
3. Evidence proves a reason: facts, numbers, examples, or the words of an expert. Evidence can be checked.The garden grew 900 pounds of vegetables.
4. A counterclaim is the other side’s opinion. State it fairly, without calling anyone names.Some neighbors say we need parking.
5. A rebuttal answers the counterclaim, best with evidence, and shows why your claim is still stronger.However, 84 of 120 shoppers walked.
6. Comma rule A: put a comma after an introductory word or phrase, like First, Finally, However, In addition, or For example.Finally, a garden helps the whole street.
7. Comma rule B: in a series of three or more items, put a comma after each item except the last.soil, insects, and plants
💡

Evidence Must Be a Fact, Not Another Opinion

A fact can be checked and proven true: “The lot has been empty for six years.” An opinion tells what someone thinks or feels: “Gardens are beautiful.” An opinion can be a claim, but it cannot prove one. When you judge an argument, ask: Could I check this? If not, it is not evidence.

claim
The main opinion a writer wants you to accept. “My claim is simple: the city should turn this lot into a community garden.”
reason
Why the writer believes the claim. “First, a garden would give families fresh food.”
evidence
Facts, numbers, examples, or expert words that prove a reason. “The nearest grocery store is two miles away.”
counterclaim
The other side’s opinion. “Some neighbors argue that Linden Street needs more parking for the shops.”
rebuttal
An answer to the counterclaim that shows why it is weaker. “However, our survey of 120 shoppers found that 84 of them walked or took the bus.”
persuade
To convince someone to agree or to act. Both letters try to persuade the city council to vote their way.

📬 Letter A: Let Linden Street Grow

This opinion letter was printed in the neighborhood newsletter. Watch for the claim, each reason, the evidence, and how the writer answers the other side. Notice the paragraph numbers; you will use them.

Letter A • Opinion LetterLet Linden Street Grow

by Nadia Brooks, fifth grader • Linden Street Neighborhood Newsletter

1Dear Members of the City Council: For six years, the lot at the corner of Linden Street and Ash Avenue has been empty. Today it holds weeds, broken glass, and an old shopping cart. My claim is simple: the city should turn this lot into a community garden, not a parking lot.

2First, a garden would give families fresh food. The nearest grocery store is two miles away, and many neighbors do not have cars. Last spring, the Hamilton Street garden, six blocks east, grew more than 900 pounds of vegetables, and gardeners shared much of it with neighbors.

3Second, a garden would be a place to learn. Mrs. Okafor, a science teacher at Ash Avenue Elementary, says her students could study soil, insects, and plant life cycles “in a living classroom only a block from school.”

4Finally, a garden helps the whole street. Plants soak up rainwater, so less water floods the basements on our block after a storm. In addition, neighbors who garden together get to know each other.

5Some neighbors argue that Linden Street needs more parking for the shops. That is a fair concern. However, our newsletter’s survey of 120 shoppers found that 84 of them walked or took the bus to get there. Only 36 drove. A parking lot would help a few drivers, but a garden would help everyone.

6For these reasons, I urge you to vote yes on the Linden Street garden. Let’s grow something good together. Sincerely, Nadia Brooks

⚖️ Name That Part

🔎

How to Tell

Ask what job the sentence does. Is it the writer’s big opinion (claim)? Does it say WHY (reason)? Can it be checked, like a number or an expert’s words (evidence)? Does it tell the other side’s view (counterclaim)? Does it answer that view (rebuttal)? Signal words help: First, Second, Finally (reasons); For example (evidence); Some people say (counterclaim); However (rebuttal).

🏗️ Build an Argument Like a Pro

🪜

Five Steps to a Strong Argument

  1. Claim: state your opinion clearly at the start.
  2. Reasons: give two or three reasons, each introduced with a signal word like First, Second, or Finally.
  3. Evidence: prove each reason with facts, numbers, examples, or an expert’s words, not more opinions.
  4. Counterclaim and rebuttal: state the other side fairly, then answer it, best with evidence.
  5. Conclusion: restate your claim and ask the reader to act.
🧪

Worked Example: Weak or Strong Evidence?

Reason: A garden would be a place to learn.

❌ Weak: Gardens are really, really cool, and everyone loves them.
This is only a feeling. No one can check “really cool,” and “everyone” is not true.

✅ Strong: Mrs. Okafor, a science teacher at Ash Avenue Elementary, says her students could study soil, insects, and plant life cycles there.
An expert gives a specific example of what students would learn. It can be checked by asking her.

🎯

Two Comma Rules Every Arguer Needs

Rule A, introductory words: Signal words at the start of a sentence are followed by a comma: First, Second, Finally, However, In addition, For example, Yes, No,.
Rule B, items in a series: When you list three or more things, put a comma after each item except the last: weeds, broken glass, and an old shopping cart. The comma before “and” is called the series comma. KCE uses it, but this lesson also accepts sentences without it.

📝 Writer’s Turn: Evidence in Your Own Words

🔄

Use Evidence the Honest Way

When you use someone else’s evidence in your own argument, you paraphrase it (say it in your own words) and name where it came from. Your sentence is checked by four fair rules: (1) enough words to be a full idea, (2) no 4 or more words in a row copied from the original (names can stay), (3) the key ideas kept, and (4) the source named. If a rule is missed, the feedback tells you which one.
