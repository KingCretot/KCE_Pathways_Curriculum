ecosystem
All the living and nonliving things in an area and the ways they affect each other. Example: the Everglades marsh, its water, its plants and its animals.
wetland
Land that is covered by shallow water for all or part of the year. The Everglades is Florida's most famous wetland.
sheet flow
Water moving slowly across wide, flat land in a thin, wide layer instead of in a narrow channel.
periphyton
A spongy mat of algae and tiny living things in Everglades water. It is a producer and feeds snails, crayfish and small fish.
mangrove
A tree that grows in salty coastal water. Its tangled roots hold the shore together and shelter young sea animals.
estuary
A place where fresh water mixes with salty ocean water, making brackish (slightly salty) water. Example: Florida Bay.
coral reef
An ocean structure built over many years by tiny animals called coral polyps.

🌾 The River of Grass

Long ago, when Lake Okeechobee filled up in the rainy season, water spilled over its southern edge. It spread into a shallow sheet, from a few inches to a few feet deep and many miles wide, and crept south toward Florida Bay. This slow sheet flow moved much slower than a person walks, so the land stayed wet for months. Tough sawgrass, with tiny sawlike teeth along its blades, grew almost everywhere, which is why writer Marjory Stoneman Douglas called the Everglades a river of grass.

👁 Watch Me Think

South Florida is very flat. The land drops only a few inches every mile. So I ask: if water sits on almost-flat ground, will it rush or creep? It creeps. Slow water has time to soak the soil, feed the plants and keep fish alive into the dry season. The slope of the land explains the speed of the water, and the speed of the water explains why so much life is here.

🧩 Picture It: Following the Water

  • North: Rain in central Florida flows down the Kissimmee River into Lake Okeechobee.
  • Middle: The lake overflows south into the sawgrass marsh, the river of grass.
  • South: Fresh water reaches the mangroves and mixes with salt water in Florida Bay.
  • Offshore: Coral reefs grow in clear ocean water along the Florida Keys.

🐊 Who Eats Whom in the Glades

Everglades food webs start with producers: sawgrass, other marsh plants and periphyton, a spongy mat of algae and tiny living things in the water. Small consumers like apple snails, crayfish and small fish eat periphyton. Wading birds such as wood storks, white ibises and roseate spoonbills eat those small animals, and American alligators eat fish, turtles and birds. Decomposers like bacteria and fungi break down dead plants and animals into nutrients that producers use again.

👁 Watch Me Think

Adaptations help each animal fit into the web. A roseate spoonbill sweeps its flat, spoon-shaped bill side to side in the water and snaps it shut when it feels prey. Its pink color comes from pigments in the shrimp and crayfish it eats. In the dry season, alligators dig out and keep open gator holes that still hold water, and fish, turtles and birds gather there. So I ask: if the alligators disappeared, which animals would have a harder dry season?

🌳 Mangroves and Coral Reefs: Florida's Coastal Guards

Where Everglades fresh water meets the sea, mangrove forests grow in salty, muddy water. Red mangroves stand on arching prop roots, and young snook, snapper, shrimp and crabs hide among those roots, so mangroves are called nurseries. Their tangled roots slow waves and storm surge and hold the shoreline in place. Farther offshore, Florida's Coral Reef stretches about 350 miles along the Florida Keys and the southeast coast. It is the only living coral barrier reef in the continental United States.

👁 Watch Me Think

A coral looks like a rock or a plant, so I check the evidence. Each coral is made of many tiny animals called polyps that build hard skeletons. Inside the polyps live tiny algae that make food from sunlight and share it, so the reef depends on producers too. When the water gets too hot, the algae leave and the coral turns white. This is called coral bleaching. Very hot summers, like 2023 in Florida, can bleach many corals at once, so scientists watch the water temperature closely.

🤝 Let's Try Together: How Are They Connected?

Mangrove roots trap mud before it reaches the reef, which helps keep reef water clear for the corals' algae. Many young fish grow up in the mangroves and then move out to the reef as adults. So if the mangroves are harmed, a scientist would predict fewer fish on the reef in later years.

🦩 Lab: Wading Birds and the Water

Scientists count wading bird nests in the Everglades every year. Nest counts are one way to check the health of the whole ecosystem, because wading birds need lots of small fish to feed their chicks. Study the data table and look for a pattern.

👁 Think Like a Ecologist

An ecologist does not trust one year by itself. I look for a pattern across several years, compare years that are alike and years that are different, and ask what else could explain the numbers. Wood storks and other wading birds feed best when dry-season water drops slowly and steadily, because fish crowd into shrinking pools where the birds can catch them. When heavy rain refills the marsh in the middle of nesting season, the fish spread out again.

👩‍🔬 People Who Protect Florida's Wild Places

Writers, scientists, planners and whole communities have worked to protect the Everglades. Notice how each one used evidence, knowledge and their voice.

Marjory Stoneman Douglas

1890–1998

A Miami journalist and writer. Her 1947 book The Everglades: River of Grass showed readers that the Everglades was a living, flowing river, not a useless swamp to drain. Everglades National Park was dedicated that same year. In 1969 she founded Friends of the Everglades, and she spoke up for the Everglades for the rest of her long life.

The Miccosukee and Seminole Peoples

1800s to today

Seminole and Miccosukee families made homes deep in the Everglades and Big Cypress in the 1800s, many of them after the United States tried to force them to move west. They learned the land closely: where fish gather, how the water moves, and how to travel by canoe and build chickees (open-sided homes with thatched roofs). Today the Miccosukee Tribe and the Seminole Tribe of Florida have lands in and near the Everglades and work to keep its water clean.

Ernest F. Coe

1866–1951

A landscape architect who moved to Miami and grew to love the Everglades. Beginning in the late 1920s, he campaigned for years to make it a national park. He is often called the father of Everglades National Park.

MaVynee Betsch

1935–2005

Known as the Beach Lady, she was the great-granddaughter of A. L. Lewis, Florida's first Black millionaire, whose company created American Beach on Amelia Island as a beach for Black families. She gave up a career as an opera singer and gave much of her money to environmental causes. She fought to save NaNa, a towering sand dune at American Beach. Today the National Park Service protects it as part of the Timucuan Ecological and Historic Preserve.
