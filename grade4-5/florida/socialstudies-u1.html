🌟 Word Wrangler

Tap each card to flip it. Tap the 🔊 to hear the word and what it means.

archaeologist
A scientist who learns about the past by studying objects and places people left behind. Example: an archaeologist maps a shell mound.
artifact
An object made or used by people long ago, such as a tool, a pot or a carving.
shell mound
A large hill made mostly of shells and other material piled up by people over many years. Some held homes or temples on top.
chiefdom
A community or group of towns led by a chief. The Timucua lived in many separate chiefdoms.
tribute
Goods or payments that weaker towns gave to a stronger ruler. Many towns paid tribute to the Calusa leader.
point of view
The way a person sees events, shaped by who they are and what they want. A Spanish soldier and a Calusa elder would tell the same event differently.
immunity
The body's protection against a disease. Indigenous Peoples had no immunity to diseases like smallpox that Europeans carried.

🗺️ Four Nations, Four Homelands

Before Europeans arrived, many Indigenous nations lived in Florida. The Calusa lived on the southwest coast and built a powerful fishing kingdom. The Timucua lived in northeast and north-central Florida in many separate chiefdoms that farmed and fished. The Apalachee lived in the panhandle near present-day Tallahassee and grew large fields of corn. The Tequesta lived around Biscayne Bay, where Miami is today, and lived by fishing and gathering.

👁 Watch Me Think

I notice that the Calusa and Tequesta lived on the southern coasts, near warm, shallow water full of fish and shellfish. The Apalachee and Timucua lived farther north, where the soil was better for corn. So I can reason that geography helped shape how each nation got its food. That is a pattern, but I still need evidence for each nation.

🐚 How Do We Know? Mounds, Artifacts and Records

These nations did not leave written books, so historians use other evidence. Archaeologists study shell mounds like Mound Key in Estero Bay, an island the Calusa built up with shells, which many experts think was the Calusa capital. At Key Marco, wet mud preserved wooden carvings like the Key Marco Cat, found in 1896. In Miami, workers found the Miami Circle in 1998, a ring of holes cut into rock about 2,000 years ago. Spanish visitors also wrote records, but those records show a Spanish point of view.

👁 Watch Me Think

When I read a Spanish record, I ask: who wrote it, and what did he want? A Spanish soldier might describe the Calusa as enemies, because Spain wanted their land. So I compare the record with archaeology. If the shells, tools and carvings agree with the record, I trust that part more. If they disagree, I keep asking questions.

🧩 Picture It: Three kinds of evidence

  • Mound Key (Estero Bay): an island built up mostly from shells, more than 30 feet high in places.
  • Key Marco Cat: a small wooden figure, part human and part cat, found in 1896 and owned by the Smithsonian.
  • Miami Circle: a ring of holes about 38 feet across, cut into limestone by the Tequesta or their ancestors.

⚖️ After the Europeans Arrived

Spanish ships reached Florida in 1513. The Calusa drove off Juan Ponce de León's expedition in 1521, and he was wounded and later died. But Europeans brought diseases like smallpox, and Florida's people had no immunity, so many died. War, forced work at Spanish missions, and raids by English colonists and their allies, who captured people and sold them into slavery, caused even more loss. By the 1700s, most of Florida's original nations were gone. Later, Creek and other Indigenous people moved into Florida and formed the Seminole and Miccosukee nations, who still live in Florida today.

👁 Watch Me Think

I ask: who benefited and who paid the cost? Spain gained land and power. The English colonists who raided Florida's towns profited by selling captives. Florida's first nations paid the highest cost: their lives, homes and freedom. But the story does not end there, because Indigenous nations still live in Florida today.

🔍 Source Detective: The Calusa Evidence File

You are a historian. You have three pieces of evidence about the Calusa. Read each one, then decide what it can and cannot tell you.

Evidence File: Artifact, Place and Record

A. The Key Marco Cat (artifact)
A wooden figure, about 6 inches tall, part human and part cat. Found in wet mud at Key Marco in 1896 by archaeologist Frank Hamilton Cushing. Made by: the Calusa or their ancestors, hundreds of years before Europeans arrived.

B. Mound Key (place)
An island in Estero Bay built up mostly from shells, with mounds more than 30 feet high, canals and room for a large town. Made by: Calusa people, over hundreds of years.

C. Fontaneda's memoir (written record)
A Spanish boy, Hernando de Escalante Fontaneda, was shipwrecked in the 1540s and lived among south Florida's nations for about 17 years. Later he wrote that the Calusa ate fish, turtles and shellfish, and that their leader Carlos ruled many towns that paid him tribute. Made by: a Spaniard, after he returned to Spanish lands (about 1575).

Key Marco Cat: Smithsonian National Museum of Natural History. Mound Key: Mound Key Archaeological State Park, Florida State Parks. Memoir of Hernando de Escalante Fontaneda (about 1575), summarized.

👁 Think Like a Historian

A historian asks of every piece of evidence: Who made this? When? Why? What can it prove, and what can it NOT prove? I also look for places where two kinds of evidence agree, because that makes a claim stronger.

🧭 People in Florida's Early Story

Meet people whose actions and records help us understand Florida's first nations, and a leader who helped one nation survive into today.

Carlos (Calos)

died 1567

Paramount leader of the Calusa when the Spanish arrived. He ruled from his capital, likely at Mound Key, over dozens of towns. He bargained with Spanish leader Pedro Menéndez de Avilés, but the Spanish later killed him in 1567.

Pedro Menéndez de Avilés

1519–1574

Spanish commander who founded St. Augustine in 1565. He met Carlos in 1566 and made alliances to bring Florida under Spanish control, including forts and missions among the Calusa and Tequesta.

Hernando de Escalante Fontaneda

shipwrecked in the 1540s; memoir about 1575

Shipwrecked as a 13-year-old boy, he lived among south Florida's nations for about 17 years. His memoir is one of the few eyewitness records of Calusa life, written from a Spanish point of view.

Frank Hamilton Cushing

1857–1900

American archaeologist who dug at Key Marco in 1896. He uncovered wooden masks, tools and the Key Marco Cat, preserved in wet mud for hundreds of years.

Buffalo Tiger

1920–2015

Miccosukee leader who helped his nation win recognition from the U.S. government in 1962 and became its first chairman. He worked so the Miccosukee could govern themselves and protect the Everglades.
