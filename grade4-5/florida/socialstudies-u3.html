🌿 A New Territory and the Seminole Wars

In 1821, Spain handed Florida to the United States under the Adams–Onís Treaty, and in 1822 Florida became a U.S. territory. Seminole people farmed and raised cattle in north and central Florida, and so did Black Seminoles, freedom seekers and their families who lived in their own towns as Seminole allies. White settlers wanted the land, and enslavers in Georgia and Florida wanted the Black Seminoles returned to slavery. After the Indian Removal Act of 1830, the U.S. tried to force the Seminole west, and the Second Seminole War (1835–1842) became one of the longest and most costly wars the U.S. Army ever fought against an Indigenous nation.

👁 Watch Me Think

I ask who wanted what. Settlers wanted farmland. Enslavers wanted to recapture people who had escaped slavery. The Seminole and Black Seminoles wanted to stay in their homes and stay free. So this war had two causes tangled together: land and slavery. That is why a U.S. general, Thomas Jesup, wrote in 1836 that this was mainly a war about the Black Seminoles, not the Seminole.

🧩 Picture It: The Three Seminole Wars

WarWhat happened
First, 1817–1818Andrew Jackson's troops invaded Spanish Florida and burned Seminole and Black Seminole towns.
Second, 1835–1842The Seminole and Black Seminoles fought removal for seven years; thousands were forced west.
Third, 1855–1858The Army pushed into the Everglades; a few hundred Seminole stayed hidden there and never surrendered.

🤝 Let's Try Together: Osceola and the White Flag

Osceola was a young Seminole leader who refused to accept removal. In October 1837, he came to talks near St. Augustine under a white flag, a sign of a truce, or a safe pause in fighting to talk. General Thomas Jesup had him seized anyway. Let's reason: a white flag is supposed to protect people who come to talk, so many Americans, even some who supported the war, criticized Jesup. Osceola was held at Fort Marion, the old Castillo de San Marcos, and then at Fort Moultrie in South Carolina, where he died of illness in January 1838. The war went on for four more years.

🛶 Abraham, the Black Seminoles and the Unconquered

Abraham was born enslaved, gained his freedom among the Seminole, and became an interpreter and adviser to the Seminole leader Micanopy; he even traveled with Seminole leaders to Washington, D.C. Black Seminoles fought hard beside the Seminole because removal could mean being returned to slavery. During the war, General Jesup promised that Black Seminoles who surrendered could go west with the Seminole instead of being enslaved, and Abraham helped bring many people in under that promise. By 1842, thousands of Seminole and Black Seminole people had been forced to move to Indian Territory, in present-day Oklahoma, but a few hundred Seminole stayed in the Everglades and never surrendered.

👁 Watch Me Think

Abraham had to stand between two worlds. He spoke English and Seminole languages, so he could carry words between generals and leaders. I ask: whose side was he on? His goal was to keep his people free and alive, and the evidence shows he bargained hard for that. Interpreters held real power, because every promise passed through their words.

🤝 Let's Try Together: Two Kinds of Freedom

Let's compare. On a Georgia plantation, enslaved people were owned, worked without pay and could be sold away from their families. In Black Seminole towns, people built their own homes, raised crops and livestock, carried weapons and chose their own leaders. Many gave a share of their crops to Seminole leaders as allies. Historians still debate how to describe that bond, but they agree it was very different from plantation slavery. Now reason: if removal or capture could send them back to a plantation, why would Black Seminoles fight so hard? Because they had the most to lose: their freedom.

⭐ Statehood, Civil War and Reconstruction in Florida

On March 3, 1845, Florida became the 27th state, a state where slavery was legal, and by 1860 almost half of its people were enslaved, many on cotton plantations around Tallahassee. In January 1861, Florida seceded and joined the Confederacy, sending beef, salt and soldiers, though Union forces held Key West and Fort Pickens near Pensacola for the whole war. On May 20, 1865, Union General Edward McCook read the Emancipation Proclamation in Tallahassee, and May 20 is still celebrated as Florida's Emancipation Day. During Reconstruction, Black Floridians voted and held office: Jonathan C. Gibbs became Florida's secretary of state in 1868, and Josiah T. Walls became Florida's first Black member of Congress in 1871.

👁 Watch Me Think

I trace one question through the years: who had power? In 1845, statehood gave power to white men, many of them enslavers. In 1865 and the years after, freedpeople gained freedom and, for Black men, the vote, so leaders like Gibbs and Walls could serve. After 1877, that power was taken back. When I see power move, I look for the laws and the violence that moved it.

🤝 Let's Try Together: Florida and the Election of 1876

In the 1876 presidential election, Florida was one of three Southern states whose votes were disputed. Each side claimed it had won Florida. Let's follow the chain: the dispute was settled by the Compromise of 1877. Rutherford B. Hayes became president, federal troops left the South, and in Florida a new governor who opposed Reconstruction took office in January 1877. Over the next years, Florida passed a poll tax in 1889 and other laws that pushed most Black men out of voting. So a close election in Florida helped end Reconstruction for the whole nation.

🔍 Source Detective: Counting Florida, 1840–1860

Every ten years, U.S. census takers counted the people in Florida. Numbers can be powerful evidence, but historians also ask who did the counting, and who was left out. Study the table.

Notes: Seminole people living in Florida were not counted in these censuses. In 1850 and 1860, census takers listed enslaved people by age and sex under their enslaver's name, usually without their own names.

👁 Think Like a Historian

First I read the title and the columns so I know what is being counted. Then I look for change over time: what grew, and how fast? Then I ask what the numbers cannot tell me. A census counts people, but it does not tell their stories, and in 1850 and 1860 enslaved people were listed by age and sex, usually without their names. Last, I check who is missing from the count entirely.

🌴 People Who Shaped Florida, 1821–1877

These leaders lived through removal, statehood, war and Reconstruction. Read each card and ask: what did this person fight for, and what did it cost?

Osceola

about 1804–1838

Born in a Creek town in present-day Alabama, he moved to Florida as a boy and became a Seminole war leader who refused removal. In 1837 he was seized while under a white flag of truce and died in prison at Fort Moultrie, South Carolina, in January 1838. Many places in Florida are named for him today.

Abraham

1790s–1870s

Born enslaved, he gained freedom among the Seminole and became an interpreter and adviser to the Seminole leader Micanopy. He traveled with Seminole leaders to Washington, D.C., and helped negotiate during the Second Seminole War, bargaining for Black Seminoles to go west with the Seminole instead of being enslaved. He later went west and died in present-day Oklahoma.

Abiaka (Sam Jones)

about 1781–1866

A Seminole medicine man and war leader who refused every order to leave Florida. During the Second Seminole War he led his people deep into the Everglades and Big Cypress. Many of today's Florida Seminole and Miccosukee people descend from the families who stayed with him.

David Levy Yulee

1810–1886

One of Florida's first two U.S. senators in 1845 and the first Jewish U.S. senator. He built the Florida Railroad from Fernandina to Cedar Key, finished in 1861. He was also an enslaver who supported secession and the Confederacy.

Jonathan C. Gibbs

1821–1874

Born free in Philadelphia, he graduated from Dartmouth College in 1852 and became a minister. He came to Florida after the war, was a delegate to the convention that wrote its 1868 constitution, and became Florida's secretary of state, the first Black person in that office. As superintendent of public instruction from 1873 to 1874, he worked to build Florida's public schools.

Josiah T. Walls

1842–1905

Born enslaved in Virginia, he was forced to work for the Confederate army, was freed when Union troops captured him in 1862, and later joined the U.S. Colored Troops, who brought him to Florida. He farmed near Gainesville and in 1871 became Florida's first Black member of Congress, serving into 1876 despite opponents who challenged his elections.
