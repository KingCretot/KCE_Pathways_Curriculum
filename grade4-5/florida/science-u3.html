🌎 Why Launch from Florida?

Earth spins toward the east, and the ground at the equator moves at about 1,040 miles per hour; the farther a place is from the equator, the slower its ground moves. A rocket launched toward the east gets a free boost from that spin, so launch sites closer to the equator need less fuel to reach orbit. Cape Canaveral, at about 28.5 degrees north latitude, is one of the farthest-south places in the continental United States with open ocean to its east. That ocean matters too: empty rocket stages and any pieces from an accident fall into the Atlantic instead of onto towns. Florida's ports also let huge rocket parts arrive by barge, since they are too big to travel easily by road.

👁 Watch Me Think

Picture a merry-go-round spinning to the right. If I stand on it and throw a ball forward in the same direction it spins, the ball gets the merry-go-round's speed plus my throw. If I throw it backward, against the spin, my throw first has to cancel out the merry-go-round's speed. Earth is a giant merry-go-round spinning east. So launching east is like throwing with the spin. And the closer to the equator, the faster the ground moves, the bigger the free boost. Comparing a big idea to something I can feel helps me reason about it.

🧩 Picture It: Map Description: The Space Coast

Picture Florida's Atlantic coast, about halfway down the state. A long, thin strip of land called a barrier island sits between the ocean and the mainland. On it, a point of land juts out into the Atlantic: that is Cape Canaveral, home of Cape Canaveral Space Force Station. Just north and west of it is Merritt Island, where NASA's Kennedy Space Center and its two big launch pads, 39A and 39B, sit near the beach. Shallow lagoons and the Banana River separate the islands from the mainland city of Titusville. To the east there is nothing but open ocean for thousands of miles.

🚀 How Rockets Reach Space

A rocket burns fuel and pushes hot gas out of its engines very fast, downward. Newton's third law says that every action has an equal and opposite reaction: when the rocket pushes the gas down, the gas pushes the rocket up with a force called thrust. At liftoff, the thrust must be greater than gravity's pull on the rocket, which makes the forces unbalanced so the rocket speeds up. To stay in orbit around Earth, a spacecraft must travel sideways at about 17,500 miles per hour, so fast that as it falls toward Earth, Earth's curved surface keeps falling away beneath it. Rockets drop empty sections called stages as they climb, so the engines have less mass to push.

👁 Watch Me Think

If I blow up a balloon and let it go, it zooms across the room. Why? The stretched balloon squeezes the air out of the opening, pushing the air backward, and the air pushes the balloon forward. That is Newton's third law: equal and opposite forces. Some people think a rocket needs air to push against, so it could not work in space. But the balloon is not pushing on the room's air; it is pushing on its own air. A rocket is the same: it pushes on its own hot gas, so it works even in empty space.

🤝 Let's Try Together: Balanced or Unbalanced at Liftoff?

Let's use force arrows. The Saturn V rocket sits on the pad, fully fueled. Gravity pulls it down with about 6.5 million pounds of force, and the pad pushes up just as hard: balanced, so it stays still. The five first-stage engines light, and in a few seconds they reach about 7.5 million pounds of thrust. Now subtract: 7.5 million up minus 6.5 million down leaves about 1 million pounds of net force upward. Unbalanced! The rocket rises, slowly at first. As it burns fuel, the rocket gets lighter, so the same thrust speeds it up faster and faster.

🐢 From the Ais to Artemis

Long before rockets, the Ais people lived along this coast, fishing and gathering shellfish in its lagoons, and Spanish sailors in the 1500s named the point Cañaveral, meaning "a place of canes." By the 1700s, raids by English colonists and their allies and diseases brought by Europeans had destroyed the Ais communities. The first rocket launched from Cape Canaveral in 1950, and in 1958 the first U.S. satellite, Explorer 1, lifted off there. In 1963, after President John F. Kennedy's death, NASA's launch center on Merritt Island was named the John F. Kennedy Space Center, and on July 16, 1969, Apollo 11 left its Launch Complex 39A for the Moon. All 135 space shuttle missions launched from Kennedy between 1981 and 2011, and on April 1, 2026, Artemis II carried four astronauts around the Moon. Most of the land around the launch pads is also the Merritt Island National Wildlife Refuge, home to sea turtles, manatees, alligators and hundreds of kinds of birds.

👁 Watch Me Think

Whose story is this? Most books tell the Space Coast story through astronauts. But who else is part of it? The Ais people lived here first. When NASA bought land on Merritt Island in the early 1960s, more than 1,500 landowners had to sell, and families who lived and farmed there had to move away. Every launch needs thousands of engineers, technicians, weather forecasters, safety workers and cafeteria and cleaning staff. For years, women and Black workers had to fight to be hired and promoted. Asking who is missing from a story gives me a fuller, more honest picture.

🤝 Let's Try Together: Rockets and Sea Turtles Share a Beach

Loggerhead, green and leatherback sea turtles lay their eggs on the beaches near the launch pads. When baby turtles hatch at night, they crawl toward the brightest horizon, which is normally moonlight over the ocean. But bright lights from buildings can lead them the wrong way. So what can a spaceport do? Kennedy Space Center limits and shields outdoor lights near the beach during nesting season and uses lighting that is easier on turtles. Scientists count nests each year to check whether the plan works. Here engineering and ecology work side by side.

👩‍🚀 The People of the Space Coast

Launches take engineers, leaders and astronauts. These five people helped make history at Kennedy Space Center.

JoAnn Morgan

at the Cape until 2003

JoAnn Morgan's family moved to Titusville, Florida, across the water from Cape Canaveral, when she was in high school. At 17 she began working summers at the Cape as an engineering trainee for the Army's rocket program. On July 16, 1969, she was the instrumentation controller for the Apollo 11 launch and the only woman in the launch firing room. Some coworkers did not think a woman belonged there, but she kept proving herself. In more than 45 years at the Cape, she became the first woman senior executive at Kennedy Space Center before retiring in 2003.

Guion "Guy" Bluford

born 1942

Guion Bluford grew up in Philadelphia and became an Air Force fighter pilot and an aerospace engineer with a doctorate. On August 30, 1983, he lifted off from Kennedy Space Center aboard the space shuttle Challenger and became the first African American in space. His launch was also the shuttle program's first night launch, lighting up the dark Florida sky. Bluford flew on four shuttle missions in all.

John Herrington

born 1958

John Herrington was born in Wetumka, Oklahoma, and is a citizen of the Chickasaw Nation. A Navy pilot and engineer, he launched from Kennedy Space Center aboard the space shuttle Endeavour in November 2002 and became the first Chickasaw citizen to fly in space. He carried the Chickasaw Nation flag with him. During the mission he made three spacewalks to help build the International Space Station.

Janet Petro

born 1960

Janet Petro graduated from the U.S. Military Academy at West Point in 1981, in only the second class to include women, and flew helicopters as an Army officer. She later earned a business degree and joined NASA. In 2021 she became the first woman to direct Kennedy Space Center, where companies and NASA now share the launch pads, making it a spaceport for many users. From January to July 2025 she also served as acting head of all of NASA, the first woman to do so. She retired from NASA in 2026.

Victor Glover

born 1976

Victor Glover grew up in Pomona, California, and became a Navy test pilot and engineer. In November 2020 he launched from Kennedy Space Center as pilot of SpaceX's Crew-1 and spent nearly six months on the International Space Station. On April 1, 2026, he lifted off from Kennedy's Launch Complex 39B as pilot of Artemis II. On that mission he became the first Black astronaut to travel around the Moon, alongside Christina Koch, the first woman, and Jeremy Hansen, the first Canadian.
