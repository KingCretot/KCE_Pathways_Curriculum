🏰 St. Augustine and the Castillo

In 1565, Spain founded St. Augustine on Timucua land, near a Timucua town called Seloy. The town guarded the sea route of Spain's treasure ships and watched the English colonies growing to the north. Wooden forts burned or rotted, so from 1672 to 1695 workers built the Castillo de San Marcos from coquina, a rock made of shells. Indigenous workers from nearby Spanish missions did much of the labor, along with skilled builders from Cuba and enslaved Africans. English forces attacked in 1702 and 1740, but the Castillo was never taken by force.

👁 Watch Me Think

I ask: why would Spain spend more than 20 years building one stone fort? St. Augustine sat between Spain's treasure route and the growing English colonies, so the Castillo protected Spain's power. Then I ask who did the hard work. The people who hauled and cut the stone were mostly not the people who decided to build it. Both answers belong in the story.

🧭 Freedom to the South

In 1687, eight men, two women and a nursing child escaped slavery in the English colony of Carolina and reached St. Augustine by boat. In 1693, King Charles II of Spain issued a royal decree: people escaping slavery in the English colonies would be free if they converted to Catholicism and served Spain. Spain had its own reasons, because every freedom seeker weakened the English colonies and could help defend Florida. Yet Spain still held enslaved Africans in Florida and its other colonies. For many enslaved people in Carolina, the road to freedom ran south, not north.

👁 Watch Me Think

I ask: who benefited? Freedom seekers gained freedom, which mattered more than anything. Spain gained defenders and hurt its English rivals. Then I notice a contradiction: Spain offered freedom to people escaping the English, while still enslaving people in its own colonies. So the offer was about Spain's power as much as about fairness.

🛡️ Fort Mose and Captain Francisco Menéndez

Francisco Menéndez was born in West Africa, enslaved in Carolina, and escaped to Florida, but Spanish officials sold him into slavery again. He led St. Augustine's Black militia and sent petitions asking for freedom until, in 1738, Governor Manuel de Montiano freed him and others. Montiano created Gracia Real de Santa Teresa de Mose, or Fort Mose, two miles north of the city, home to about 100 free Black people led by Captain Menéndez. In June 1740, Mose's militia, Spanish soldiers and Indigenous allies retook the fort from British troops in the Battle of Bloody Mose. When Spain gave Florida to Britain in 1763, Mose's families sailed to Cuba.

👁 Watch Me Think

I ask: who decided that Menéndez would be free? The governor made the final choice, so Spain held the power. But Menéndez kept pushing with petitions and service until the answer changed. So freedom at Mose came from a Spanish policy AND from the people who demanded it. In 1763, keeping that freedom meant leaving their homes behind.

🧩 Picture It: Fort Mose at a glance

  • Full name: Gracia Real de Santa Teresa de Mose
  • Where: about two miles north of St. Augustine
  • Founded: 1738, by order of Governor Manuel de Montiano
  • People: 38 men and their families, about 100 people in all
  • Leader: Captain Francisco Menéndez
  • Found by archaeologists: gunflints, musket balls, thimbles, pottery, animal bones, and a small silver medal of Saint Christopher
  • Today: Fort Mose Historic State Park, a National Historic Landmark

📜 Source Detective: The Mose Evidence File

You are a historian. You have three pieces of evidence from Spanish records. The words in quotation marks are English translations of Spanish documents. Decide what each one can and cannot tell you.

Evidence File: Spanish Records About Mose

A. The king's decree (1693)
King Charles II of Spain ordered that people escaping slavery in the English colonies be freed, "giving liberty to all…the men as well as the women…so that by their example and by my liberality others will do the same."

B. The Mose militia's vow
In a declaration sent to the Spanish king, the men of the Mose militia promised to be "the most cruel enemies of the English" and to spill their "last drop of blood in defense of the Great Crown of Spain and the Holy Faith."

C. A Spanish count of Mose (1759)

A: Royal decree of Charles II (1693), as translated and quoted by the National Park Service, Castillo de San Marcos National Monument. B: Declaration of the Mose militia to the Spanish king, as translated in Archaeology magazine, "Freedom Fort" (March/April 2024). C: Spanish census of Mose (1759), as reported by the Florida Museum of Natural History and the National Park Service.

👁 Think Like a Historian

A historian asks: who wrote each document, for whom, and why? All three were written for the Spanish government. The militia's vow was written to a king whose protection they needed, so it shows what they wanted him to believe. And none of these documents tells us what Mose families said to each other at home.

🧭 People of Fort Mose and Spanish Florida

Meet people who shaped, attacked or uncovered the story of Fort Mose. Ask what each one wanted and what each one did.

Francisco Menéndez

active 1715–1760s

A Mandinga man born in West Africa, he was enslaved in Carolina, fought alongside the Yamasee nation in the Yamasee War, and escaped to Florida. Re-enslaved there, he petitioned for years and won his freedom in 1738. As captain of the Mose militia he led the town, and after 1763 he and the Mose families resettled in Cuba.

The freedom seekers of 1687

arrived 1687

Eight men, two women and a nursing child escaped Carolina by boat. In St. Augustine they worked for pay, some on the Castillo. When an English official came to take them back, the Spanish governor refused, saying they had become Catholic and married in the town.

Manuel de Montiano

1685–1762

Spanish governor of Florida from 1737. In 1738 he freed Menéndez and other freedom seekers and founded Fort Mose, partly as a guard post on the northern route English attackers would use. He led St. Augustine's defense during the 1740 siege.

James Oglethorpe

1696–1785

British general who founded the colony of Georgia in 1733. In 1740 he invaded Florida and fired on St. Augustine for 27 days. His troops captured Fort Mose, but they were driven out in the Battle of Bloody Mose, and he retreated to Georgia.

Kathleen Deagan and Jane Landers

Fort Mose research team, 1986–1988

Archaeologist Kathleen Deagan and historian Jane Landers helped lead the team that studied Fort Mose. Archaeologists found the site of the second fort in 1986, and Landers studied Spanish records about Mose's people. Their work helped make Mose a National Historic Landmark in 1994.
