🌀 How a Hurricane Forms

A hurricane forms over warm ocean water, usually at least about 80°F. The Sun heats the ocean, and water evaporates into warm, moist air that rises. As the air rises and cools, the water vapor condenses into towering clouds and gives off heat, which makes the air rise even faster. Rising air leaves low pressure at the ocean surface, so more air rushes in, and Earth's spin turns it into a spiral. In the Northern Hemisphere, hurricanes spin counterclockwise around a calm center called the eye.

👁 Watch Me Think

I connect this to the water cycle. Evaporation carries energy from the warm ocean into the air, and condensation releases that energy inside the clouds. So warm water is the storm's fuel. Now I can predict: what happens when a hurricane moves over land or cooler water? It loses its fuel, so it usually weakens. If warm water feeds the storm, taking the warm water away should weaken it. That is reasoning from evidence.

🧩 Picture It: Inside a Hurricane

  • Eye: the calm center, often about 20 to 40 miles wide. The sky can be clear.
  • Eyewall: a ring of tall thunderstorms around the eye with the strongest winds and heaviest rain.
  • Rain bands: spiral arms of clouds and rain that can stretch hundreds of miles out.
  • Ocean below: warm water, at least about 80°F, that fuels the storm.

🤝 Let's Try Together: From Clouds to Hurricane

Follow the steps together. The Sun warms the ocean. Water evaporates, and warm, moist air rises. The vapor condenses into tall clouds and releases heat. Low pressure at the surface pulls in more air, and Earth's spin turns it. Forecasters name the stages by wind speed: a tropical depression has winds under 39 mph, a tropical storm has winds of 39 to 73 mph and gets a name, and a hurricane has winds of 74 mph or more.

🌊 Measuring a Hurricane: Wind and Storm Surge

Forecasters rank hurricanes with the Saffir-Simpson Hurricane Wind Scale, from Category 1 (winds of 74 to 95 mph) to Category 5 (157 mph or higher). Category 3 and above are called major hurricanes. But the scale measures only wind. Storm surge, ocean water pushed toward the shore by a storm's winds, is often the greatest danger to life in a hurricane. In 2022, Hurricane Ian came ashore in southwest Florida as a Category 4, and its surge flooded Fort Myers Beach with water about 10 to 15 feet above the ground in some spots, and nearby Sanibel Island flooded too.

👁 Watch Me Think

The category tells me about wind, so I ask: what else matters? Storm surge depends on more than wind. It also depends on the storm's size, its path, the tide, and the shape of the sea floor. Southwest Florida's Gulf coast has a wide, shallow sea floor offshore, so water piles up easily. That is why even a lower-category storm can bring a dangerous surge. Heavy rain is a third danger. Ian's rain also flooded rivers far from the coast, including the Peace River.

📡 Forecasting and Staying Safe

The Atlantic hurricane season runs from June 1 to November 30. Scientists at the National Hurricane Center in Miami use satellites, radar, ocean buoys and computer models to forecast where a storm will go. Hurricane hunters fly airplanes into storms and drop instruments called dropsondes, which measure wind, temperature, humidity and pressure as they fall. Forecasts show a cone of uncertainty, the area where the storm's center will probably travel. Families use forecasts to learn their evacuation zone, pack a kit, and leave when officials tell their zone to go.

👁 Watch Me Think

The cone is easy to misread, so I slow down. It shows where the center of the storm will probably go, and the center stays inside the cone only about two-thirds of the time. It does not show how big the storm is, so wind, rain and surge can reach places outside the cone. Evacuation zones are based mostly on storm surge risk. That is why Florida emergency managers say, 'Run from water, hide from wind.' Families in a zone that is told to leave go. Many families outside the zones can shelter in a sturdy home, but mobile homes are not safe in hurricane winds.

🧩 Picture It: Watch, Warning and Kit

AlertWhat it means
Hurricane WatchHurricane conditions are possible, usually issued about 48 hours ahead. Finish getting ready.
Hurricane WarningHurricane conditions are expected, usually issued about 36 hours ahead. Finish preparing and leave if your zone is told to.

Kit: water for each person and pet for several days, food that needs no cooking, flashlights and batteries, medicines, important papers, phone chargers.

🤝 Let's Try Together: Read the Cone Together

Imagine a cone drawn from the Gulf toward Tampa Bay, and your town is just outside its edge. Does that mean you are safe? No. The cone shows the likely path of the storm's center only. Strong winds and heavy rain can reach far outside it. So your family keeps watching official forecasts, checks your evacuation zone, and gets the kit ready.

A forecaster looks for patterns across the whole table, not one row. I compare the winds and the pressure each day, then check what was under the storm: warm water or land. ✈️ People Who Track Hurricanes

Hurricane science takes pilots, engineers, forecasters and researchers. Notice how each person gathered evidence or turned evidence into warnings that help families.

NOAA Hurricane Hunters

Today

NOAA's hurricane hunter airplanes are based in Lakeland, Florida. Two four-engine propeller planes nicknamed Kermit and Miss Piggy fly through the eyewall into the eye, and a jet flies high around storms. Crews of pilots, engineers and scientists use radar and drop dropsondes. In 2019, three women formed NOAA's first all-woman hurricane hunter pilot team, flying the jet on a mission to study Hurricane Dorian.

Karen Moore

Air Force Reserve hurricane hunter

Karen Moore grew up in Norfolk, Virginia, served in the Marine Corps, and later joined the U.S. Air Force Reserve's 53rd Weather Reconnaissance Squadron, the Air Force hurricane hunters based in Mississippi. She has been called the first African American woman to serve as an Air Force hurricane hunter. She flew through Hurricane Maria in 2017. Her advice: 'Go for what you want to do against all odds.'

Lixion Avila

born 1950

Born and raised in Cuba, Lixion Avila studied meteorology at the University of Havana. After moving to the United States, he joined the National Hurricane Center in Miami in 1983, first sharing hurricane warnings in Spanish with radio and TV stations. He became a hurricane specialist in 1989, later earned a Ph.D., and forecast storms there until he retired in 2020.

Shirley Murillo

NOAA hurricane scientist

Shirley Murillo was born and raised in Miami. Seeing what Hurricane Andrew did to South Florida in 1992 sparked her interest in weather science. She studied meteorology at Florida State University and became a research meteorologist at NOAA's Hurricane Research Division in Miami, where she later helped lead the team. She flies on hurricane hunter planes to collect data and helps turn new research into better forecasts. Her advice: 'Take the initiative and ask.'

Herbert Saffir and Robert Simpson

1917–2007 and 1912–2014

Herbert Saffir was an engineer in Coral Gables, Florida, who studied how wind damages buildings. Robert Simpson was a meteorologist who directed the National Hurricane Center. In the early 1970s, they put their knowledge together to create the 1-to-5 hurricane wind scale used today.
