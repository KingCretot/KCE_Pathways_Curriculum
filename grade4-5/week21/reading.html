🖼️ Picture It: How Is This Text Built?

Every informational text has a skeleton you cannot see at first. The way a writer organizes the ideas is called the text structure. A writer picks the structure that fits the purpose: telling what happened over time, explaining why something happened, or showing how two things differ. Signal words work like trail markers. They point to the structure the writer chose.

StructureWhat it doesSignal words
Chronology (time order)Tells events or stages in the order they happenedfirst, at first, next, later, then, finally, in 1906, by 1300
Cause and effectExplains why something happened and what happened as a resultbecause, so, since, as a result, therefore, led to
Problem and solutionNames a problem and how people tried to solve itproblem, challenge, solved, one answer was, to fix this
Compare and contrastShows how two or more things are alike and differentboth, alike, similarly, however, but, unlike, while
DescriptionGives the features, details, or examples of one topicfor example, such as, also, in addition, features
💡

Signal Words Are Clues, Not Proof

One “then” does not make a whole text chronology. Ask: what is the WHOLE text mostly doing? Is it walking through time, explaining why, solving a problem, comparing, or describing? Some texts mix two structures. When that happens, name both and tell which parts use each one.

📖 Text A: From Pithouses to Cliff Dwellings

Yesenia chose this article for the first part of the report. As you read, circle the words in your head that tell WHEN. Notice the paragraph numbers; you will use them.

Text A • Informational ArticleFrom Pithouses to Cliff Dwellings

An original article written for this lesson, using facts from the National Park Service

1Mesa Verde means “green table” in Spanish. It is a high, flat-topped land in southwestern Colorado, cut by deep canyons. For about 700 years, from around 600 to 1300 CE, Ancestral Pueblo people made their homes here. (CE stands for Common Era, the way most calendars count years today.)

2At first, families lived on the mesa tops in pithouses. A pithouse was built partly below the ground and partly above it. Families grew corn, beans, and squash, and they wove baskets so skillfully that archeologists call these early people Basketmakers.

3Later, around 1000 CE, people began building pueblos, or villages, on the mesa tops. These villages had thick stone walls that often rose two or three stories high.

4Then, in the late 1190s, many families moved into alcoves, shallow caves under the overhanging canyon walls. There they built cliff dwellings from sandstone, mortar, and wooden beams. The largest, Cliff Palace, had about 150 rooms and 23 kivas, which were rooms for ceremonies and gatherings. Families reached their fields by climbing hand-and-toe-hold trails pecked into the canyon walls.

5Finally, by about 1300, families had left Mesa Verde and migrated south to what is now New Mexico and Arizona. They had lived in the cliff dwellings for less than 100 years. Their descendants include the Hopi, Zuni, and Acoma people and other Pueblo communities today.

💧 Text B: Living With Little Water

Zane chose this article for the second part of the report. It covers the same people, but the writer built it differently. Look for a problem, the solutions, and the words that tell why.

Text B • Informational ArticleLiving With Little Water

An original article written for this lesson, using facts from the National Park Service

1Mesa Verde is a dry place. Farmers there depended on rain and melting snow for their crops, and the area had few water sources that lasted all year. Finding enough water was one of the biggest challenges of daily life.

2People solved this problem in several ways. Many cliff dwellings were built near seep springs, places where water slowly trickles out of the rock. Most archeologists think that being close to water was a main reason people built in the alcoves. Communities also built reservoirs on the mesa tops to store water.

3In the last 25 years of the 1200s, however, a long drought struck. Because rain and snow were scarce, crops failed, and the mesa-top reservoirs became less reliable. Scientists know about these dry years because the growth rings in old wood samples show them.

4As a result, families had to make hard choices. Archeologists think the drought, worn-out farmland, and problems between communities may all have led people to move. The people did not vanish. They migrated south and built new communities, where their descendants still live.

📝 Summarize by Structure

🪜

Let the Structure Build Your Summary

  1. Name the structure. Look for the signal words, then ask what the whole text is mostly doing.
  2. Pick the matching frame. Chronology: First… later… finally… Cause and effect: Because… as a result… Problem and solution: The problem was… people solved it by… Compare and contrast: Both… but… Description: The topic is… its main features are…
  3. Fill the frame with big ideas only. Keep the stages, causes, or solutions. Cut small numbers and examples unless the main idea needs them.
  4. Cut and check: use your own words, add no opinions, and don’t copy 4 or more words in a row.
🧪

Worked Example: Summarizing Text B

❌ Weak: Mesa Verde is dry. There are springs. Scientists have wood samples. Cliff Palace is really cool.
It lists facts with no connections, so the reader never learns WHY anything happened. It also adds an opinion and a detail from a different text.

✅ Strong: Water was scarce at Mesa Verde, so people built homes near springs and stored water in reservoirs. When a long drought hit in the late 1200s, crops failed, and as a result families moved south.
Problem, solutions, cause, and effect, in that order, with signal words that show how the ideas connect.

🎯

Wrong Frame, Lost Meaning

If you squeeze a cause-and-effect text into a “first, second, third” list, you lose the why. If you squeeze a timeline into a “both… but…” frame, you lose the order. Match the frame to the structure.

📝 Writer’s Turn: Summarize by Structure

🔄

Your Summary Is Checked by Three Fair Rules

Your summary must (1) have enough words to cover the big ideas, (2) not copy 4 or more words in a row from the text (names like Mesa Verde and Ancestral Pueblo can stay), and (3) include every key idea listed. Use the frame that matches the structure. Write one or two sentences. If a rule is missed, the feedback tells you which one.

+6

Summarize ALL of Text A in one or two sentences, in your own words. Use a time-order frame (first… later… finally…).

Text A, key lines: At first, families lived on the mesa tops in pithouses… Later, around 1000 CE, people began building pueblos, or villages, on the mesa tops… Then, in the late 1190s, many families moved into alcoves… Finally, by about 1300, families had left Mesa Verde and migrated south…
  • Use at least 18 words.
  • Do not copy 4 or more words in a row from the text. (Names like Mesa Verde and Ancestral Pueblo are fine to keep.)
  • Include the first stage: pithouses.
  • Include the second stage: stone villages (pueblos) on the mesa.
  • Include the third stage: homes in the cliffs or alcoves.
  • Include the last stage: people migrated or moved south.

+6

Summarize paragraphs 1 and 2 of Text B in one or two sentences, in your own words. Use a problem-and-solution frame.

Text B, paragraphs 1–2: Mesa Verde is a dry place… Finding enough water was one of the biggest challenges of daily life. People solved this problem in several ways. Many cliff dwellings were built near seep springs… Communities also built reservoirs on the mesa tops to store water.
  • Use at least 15 words.
  • Do not copy 4 or more words in a row from the text. (Single words like springs and reservoirs are fine to keep.)
  • Name what was hard to find: water.
  • State the problem: the land was dry or water was scarce.
  • Give solution 1: building near springs.
  • Give solution 2: storing water in reservoirs.

+6

Summarize paragraphs 3 and 4 of Text B in one or two sentences, in your own words. Use a cause-and-effect frame.

Text B, paragraphs 3–4: In the last 25 years of the 1200s, however, a long drought struck. Because rain and snow were scarce, crops failed… As a result, families had to make hard choices… They migrated south and built new communities, where their descendants still live.
  • Use at least 15 words.
  • Do not copy 4 or more words in a row from the text.
  • Name the cause: the drought.
  • Name one effect: the crops failed.
  • Name the final effect: people moved south.
  • Use a cause-and-effect signal word such as because, so, or as a result.
