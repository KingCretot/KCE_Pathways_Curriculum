🔍 Clues, Not Proof: Spotting a Chemical Change

In Week 11 you learned that a chemical change makes a new substance with new properties. Scientists also call it a chemical reaction. You cannot watch a new substance being made, so you look for clues: a gas forming when nothing is boiling, a new color, a new smell, light or heat given off, or a precipitate, a solid that forms when two liquids are mixed. But a clue is not proof. Boiling water makes bubbles, yet the bubbles are water vapor, so boiling is a physical change. Mixing blue and yellow paint makes green, but no new substance forms. Dissolving sugar in water is a physical change too. Many people think a change is physical if you can undo it and chemical if you cannot, but that test does not always work. The real test is one question: did a new substance with new properties form? Scientists look for more than one clue and test the properties before they decide.

👁 Watch Me Think

At Aunt Bernadette's house, a pot of water bubbles on the stove while she cooks. A minute later, Boaz drops a fizzing antacid tablet into a glass of cold water, and it bubbles too. Are both chemical changes? Bubbles are only a clue, so I ask where the bubbles come from. In the pot, heat turns liquid water into water vapor. That is still water, so it is a physical change. The cold glass is not boiling, so its bubbles must be a new gas. The tablet's ingredients react with water and make carbon dioxide, a substance that was not there before. Same clue, two different stories. I decide only after I ask: is there a new substance?

🤝 Let's Try Together: Judge the Pancake Evidence

Let's judge the evidence while Bianca helps Aunt Bernadette make pancakes. Step 1: the butter melts in the warm pan. It is still butter in a new state, so that is a physical change. Step 2: she stirs sugar into the milk and it dissolves. The sugar is still there, so that is physical too. Step 3: the batter has baking powder, a mix of baking soda and an acid. On the hot pan, tiny bubbles appear without the batter boiling. The baking powder reacts and makes carbon dioxide gas, which helps the pancakes rise. That is chemical. Step 4: the pancake turns golden brown and smells toasty. A new color and a new smell together point to new substances, so browning is chemical. Two clues that agree make strong evidence.

⚛️ Atoms Rearrange: Why Mass Is Conserved

All matter is made of tiny particles called atoms, far too small to see. In a chemical reaction, the starting substances are the reactants, and the new substances made are the products. During the reaction, the atoms of the reactants come apart and join up in new ways, like taking apart a model built from toy bricks and using the very same bricks to build something new. No new atoms are made and no atoms are destroyed, so the total mass of the products equals the total mass of the reactants. That is why mass is conserved. Then why does a burning candle get lighter? Candle wax reacts with oxygen from the air. The products are carbon dioxide and water vapor, two invisible gases that drift away. Gases have mass, even though you cannot see them. An open system lets matter get in or out. A closed system is a container that nothing can get into or out of, so it is the fair way to test conservation of mass.

👁 Watch Me Think

Aunt Bernadette sets a candle on a scale, and a grown-up lights it. It starts at 40 grams. Later it weighs 37 grams. Were 3 grams of matter destroyed? No. The wax joined with oxygen and became carbon dioxide and water vapor, gases that floated off the scale. Then she shows a surprise from her classroom: when steel wool burns, it gets HEAVIER. Did that reaction make new matter? No again. Oxygen atoms from the air joined the iron atoms and stayed on the scale as a new solid. In the candle, matter left the open system. In the steel wool, matter came in from the air. It is the same rule working in two directions.

🧩 Picture It: Same Rule, Two Directions

ChangeReactantsProductsWhat the scale shows (open dish)
Candle burningWax and oxygenCarbon dioxide and water vapor (gases)Mass goes down: the gases leave
Steel wool burningIron and oxygenIron oxide (a solid)Mass goes up: oxygen joins from the air
Any reaction in a sealed containerAll trapped insideAll trapped insideMass stays the same

If the mass changes, look for matter that moved in or out. It was never created or destroyed.

🤝 Let's Try Together: Do the Atom Math

Let's do the atom math together. Imagine a model built from 6 red bricks and 4 blue bricks. You take it apart and build a new shape. How many bricks now? Still 10, just arranged differently. Atoms work the same way in a reaction. Now let's use grams. In a sealed jar, 50 grams of reactants react. What do the products weigh? Exactly 50 grams, because nothing got in or out. Next, the same reaction happens in an open cup and makes a gas. Before, the cup weighs 50 grams. After, it weighs 46 grams. Subtract: 50 minus 46 is 4. So 4 grams of gas escaped into the air. The gas was not destroyed. It just was not on the scale anymore.

🗽 Chemical Changes All Around Us

Chemical changes happen all around you. When the Statue of Liberty was unveiled in New York Harbor in 1886, its copper skin was the shiny brown color of a new penny. Over many years, the copper reacted with oxygen, water, carbon dioxide and other substances in the air, forming a new blue-green layer called a patina. By about 1920, the statue was fully green. The patina sticks tightly and helps protect the copper underneath. Rust is different. When iron reacts with oxygen and water, it forms rust, which does not protect the iron, so the iron keeps rusting. That is why people paint steel bridges or coat iron with a metal called zinc. In the kitchen, baking powder makes gas bubbles that help batter rise, and browning toast makes new substances with new flavors and smells. A glow stick gives off light when two liquids inside it mix and react. Inside your body, digestion uses chemical reactions to break food into smaller substances your cells can use.

👁 Watch Me Think

On a ferry ride to Liberty Island, Bianca asks why the statue did not crumble the way the rusty chain on her old bike did. Both are chemical changes with substances from the air, so why are the results so different? The answer is in the properties of the products. The patina is a new substance that sticks tightly to the copper like a shield. Rust is a new substance that does not protect the iron, so fresh iron underneath keeps reacting. So whether a chemical change helps or harms depends on what new substance forms and what its properties are.

🤝 Let's Try Together: Make Pennies Shine (Try It at Home)

Try It at Home, only with a grown-up's help. In a glass or plastic bowl, not a metal one, stir 1 teaspoon of salt into one quarter cup of white vinegar until it dissolves. Add about 10 dull pennies and wait 5 minutes. The dull coating is copper oxide, which formed when copper reacted with oxygen in the air. The vinegar and salt react with that coating and dissolve it, so the pennies shine. Now rinse half of the pennies well under running water and dry them. Set the other half on a paper towel without rinsing. After about an hour, compare them. The unrinsed pennies may grow a blue-green coating, a new copper substance, a little like the statue's patina. Safety rules: a grown-up must help the whole time, keep the vinegar away from your eyes, never put the pennies or the liquid in your mouth, pour the liquid down the sink when you finish and wash your hands.

👁 Think Like a Chemist

A chemist never decides that matter vanished. For each trial she asks: Was the system open or closed? Could a gas escape, or could a gas from the air join in? If the mass changed, she finds the matter that moved. If the mass stayed the same, she checks that the container was sealed. The rule does not break. The matter just moves.

🧪 Scientists Who Followed the Atoms

In Week 11 you met Antoine and Marie-Anne Lavoisier. These four scientists, from Russia, the United States and Egypt, weighed, studied and even watched chemical changes.

Mikhail Lomonosov

1711–1765

Mikhail Lomonosov was born in a village near Arkhangelsk in northern Russia, the son of a fisherman. At 19, he rode with a sleigh convoy carrying frozen fish all the way to Moscow so he could go to school. He became a scientist, a poet and a teacher, and he helped create Moscow University, founded in 1755. In 1756 he heated metal inside a sealed glass container and found that the total weight of the container and everything in it stayed the same. That was early evidence for conservation of mass, years before Antoine Lavoisier's famous experiments.

Marie Maynard Daly

1921–2003

Marie Maynard Daly was born in Queens, New York. In 1947 she became the first Black woman in the United States to earn a doctoral degree (PhD), the highest university degree, in chemistry, from Columbia University. Her research studied how amylase, a digestive substance made by the pancreas, breaks down corn starch. Those are chemical changes that happen when we digest food. Later she studied parts of the cell nucleus and the link between cholesterol and heart attacks. In 1988 she started a scholarship at Queens College for students studying science.

Percy Julian

1899–1975

Percy Julian was born in Montgomery, Alabama, and graduated first in his class at DePauw University in 1920. In 1935 he and Josef Pikl made physostigmine in the lab, a medicine still used to treat glaucoma, an eye disease. At the Glidden Company, he used chemical reactions to turn substances from soybeans into medicines. He made progesterone, a hormone, in large amounts in 1939 and later helped make cortisone, a medicine for arthritis, much cheaper. He faced racism throughout his career. In 1973 he became the second African American elected to the National Academy of Sciences.

Ahmed Zewail

1946–2016

Ahmed Zewail was born in Damanhur, Egypt, and became a professor at the California Institute of Technology in the United States. Atoms in a molecule move unbelievably fast, vibrating every 10 to 100 femtoseconds. A femtosecond is one millionth of a billionth of a second. Zewail used laser flashes only a few femtoseconds long, like a camera with an incredibly fast shutter, to watch atoms move as one substance changed into another. In 1999 he won the Nobel Prize in Chemistry for this work, called femtochemistry.
