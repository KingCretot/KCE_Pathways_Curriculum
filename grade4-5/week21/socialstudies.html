🌍 Why the United States Went to War

World War I began in Europe in 1914. On one side were the Allies, including France, Britain and Russia. On the other side were the Central Powers, including Germany and Austria-Hungary. At first, President Woodrow Wilson kept the United States neutral. Then events pulled the country in. On May 7, 1915, a German submarine sank the British ocean liner Lusitania; 1,198 people died, including 128 Americans. In early 1917, Germany began attacking ships without warning, and American ships were sunk. Then Britain revealed the Zimmermann Telegram, a secret German message asking Mexico to join the war and win back Texas, New Mexico and Arizona. Wilson gave it to newspapers on March 1, 1917. On April 2, 1917, Wilson asked Congress to declare war, saying, "The world must be made safe for democracy." On April 6, 1917, the United States entered World War I.

👁 Watch Me Think

Wilson said the war was for democracy. A historian asks: was democracy safe at home? In 1917, most women still could not vote, and Jim Crow laws blocked many Black Americans from voting. The Silent Sentinels from Week 19 pointed out this gap. On a banner in August 1917, they called the president "Kaiser Wilson," comparing him to the Kaiser, the emperor of Germany. Angry crowds tore up their banners, but the women kept picketing. So I ask: can a country fight for democracy abroad while it denies democracy at home?

🎖️ The Harlem Hellfighters and Soldiers of Many Backgrounds

More than 380,000 Black Americans served in the military during the war, and the military was strictly segregated. Most Black soldiers were assigned to labor units, building roads and unloading ships. One famous combat unit began as the 15th New York National Guard, an all-Black unit, and became the 369th Infantry Regiment. Because many white American soldiers refused to serve beside Black troops, the army placed the 369th with the French army. The soldiers, later known as the Harlem Hellfighters, spent 191 days in front-line trenches, more than any other American unit of its size. France awarded its Croix de Guerre ("cross of war") medal to 171 of them. The regiment's band, led by Lieutenant James Reese Europe, helped spread jazz across France. On February 17, 1919, about 3,000 members marched up Fifth Avenue in New York City as crowds cheered.

👁 Watch Me Think

Whose story is this, and who was left out of it? On May 15, 1918, Private Henry Johnson and Private Needham Roberts of the 369th fought off a German raid even though both were badly wounded. France gave Johnson the Croix de Guerre with Palm, its highest award for bravery. The United States did not give him the Medal of Honor until June 2, 2015, almost 100 years later. Soldiers from other groups served too. About 12,000 Native Americans served, though many were not yet citizens. In October 1918, Choctaw soldiers sent army messages in the Choctaw language, which German listeners could not understand. So I ask: why did it take so long to honor some soldiers?

🏭 The Home Front and the Great Migration

The war changed life on the home front. On March 21, 1917, Loretta Walsh became the first woman sworn in as a chief yeoman in the Navy, and more than 11,000 women served as Yeomen (F) during the war. The army sent 223 women telephone operators, called the Hello Girls, to connect calls in France. Factories needed workers, and the Great Migration you met in Week 17 grew faster. The Chicago Defender, a Black newspaper founded by Robert S. Abbott in 1905, printed job listings and train schedules and named May 15, 1917, the start of a "Great Northern Drive." Pullman railroad porters carried copies into the South. At least 50,000 Black Southerners moved to Chicago between 1916 and 1920, and families also moved to cities in the Northeast and the West.

👁 Watch Me Think

Did the North keep its promise? Many migrants found better-paying jobs. But they also met segregation in housing, and most labor unions shut them out. In the summer of 1919, white mobs attacked Black neighborhoods in Chicago and other cities. James Weldon Johnson of the National Association for the Advancement of Colored People called it the "Red Summer." Many Black veterans who had fought for democracy in France now had to fight for it at home. So I weigh both sides: the North offered new chances, and it also had its own barriers.

🔍 Source Detective: Three Voices on Democracy, 1917 to 1919

Here are three short primary sources: a war speech from 1917, a speech to the Senate from 1918 and a magazine editorial from 1919. Read each part, then compare them.

Primary Sources: 1917, 1918 and 1919

Part 1 (President Woodrow Wilson, April 2, 1917): "The world must be made safe for democracy."

Part 2 (President Woodrow Wilson to the Senate, September 30, 1918): "We have made partners of the women in this war."

Part 3 (W. E. B. Du Bois, "Returning Soldiers," May 1919): "This is the country to which we Soldiers of Democracy return." ... "We return. We return from fighting. We return fighting. Make way for Democracy!"

Word help: democracy = government by the people, where people have a voice and a vote; partners = people who share the work; Soldiers of Democracy = Black soldiers who had fought for democracy in France; make way = clear the path.

Part 1: Address to Congress asking for a declaration of war against Germany (National Archives). Part 2: Address to the Senate on the women's suffrage amendment (United States Senate). Part 3: W. E. B. Du Bois, "Returning Soldiers," The Crisis, May 1919, the magazine of the National Association for the Advancement of Colored People. Real wording; short parts.

👁 Think Like a Historian

First I check who is speaking: a president twice, then a Black writer and leader. Then I check when. Part 1 comes as the war begins, Part 2 near its end, and Part 3 after the soldiers came home. All three use the idea of democracy. In Part 2, Wilson uses women's war work as a reason they should vote. In Part 3, Du Bois turns Wilson's promise around: if Black soldiers saved democracy in France, America must now give it to them at home.

🎖️ People Who Served and Spoke Up

Some fought, some worked, some wrote. Read each card and ask: how did this person serve, and what did they have to push against?

Henry Johnson

Soldier, 369th Infantry Regiment

Born in Winston-Salem, North Carolina, he worked as a porter in Albany, New York, before joining the all-Black 15th New York National Guard. On May 15, 1918, he and Needham Roberts fought off a German raid while badly wounded. France gave him its Croix de Guerre with Palm. He died in 1929. President Barack Obama awarded him the Medal of Honor on June 2, 2015.

Addie Waites Hunton

YMCA worker in France, 1918 to 1919

She was one of only 19 Black women sent to France as YMCA workers to serve Black soldiers. At Camp Lusitania in St. Nazaire, she ran the library, wrote letters for soldiers and taught soldiers to read and write. With Kathryn Johnson, she wrote a book about their work, Two Colored Women with the American Expeditionary Forces, published around 1920.

Marcelino Serna

Born 1896, Chihuahua, Mexico

He moved to the United States and lived in El Paso, Texas. He enlisted in the army at age 20. When officers learned he was not a citizen, they offered to send him home, but he chose to stay and fight. General John J. Pershing gave him the Distinguished Service Cross, and France gave him two Croix de Guerre medals.

Robert S. Abbott

Newspaper founder

Born on St. Simons Island, Georgia, he started the Chicago Defender in 1905. It became the first African American newspaper to sell more than 100,000 copies. The Defender printed job listings and train schedules to help Black Southerners move north, and two-thirds of its readers lived outside Chicago.

Loretta Perfectus Walsh

Sailor, United States Navy

On March 21, 1917, she was sworn in as a chief yeoman, the first woman to serve as a chief petty officer in the Navy. More than 11,000 women followed her as Yeomen (F), nicknamed "Yeomanettes," doing office and communications work so more men could go to sea.
