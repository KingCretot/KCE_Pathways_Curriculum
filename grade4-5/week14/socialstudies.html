The years from 1865 to 1877 when the nation rebuilt the South and decided what freedom and citizenship would mean.
freedpeople
People who had been enslaved and were now free. Older records often say "freedmen."
Freedmen's Bureau
An agency Congress created in 1865 to help freedpeople with food, schools, work contracts and finding family.
citizenship
Being a full member of a nation, with rights the government must protect. The 14th Amendment made everyone born in the U.S. a citizen.
Black Codes
Laws passed by Southern states in 1865 and 1866 that limited freedpeople's rights, like where they could work and travel.
sharecropping
Farming someone else's land and paying with part of the crop. Many freedpeople became sharecroppers and fell into debt.
transcontinental
Crossing a whole continent. The Transcontinental Railroad linked the East and West coasts in 1869.

📜 Juneteenth and Three New Amendments

President Lincoln was assassinated in April 1865, just days after the main Confederate army surrendered. Over the next five years, Congress and the states added three amendments, often called the Reconstruction Amendments. The 13th Amendment (1865) ended slavery, except as punishment for a crime. The 14th Amendment (1868) made everyone born in the United States a citizen and promised every person "the equal protection of the laws." The 15th Amendment (1870) said the vote could not be denied because of race or color, but women of every race still could not vote.

👁 Watch Me Think

I read each amendment as an answer to a problem. Problem one was slavery, so the 13th ended it. Problem two: Southern states passed Black Codes to control freedpeople, and an 1857 Supreme Court case had said Black Americans could not be citizens, so the 14th made them citizens with equal protection. Problem three was who gets a voice, so the 15th protected Black men's votes. Then I look for the gaps: the 13th left an exception for crime, and the 15th left out women.

🧩 Picture It: The Reconstruction Amendments

ChangeWhat it promised, and the gap
13th, 1865Ended slavery. Gap: allowed forced labor as punishment for a crime.
14th, 1868Citizenship for all born in the U.S. and equal protection of the laws. Gap: states found ways around it for decades.
15th, 1870No denying the vote because of race or color. Gap: women still could not vote, and poll taxes and tests later blocked Black voters.

🤝 Let's Try Together: Juneteenth: Galveston, June 19, 1865

The war's main fighting ended in April 1865, but news of freedom did not reach every enslaved person at once. On June 19, 1865, Union General Gordon Granger arrived in Galveston, Texas, and announced that all enslaved people in Texas were free, about 250,000 people by most estimates. Let's do the date math: the Emancipation Proclamation took effect on January 1, 1863. From then until June 19, 1865 is about two and a half years. Freedom needed Union troops to enforce it. Freedpeople in Texas began celebrating June 19 the next year and called it Juneteenth. In 2021 it became a federal holiday. Slavery in the last border states ended when the 13th Amendment was ratified in December 1865.

🏫 Freedom in Action: Schools and Black Lawmakers

Freedpeople acted on freedom right away: they searched for family members who had been sold away, made their marriages legal, built churches and opened schools. The Freedmen's Bureau, created by Congress in 1865, helped open schools across the South and colleges such as Howard University (1867). Under new laws and the 15th Amendment, Black men voted in large numbers, and more than 1,500 Black men held public office during Reconstruction. In 1870, Hiram Revels of Mississippi became the first Black U.S. senator, and Joseph Rainey of South Carolina became the first Black member of the House of Representatives.

👁 Watch Me Think

I notice who started the schools. The Freedmen's Bureau helped, but freedpeople themselves raised money, built schoolhouses and filled them with children and adults. Learning to read had been against the law for enslaved people in many states, so reading was a way to claim freedom. When I read the table, I look for change over time: did the share who could not read go up or down?

🤝 Let's Try Together: Reading the School Table

Let's read the table step by step. In 1870, five years after slavery ended, about 80 percent of Black Americans could not read, because most had been kept from learning. By 1900, the share had fallen to about 45 percent. That is a drop of about 35 percentage points in 30 years. Now let's reason: what caused the change? The table cannot tell us by itself, so we connect it to other evidence: thousands of schools opened by freedpeople, churches, the Freedmen's Bureau and teachers like Charlotte Forten. A historian says the evidence suggests schools made a big difference, and still asks what else might explain the change.

🚂 The Railroad and the End of Reconstruction

In 1862, Congress backed two companies to build a transcontinental railroad. The Central Pacific built east from Sacramento, California, and most of its workers were Chinese immigrants who blasted tunnels through the Sierra Nevada; the Union Pacific built west from Omaha, Nebraska, with many Irish immigrants and Civil War veterans. The lines met at Promontory Summit, Utah Territory, on May 10, 1869. Meanwhile in the South, groups like the Ku Klux Klan used threats and violence to stop Black men from voting, and many Northern voters lost interest in Reconstruction. After a disputed election in 1876, the Compromise of 1877 made Rutherford B. Hayes president, federal troops left the South, and Reconstruction ended.

👁 Watch Me Think

I ask two questions: who built it, and who paid the cost? Chinese and Irish workers did the dangerous work. The railroad also cut across the homelands of Indigenous nations like the Lakota, Cheyenne and Shoshone, and it brought settlers and hunters who nearly wiped out the bison those nations depended on. For Reconstruction, I ask: what was promised, and what was taken back? After 1877, Southern states used poll taxes, reading tests and violence to take away Black men's votes, and the full promise of the 14th and 15th Amendments was not enforced until the civil rights laws of the 1960s.

🤝 Let's Try Together: Who Built the Railroad, and Who Is Missing?

Chinese workers, most from Guangdong province, made up most of the Central Pacific's workforce. They were paid less than white workers and had to buy their own food. In June 1867, about 3,000 of them stopped work to ask for equal pay and shorter hours in the dangerous tunnels. The company cut off their food, and after about a week they went back to work. Let's weigh it: they did not win, but it was one of the largest strikes in the country up to that time. Now look at the famous photo taken at Promontory in 1869: it shows crowds of workers and officials, but Chinese workers are not in it. A historian asks: whose story does the photo tell, and who is missing?

🔍 Source Detective: A Freedman Writes Back

In August 1865, Jourdon Anderson, who had gained his freedom during the war, was working for wages in Dayton, Ohio, with his wife Mandy and their children. His former enslaver in Tennessee wrote and asked him to come back. Anderson's reply was printed in newspapers. Read three short parts.

Primary Source: Jourdon Anderson's letter to his former enslaver, August 7, 1865

Part 1: "I got your letter, and was glad to find that you had not forgotten Jourdon, and that you wanted me to come back and live with you again, promising to do better for me than anybody else can."

Part 2: "I served you faithfully for thirty-two years, and Mandy twenty years. At twenty-five dollars a month for me, and two dollars a week for Mandy, our earnings would amount to eleven thousand six hundred and eighty dollars."

Part 3: "The great desire of my life now is to give my children an education, and have them form virtuous habits."

Word help: faithfully = loyally, without stopping; amount to = add up to; eleven thousand six hundred and eighty dollars = $11,680; desire = wish; virtuous = good and honest.

Jourdon Anderson to P. H. Anderson, dated Dayton, Ohio, August 7, 1865; Anderson dictated it to his employer, Valentine Winters. First printed in the Cincinnati Commercial, 1865, then reprinted in the New-York Daily Tribune and other papers. Real wording; three short parts.

👁 Think Like a Historian

First I check who is writing to whom. A freedman is writing to the man who enslaved him, and now he can speak freely. Then I watch his strategy. He stays polite, but he adds up the wages he was never paid. When a writer turns years of unpaid work into dollars, he is showing what slavery took. Finally I ask: what does he want now? That tells me what freedom meant to him.

🏛️ People Who Built Freedom After the War

Some built freedom in Congress and statehouses, some in classrooms, and some with their hands. Read each card and ask: what did this person or group build, and what did they have to fight against?

Hiram Revels

1827–1901

Born free in Fayetteville, North Carolina, he became a minister and teacher and served as a Union army chaplain. In February 1870, he became the first Black U.S. senator, representing Mississippi. Some senators argued he had not been a citizen long enough, because of an 1857 ruling that Black Americans were not citizens, but the Senate seated him. In 1871 he became the first president of Alcorn University, a college for Black students.

Robert Smalls in Congress

1839–1915

After his escape on the Planter, Smalls became a lawmaker. At South Carolina's 1868 constitutional convention he pushed for free public schools for all children. He served in the state legislature and then in the U.S. House of Representatives for five terms, starting in 1875. In 1895, he was one of six Black delegates who spoke out against a new state constitution written to take away Black men's votes.

Charlotte Forten

1837–1914

Born free in Philadelphia to an abolitionist family, she became a teacher. In 1862, during the war, she went to St. Helena Island, South Carolina, to teach children and adults who had just been freed from slavery. She wrote about the school in her diary and in a magazine article, "Life on the Sea Islands," in 1864.

Chinese Railroad Workers of the Central Pacific

1860s

Thousands of workers, mostly from Guangdong province in China, did most of the Central Pacific's work, including blasting tunnels through the Sierra Nevada in snow and cold. They were paid less than white workers and went on strike in 1867. They were left out of the famous 1869 photo, but in 2014 the U.S. Department of Labor honored them in its Hall of Honor.

The Freedpeople of Galveston

1865 onward

After General Granger's announcement on June 19, 1865, freedpeople in Texas began celebrating each year with prayer, food and speeches. In 1872, Black leaders in Houston pooled their money to buy land for Emancipation Park, a place to hold the celebration. Texas made Juneteenth a state holiday in 1980, and it became a national holiday in 2021.
