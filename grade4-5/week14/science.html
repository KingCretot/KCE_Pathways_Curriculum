🧩 Cells to Systems: The Body's Teamwork

Your body is built in levels. The smallest living unit is the cell, and your body has trillions of them. Cells of the same kind work together as a tissue, such as muscle tissue, and different tissues join to form an organ, such as the heart, lungs, brain, stomach or skin. Organs that work together on one big job form a body system: the digestive system breaks food into nutrients, the skeletal system supports and protects, the muscular system moves you, and the nervous system carries messages. No system works alone; each one depends on the others.

👁 Watch Me Think

When I run to catch a ball, which systems are working? My eyes see the ball, and nerves carry that message to my brain: that is the nervous system. My brain sends signals to my muscles, and they pull on my bones: the muscular and skeletal systems. I start breathing faster, which is the respiratory system, and my heart beats faster to move blood, which is the circulatory system. Even my digestive system helped earlier by getting nutrients from my breakfast. One simple catch needs at least six systems! When I explain the body, I trace the chain step by step.

🤝 Let's Try Together: Put the Levels in Order

Let's build a body from the smallest piece to the biggest. Start with one heart muscle cell. Many heart muscle cells together make heart muscle tissue. Heart muscle tissue, plus other tissues like nerve tissue and the tissue lining the inside, makes an organ: the heart. The heart, the blood and the blood vessels together make the circulatory system. And all the systems together make a whole living person, an organism. So the order is: cell → tissue → organ → system → organism. Notice that each level is made of the level before it.

🫀 Heart and Lungs: Delivering Oxygen

Every cell in your body needs oxygen to release energy from food. When you breathe in, air travels down your windpipe into your lungs, which are filled with millions of tiny air sacs. Oxygen passes from those air sacs into blood in tiny blood vessels called capillaries, while carbon dioxide, a waste gas, passes the other way so you can breathe it out. Your heart, a muscle about the size of your fist, pumps oxygen-rich blood through arteries to every part of your body, and veins carry blood back to the heart. When you exercise, your muscles need more oxygen, so your heart beats faster and you breathe faster.

👁 Watch Me Think

How do we know blood travels through the lungs? For more than 1,000 years, many doctors followed the Greek doctor Galen, who taught that blood seeped through tiny invisible holes in the wall in the middle of the heart. In the 1200s, the Arab doctor Ibn al-Nafis looked at the evidence: that wall is thick and solid, with no holes. So he reasoned that blood must leave the heart, travel through the lungs to meet the air, and then return. In 1628, the English doctor William Harvey used careful experiments to show that the heart pumps blood around the body in a loop. Even a famous expert's idea has to match the evidence.

🧩 Picture It: The Path of a Breath

StepWhat happens
1You breathe in air through your nose or mouth.
2Air travels down your windpipe into your lungs.
3Oxygen moves from tiny air sacs into the blood in capillaries.
4The heart pumps oxygen-rich blood through arteries to your cells.
5Cells use the oxygen and make carbon dioxide as waste.
6Veins carry blood back to the heart, then to the lungs, where you breathe out the carbon dioxide.

🤝 Let's Try Together: Find Your Pulse

Each heartbeat pushes a wave of blood through your arteries, and you can feel it as your pulse. Let's measure it. Gently press two fingers, not your thumb, on the inside of your wrist below your thumb, or on the side of your neck. When you feel the beat, count the beats for 15 seconds while someone watches a clock. Then multiply by 4 to get beats per minute. Suppose you count 22 beats in 15 seconds: 22 × 4 = 88 beats per minute. Why multiply by 4? Because there are four 15-second periods in one minute.

🛡️ Defending and Caring for the Body

Your immune system defends your body against germs, such as some bacteria and viruses. Your skin is the first barrier, and white blood cells in your blood find and destroy germs that get inside. After your body fights off a germ, it can remember it and fight faster next time, and vaccines safely train the immune system to recognize a germ before you meet it. Scientists have found evidence that daily habits help all your body systems: washing hands with soap for at least 20 seconds, getting enough sleep (most children ages 6 to 12 need about 9 to 12 hours a night), moving and playing actively, drinking water, and eating a variety of foods like fruits, vegetables, grains and proteins. Every body is different, and healthy habits are about helping your systems work well, not about looking a certain way.

👁 Watch Me Think

How do we know handwashing matters? In 1847, a doctor named Ignaz Semmelweis worked in a hospital in Vienna, Austria. He noticed that many new mothers in one ward were getting very sick, and that the doctors there often came straight from other work without washing. He asked them to wash their hands in a cleaning solution first. The number of mothers who got sick dropped sharply. Many doctors did not believe him at first, because germs had not yet been discovered. Today we know he was right. Good evidence can be true even before people understand why.

🤝 Let's Try Together: Check the Claim

An ad says: "Kids who drink SuperJuice get fewer colds!" Should we believe it? Let's ask a scientist's questions. How many kids were studied, 5 or 5,000? Was there a comparison group of kids who did not drink SuperJuice? Were both groups alike in other ways, like sleep and handwashing? Who paid for the study: the company that sells the juice? If the ad cannot answer these questions, the claim is not strong evidence. A fair test compares two similar groups and changes only one thing.

👁 Think Like a Physiologist

A physiologist studies how the body works. She knows that every body is different, so she does not compare one student's resting heart rate with another's. Instead, she compares each student with themselves: how much did the rate go up during exercise, and how quickly did it come back down? Then she looks for a pattern that is true for everyone.

🩺 Doctors and Scientists Who Changed Medicine

From the 1200s to today, these people used evidence to understand the body and to save lives, sometimes while facing unfair barriers.

Charles Drew

1904–1950

Charles Drew grew up in Washington, D.C., and was a star athlete and a top student. As a surgeon and researcher, he studied how to store blood and showed that plasma, the liquid part of blood, could be kept much longer than whole blood. In 1940 he was medical director of the "Blood for Britain" program, which shipped plasma to help people hurt in World War II, and he became the first Black American to earn a Doctor of Science in Medicine degree from Columbia University. In 1941 he directed the American Red Cross's first blood bank. The armed forces first refused blood from Black donors, then accepted it only if it was kept separate. Drew said publicly that there was no scientific reason for this rule. He returned to Howard University, where he led the surgery department and trained many Black surgeons.

Daniel Hale Williams

1856–1931

Daniel Hale Williams was born in Hollidaysburg, Pennsylvania, and became a surgeon in Chicago. In 1891, when Black doctors and nurses were often shut out of hospitals, he founded Provident Hospital, which welcomed patients of every race and trained Black nurses. In 1893 he operated on a man named James Cornish, who had a serious chest wound, and repaired the sac around his heart. Cornish recovered and lived for many years. It was one of the first successful operations on the heart recorded in the United States. Williams later led Freedmen's Hospital in Washington, D.C., and in 1913 became the only Black founding member of the American College of Surgeons.

Ibn al-Nafis

about 1213–1288

Ibn al-Nafis was an Arab doctor who was born near Damascus, in what is now Syria, and later worked in Cairo, Egypt. He wrote books about medicine and the human body. In the 1200s, he described how blood travels from the heart through the lungs, where it mixes with air, and then returns to the heart. He reached this idea by looking at evidence, and it disagreed with what the famous Greek doctor Galen had taught. Ibn al-Nafis described this path about 300 years before European doctors did.

Kizzmekia Corbett

born 1986

Kizzmekia Corbett grew up in a small town in North Carolina and loved science from an early age. She earned a doctorate studying the immune system and became a scientist at the National Institutes of Health. In 2020, she was a lead scientist on the team that designed one of the first COVID-19 vaccines with the company Moderna. Her team was ready because they had already spent years studying how the immune system responds to coronaviruses. In 2021 she became a professor at Harvard University, and she encourages young people of color to become scientists.
