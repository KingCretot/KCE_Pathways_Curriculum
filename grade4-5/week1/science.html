hypothesis
A prediction you can test, often written “If… then… because…”. Example: If I add salt, then the ice will melt faster.
independent variable
The one thing you change on purpose in a test. Example: the height of a ramp.
dependent variable
What you measure to see what happened. Example: how far the car rolls.
controlled variable
Something you keep the same in every trial so the test is fair. Example: using the same car each time.
fair test
A test that changes only one variable at a time, so you know what caused the result.
data
Facts and measurements recorded during a test, like times, lengths or counts.
conclusion
A statement at the end of a test that tells what the data show and whether they support the hypothesis.

❓ Asking Testable Questions

Science starts with a question. A testable question is one you can answer by doing an investigation and collecting evidence, such as “Does a ball bounce higher on wood or on carpet?” Questions about opinions, like “Which ball is the coolest?”, cannot be tested. Next, a scientist writes a hypothesis: a prediction you can test, often in the form “If… then… because…”. A hypothesis does not have to be right, because the test will tell us.

👁 Watch Me Think

Here is how I check a question. Step 1: Can I measure something to answer it? “Which paper towel soaks up the most water?” Yes, I can measure the water in milliliters. Step 2: Would two people doing the same test get about the same answer? Yes. So it is testable. “Which paper towel is the prettiest?” fails Step 1, because “pretty” is an opinion, not a measurement.

Question: Does warm water dissolve sugar faster than cold water?
If I stir sugar into warm water, then it will dissolve faster than in cold water, because the particles in warm water move faster.
Notice that the hypothesis names what we will change (water temperature) and what we will measure (time to dissolve).

⚖️ Variables and Fair Tests

A variable is anything in a test that can change. The independent variable is the one thing you change on purpose. The dependent variable is what you measure to see what happened. Everything else must stay the same; these are the controlled variables. A fair test changes only one variable at a time, so you know what caused the result.

👁 Watch Me Think

Imagine testing whether ramp height changes how far a toy car rolls. I ask three questions. What am I changing on purpose? Ramp height, so that is the independent variable. What am I measuring? How far the car rolls, so that is the dependent variable. What must stay the same? The same car, the same floor, and letting go without a push. If I changed the car AND the height, I could not tell which one made the difference.

🤝 Let's Try Together: Spot the Unfair Test

Lena tests whether salt water or fresh water freezes faster. She puts the salt water in the freezer and the fresh water outside on a cold porch. Is it fair? No! She changed two things: the kind of water AND the place. To fix it, she should put both cups in the same freezer, with the same amount of water, in the same kind of cup.

📊 Data, Evidence and Conclusions

Data are the facts and measurements you record during a test. Scientists repeat each test several times, and each repeat is called a trial, because one result can be a fluke. They organize data in tables and graphs to look for patterns. A conclusion tells what the data show and whether they support the hypothesis. A good conclusion uses evidence, not wishes, even when the evidence surprises everyone.

👁 Watch Me Think

Say my plant data show: without fertilizer, 10 cm, 11 cm and 9 cm; with fertilizer, 15 cm, 14 cm and 16 cm. First I look at each trial: the fertilizer group is taller every time. Next I find a typical value: about 10 cm compared with about 15 cm. Then I write: “The data support my hypothesis. Plants with fertilizer grew about 5 cm taller in all three trials.” I name the evidence, with numbers.

🧩 Picture It: Chien-Shiung Wu’s Big Surprise

For many years, physicists assumed that nature treats left and right exactly the same. They treated this idea like a law. In 1956 and 1957, Chien-Shiung Wu led a careful experiment with radioactive cobalt atoms chilled to nearly absolute zero, the coldest temperature possible. The tiny particles the atoms gave off shot out more in one direction than the other. Her evidence showed the “law” was not true for these atoms.

🤝 Let's Try Together: Write a Conclusion

Question: Does a ball bounce higher on wood or on carpet?
Data (3 trials): wood 60 cm, 62 cm, 61 cm; carpet 20 cm, 22 cm, 21 cm.
Conclusion: “The ball bounced about 40 cm higher on wood than on carpet in all three trials, so the floor surface changes how high the ball bounces.”
Notice that we used numbers, and we did not claim more than the test showed.

🧑‍🔬 Scientists Who Followed the Evidence

These scientists trusted careful data over what other people assumed.

Chien-Shiung Wu

1912–1997

Born in China, Wu came to the United States in 1936 to study physics and became a professor at Columbia University. Other scientists called her experiments some of the most careful in the world. In 1956–1957 she tested a rule called conservation of parity, which said nature treats left and right the same. Her data proved it was not true for certain atoms. The two theorists who suggested the test, Tsung-Dao Lee and Chen-Ning Yang, won the 1957 Nobel Prize, but Wu was left out. She later won the first Wolf Prize in Physics (1978) and became the first woman president of the American Physical Society.

Percy Lavon Julian

1899–1975

Julian was a Black chemist who grew up in Montgomery, Alabama, where public school for Black students ended after eighth grade. In 1935 he and his partner Josef Pikl made physostigmine, a medicine for the eye disease glaucoma, in the lab. A famous English chemist, Robert Robinson, had published a different method, but Julian found a mistake in it, and careful tests showed that Julian’s work was right. He later found ways to make important medicines from soybeans.

Florence Nightingale

1820–1910

Nightingale was a British nurse and a skilled user of data. During the Crimean War in the 1850s, she collected numbers showing that far more soldiers died from diseases in dirty hospitals than from battle wounds. She drew diagrams so leaders could see the pattern quickly, and her evidence helped change how hospitals were kept clean.
