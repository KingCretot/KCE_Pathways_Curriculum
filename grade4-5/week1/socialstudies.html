Primary source
Evidence made by someone who was there at the time, like a letter, diary, photo, or tool. Example: a soldier's letter home.
Secondary source
Something made later by someone who studied the past, like a textbook or encyclopedia article.
Artifact
An object made or used by people, like a pot, tool, bead, or coin.
Archaeologist
A scientist who studies the past by carefully digging up and examining artifacts and places.
Oral history
History passed on by speaking and listening, such as stories, songs, and interviews with people who were there.
Corroborate
To check if other sources agree. If three sources tell the same fact, they corroborate it.
Perspective
A person's point of view, shaped by who they are and what they experienced.

📜 Primary and Secondary Sources

Historians cannot travel back in time, so they study sources, which are pieces of evidence. A primary source was made by someone who was there at the time, like a letter, a diary, a photograph, or a tool. A secondary source was made later by someone who studied the primary sources, like a textbook or a biography. Both are useful, but every source has a perspective, so historians always ask: Who made this, when, and why?

👁 Watch Me Think

Here is a letter written in 1863 by a soldier. Step 1: Was the writer there? Yes, he lived through it. Step 2: When was it made? At the time of the event. So it is a primary source. Now here is a book about that war written in 2010. The author was not there; she read many letters and records. That makes it a secondary source. Last step: I ask what each one might leave out. The soldier only saw his part of the battle.

🏺 Artifacts and Archaeology

Most people in history never wrote a book, but they all left things behind. Archaeologists study artifacts such as tools, pots, beads, bones from meals, and the remains of houses. They dig slowly in layers, because deeper layers are usually older than the layers on top. Artifacts help us learn about everyday people whose lives were never written down. In Florida, archaeologists dug at Fort Mose, near St. Augustine, the first legal free Black town in what is now the United States.

👁 Watch Me Think

Imagine a trash pile that people used for hundreds of years. Step 1: Each year, new trash lands on top. Step 2: So the oldest trash is at the bottom. Step 3: If I find a clay bowl deep down and a metal button near the top, I can say the bowl is probably older. Step 4: I write down exactly where each object was found, because once it is dug up, that clue is gone forever.

🎙️ Oral History and Who Got Left Out

Many peoples keep history by speaking, singing, and remembering. This is oral history. The Haudenosaunee use wampum belts, made of white and purple shell beads, as records of agreements; trained keepers remember and speak their meaning. For a long time, many American history books left out Black Americans, Indigenous Peoples, and women. In 1926, historian Carter G. Woodson started Negro History Week so students would learn Black history. It grew into Black History Month in 1976.

👁 Watch Me Think

The name “Negro History Week” may sound strange today. Step 1: I check the time. In the 1920s, “Negro” was a respectful word that many Black Americans used for themselves. Step 2: Words change over time. Today we say Black or African American. Step 3: So I keep the historical name when I quote it, but I use today's words when I speak. Historians always read words in their own time.

🧩 Picture It: A Wampum Belt as a Record

The Two Row Wampum is a long belt of white shell beads with two purple rows running side by side. Haudenosaunee keepers explain that it records an agreement with Dutch traders in the early 1600s: two boats, a canoe and a ship, travel down the same river side by side. Neither tries to steer the other.

🤝 Let's Try Together: Corroborate It

A history book says a town had a big flood in 1900. How can we check? We look for other sources: a 1900 newspaper story, a photo of flooded streets, and an elder's memory passed down in her family. If they agree, they corroborate the fact. If they disagree, we dig deeper.

🔍 Source Detective: How Old Was Frederick Douglass?

Frederick Douglass escaped slavery and became one of the most famous speakers and writers in American history. But there was one thing about his own life he never knew for sure. Read both sources carefully.

Primary Sources: Frederick Douglass and a Plantation Record

Source A — Frederick Douglass, writing in his book in 1845:

“I have no accurate knowledge of my age, never having seen any authentic record containing it… The white children could tell their ages. I could not tell why I ought to be deprived of the same privilege.”

Source B — A handwritten list kept by Aaron Anthony, the man who enslaved Frederick's family in Maryland. In the 1900s, a historian found it. Next to the name “Frederick Augustus,” son of Harriet, it records his birth as February 1818.

Also known: Douglass chose February 14 as the day to celebrate his birthday.

Source A: Narrative of the Life of Frederick Douglass, an American Slave (1845), Chapter 1. Source B: described by historian Dickson J. Preston in Young Frederick Douglass (1980).

👁 Think Like a Historian

A historian reads each source and asks: Who made this? When? Why? Then the historian compares them. Source A tells us how Douglass felt. Source B tells us what his enslaver wrote down. Neither source alone tells the whole story. Together, they show who controlled the written record.

🌟 Keepers of the Story

These people made sure that stories left out of the record were collected, saved, and taught.

Carter G. Woodson

1875–1950

Born in Virginia to parents who had been enslaved, he worked in coal mines and started high school at about 20. He earned a Ph.D. in history from Harvard in 1912 and started Negro History Week in 1926. He is called the Father of Black History Month.

Arturo Schomburg

1874–1938

Born in Puerto Rico, he spent his life collecting books, letters, and art made by people of African descent. His collection became part of the New York Public Library and is now the Schomburg Center for Research in Black Culture.

Zora Neale Hurston

1891–1960

She grew up in Eatonville, Florida, one of the first towns in the U.S. founded and governed by Black Americans. She studied anthropology and traveled the South collecting folktales and songs, which she published in Mules and Men (1935).

Haudenosaunee Wampum Keepers

Long ago–today

Keepers care for wampum belts and remember the agreements and history each belt records. At councils, they speak the meaning of the belts so it is passed to the next generation.
