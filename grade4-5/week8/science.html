🌍 Rotation: Why We Have Day and Night

Earth spins like a top around an imaginary line called its axis, which runs through the North and South Poles. One full spin is a rotation, and it takes about 24 hours. The half of Earth facing the Sun has daytime, and the half facing away has night. Because Earth spins from west to east, the Sun appears to rise in the east and set in the west, even though it is Earth that is moving.

👁 Watch Me Think

For thousands of years, many people believed the Sun traveled around Earth, because that is what it looks like. So how do we know Earth is the one turning? In 1851, a French scientist named Léon Foucault hung a heavy ball on a very long wire in Paris and set it swinging. Over the day, the direction of the swing slowly turned, even though nothing pushed it. The best explanation is that the floor, and all of Earth, was turning underneath it. I trust an idea more when it explains evidence like this, not just what my eyes first tell me.

🤝 Let's Try Together: Globe and Flashlight Model

In a dark room, shine a flashlight (the Sun) at a globe (Earth). Put a small sticker on Florida. Slowly turn the globe from west to east, the way Earth spins. Watch the sticker: it moves out of the dark and into the light. That is sunrise for Florida. Keep turning, and the sticker moves back into the dark. That is sunset. The flashlight never moved. Only the globe did, and that one motion made both day and night.

☀️ Revolution and Tilt: The Year and the Seasons

While it spins, Earth also travels around the Sun on a path called an orbit. One full trip is a revolution, and it takes about 365¼ days, so every four years we add a leap day to keep our calendar on track. Earth's axis is tilted about 23.5 degrees, and it always leans the same way in space. In June, the Northern Hemisphere tilts toward the Sun, so it gets more direct sunlight and longer days: it is summer there and winter in the Southern Hemisphere. In December it is the opposite, and on the March and September equinoxes, neither hemisphere tilts toward the Sun, so day and night are about equal.

👁 Watch Me Think

Many people think summer happens when Earth is closest to the Sun. Let me test that idea. If distance caused the seasons, the whole planet would have summer at the same time. But when Florida has summer in July, Australia has winter. And Earth is actually closest to the Sun in early January, during our winter! The tilt idea explains both facts: one hemisphere leans toward the Sun while the other leans away. A good explanation must fit all the evidence.

🤝 Let's Try Together: Direct and Slanted Light

Shine a flashlight straight down onto graph paper. You get a small, bright circle. Now tilt the flashlight. The same light spreads into a bigger, dimmer oval. Each square in the oval gets less light. Sunlight works the same way. When your hemisphere tilts toward the Sun, sunlight hits it more directly, like the bright circle, and heats the ground more. Longer days add even more heating time. That is why summer is warm.

🌙 Why the Moon Has Phases

The Moon makes no light of its own. We see it because sunlight reflects off its surface, and the Sun always lights up half of the Moon. As the Moon orbits Earth, we see different amounts of that sunlit half, and these changing shapes are called phases. At new moon, the Moon is between Earth and the Sun, so its lit side faces away from us; at full moon, Earth is between, so we see the whole lit side. When the lit part we see grows each night, the Moon is waxing; when it shrinks, it is waning. One full cycle of phases takes about 29½ days.

👁 Watch Me Think

A common idea is that Earth's shadow covers part of the Moon to make the phases. Let me check that with evidence. Earth's shadow always points away from the Sun. But a thin crescent Moon shows up in the sky close to the Sun, just after sunset. Earth's shadow cannot be over there! Also, the curved edge of a crescent is on the side facing the Sun. So the phases come from where the Moon is in its orbit, not from a shadow. When Earth's shadow really does fall on the Moon, that is a lunar eclipse, and it happens only at some full moons, not every month.

🔭 Sky Watchers Who Used Math

Long before spacecraft, people around the world predicted the movements of the Sun, Moon and planets using careful observation and mathematics.

Benjamin Banneker

1731–1806

Banneker was born free in Maryland to a free Black family and had only a few years of school. As a young man he studied a borrowed pocket watch and built a wooden clock that struck the hours and kept time for decades. Later, using astronomy books and a telescope lent by a neighbor, he taught himself astronomy and the math to predict eclipses. In 1791 he worked with surveyor Andrew Ellicott, using star observations to help mark the boundaries of the new federal district, the District of Columbia. His almanacs for 1792 through 1797 gave sunrise and sunset times, Moon phases, eclipses and tides.

Wang Zhenyi

1768–1797

Wang Zhenyi was a Chinese astronomer and mathematician during the Qing dynasty, a time when few women were allowed to study science. She read widely and taught herself. To show how a lunar eclipse works, she built a model: a round table stood for Earth, a hanging lamp for the Sun and a round mirror for the Moon. She also wrote books that explained math and astronomy in plain language so more people could learn them. A crater on Venus is named in her honor.

Maya Astronomers

for many centuries

Maya astronomers in what is now southern Mexico and Central America watched the sky for generations and tracked the Sun, Moon and Venus with great care. Spanish colonizers burned many Maya books in the 1500s, but a few survived. One of them, now called the Dresden Codex, holds tables that predict when eclipses could happen and follow the cycle of Venus. Maya communities used this knowledge to plan their calendars and ceremonies, and Maya people still live in the region today.
