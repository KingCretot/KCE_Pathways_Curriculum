A material that lets electricity or heat flow through it easily. Most metals, like copper, are good conductors.

A material that blocks or slows the flow of electricity or heat. Rubber, plastic, glass and wood are insulators.

⚡ Forms of Energy

Energy is the ability to cause change: to make something move, heat up, light up or make sound. Energy comes in many forms, including motion, heat, light, sound, electrical and chemical energy. Moving things have kinetic energy, while potential energy is stored, like the chemical energy in food and batteries or the energy of a stretched rubber band. Energy is never created or destroyed; it changes from one form to another, just as matter is conserved in chemical changes. Most of the energy we use started as sunlight: plants store the Sun's energy as chemical energy in food, and that energy flows to every animal that eats them.

👁 Watch Me Think

Let me follow the energy in a flashlight, one step at a time. The battery stores chemical energy. When I close the switch, the battery's chemical energy becomes electrical energy flowing through the wires. In the bulb, electrical energy becomes light, but the bulb also gets warm, so some becomes heat. The energy did not disappear; it changed form. If I count up all the light and heat, it equals the energy that left the battery.

🤝 Let's Try Together: From the Sun to a Soccer Goal

Let's trace the energy in a kicked soccer ball all the way back. Step 1: sunlight shines on a field of corn. Step 2: through photosynthesis, the corn stores light energy as chemical energy. Step 3: you eat a corn tortilla, and your body stores that chemical energy. Step 4: your leg muscles turn chemical energy into motion, which is kinetic energy. Step 5: the ball flies, and the thump makes sound energy, while your body warms up with heat energy. One chain of changes, and it started at the Sun.

🔋 Circuits: A Path for Electricity

Electricity is energy carried by tiny charged particles called electrons as they flow through a material. That flow is called an electric current, and it needs a complete loop to follow, called a circuit. A simple circuit has an energy source such as a battery, wires, and a device such as a bulb, all connected in a closed loop. If there is any gap, the circuit is open and the current stops. A switch opens and closes that gap on purpose. In a series circuit there is only one path, so if one bulb breaks, everything stops; in a parallel circuit there are several paths, so the other bulbs stay lit.

👁 Watch Me Think

A string of old holiday lights goes dark when just one bulb burns out. What does that tell me? If the other bulbs went out too, the current must have had only one path, and the broken bulb made a gap in it. That is a series circuit. Now I think about my house: when I turn off the kitchen light, the TV stays on. So my house must be wired in parallel, with a separate path for each device. The way things fail is evidence about how they are built.

🤝 Let's Try Together: Light the Bulb with One Wire

We have one battery, one wire and one small flashlight bulb. A bulb has two contact points: the metal tip on the bottom and the metal side of its base. Let's build a loop. Step 1: touch the bulb's bottom tip to the battery's + end. Step 2: tape one end of the wire to the battery's − end. Step 3: touch the other end of the wire to the metal side of the bulb's base. The bulb lights! The current flows out of the battery, through the bulb and back. If we touch the wire to the bulb's tip instead of its side, there is no loop through the bulb, and it stays dark.

🧤 Conductors and Insulators

Some materials let electricity flow through them easily; they are called conductors. Most metals, like copper, aluminum, iron and gold, are good conductors, and so is graphite, the gray material in a pencil. Water with dissolved salts and minerals, like tap water and the water in our bodies, also conducts electricity, which is why we keep electrical devices away from sinks and bathtubs. Materials that block the flow are insulators: rubber, plastic, glass, wood and dry air. Engineers use both: a power cord has copper wires inside to carry the current and a plastic coating outside to keep it from reaching your hands.

👁 Watch Me Think

Why are pot handles and wire coatings made of plastic? Let me think about what each part needs to do. The copper inside a cord must carry current, so it must be a conductor. But the outside must keep current away from people, so it must be an insulator. The same thinking works for heat: a metal pot conducts heat to the food, and a plastic or wooden handle insulates my hand. When I see two different materials joined together, I ask what job each one is doing.

🧩 Picture It: Inside a Power Cord

  • Center: thin strands of copper metal. Copper is an excellent conductor, so current flows easily.
  • Coating: plastic or rubber wrapped around each wire. It is an insulator, so the current stays inside.
  • Plug prongs: metal, so they can connect to the outlet's metal parts.
  • Plug body: hard plastic, so your fingers touch only an insulator.

A cord with torn coating and bare copper showing is dangerous. Tell an adult and never touch it.

🤝 Let's Try Together: Does a Pencil Conduct?

Let's predict and test. A pencil has a wood outside and a gray core called graphite (it is not really lead). Prediction: wood is an insulator, and graphite is not a metal, so maybe the pencil won't conduct. Test: we sharpen both ends and touch our battery-and-LED tester's two wires to the gray graphite tips. The LED glows! Evidence beats our prediction: graphite is a conductor even though it is not a metal. Then we touch the wires to the wood only. The LED stays dark. Wood is an insulator. One pencil, two materials, two results.

💡 Inventors Who Powered the World

Electric lights, safer trains and the power lines that reach our homes all depend on scientists, inventors and engineers like these.

Lewis Latimer

1848–1928

Lewis Latimer was born in Massachusetts to parents who had escaped slavery in Virginia. As a teenager he served in the U.S. Navy during the Civil War. Working as an office boy at a patent law firm, he taught himself technical drawing and became its head draftsman; in 1876 he drew the diagrams for Alexander Graham Bell's telephone patent. In 1882 he patented a better way to make the carbon filaments that glow inside light bulbs, so fewer filaments broke and bulbs became cheaper and more reliable. He helped set up electric lighting in cities including New York, Philadelphia, Montreal and London, joined Thomas Edison's company in 1884, and wrote an 1890 book on electric lighting.

Granville T. Woods

1856–1910

Granville T. Woods was born in Columbus, Ohio, and left school as a boy to work in a machine shop. He learned engineering by working on railroads and studying on his own. He earned more than 50 patents, many for electric railways and communication. His best-known invention, patented in 1887, let moving trains send telegraph messages to stations and to each other, helping prevent crashes. When Thomas Edison claimed the idea was really his, Woods successfully defended his patent. He also improved systems that powered electric streetcars. Newspapers called him the 'Black Edison.'

Edith Clarke

1883–1959

Edith Clarke was born in Howard County, Maryland. In 1919 she became the first woman to earn a master's degree in electrical engineering from MIT. At General Electric she is recognized as the first woman in the United States to work as a professional electrical engineer. She invented the Clarke calculator, a tool that helped engineers solve hard math problems about long power lines much faster. In 1947 she became the first woman to teach electrical engineering as a professor in the United States, at the University of Texas.

Michael Faraday

1791–1867

Michael Faraday grew up poor in London and had little schooling. As a teenager working for a bookbinder, he read the science books he bound, and later became an assistant to the chemist Humphry Davy. In 1831 he discovered that moving a magnet in and out of a coil of wire makes an electric current. Power plants still use this idea in their generators today. He also built an early electric motor, and he started science lectures for young people at Christmas that are still held in London.
