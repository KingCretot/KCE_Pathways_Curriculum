🇲🇽 Mexico's Independence and Texas

On September 16, 1810, a priest named Miguel Hidalgo called on the people of Mexico to rise up against Spain, and after more than ten years of war, Mexico won its independence in 1821. Mexico's northern lands stretched from Texas to California and were home to many Indigenous nations, such as the Comanche, Apache, Diné (Navajo), Pueblo peoples, and Tohono O'odham, as well as Mexican families like the Tejanos of Texas. In the 1820s, Mexico invited U.S. settlers into Texas if they became Mexican citizens, and soon they outnumbered Tejanos. Mexico ended slavery in 1829, but Texas settlers won an exception and found ways around Mexico's laws to keep enslaving people, and in 1835 and 1836 settlers and some Tejanos fought for Texas independence; the new Republic of Texas made slavery legal, and the United States annexed Texas in 1845.

👁 Watch Me Think

The Texas story is often told as brave settlers fighting a cruel government. I want to see it from more sides. Mexico saw newcomers who had promised to follow its laws and then kept finding ways around its laws against slavery. Tejanos like Juan Seguín fought for Texas because they wanted more local self-rule, not slavery. Enslaved people in Texas lost the most: under Mexico, slavery was illegal, but the new Republic of Texas protected it. So I ask: freedom for whom?

🤝 Let's Try Together: Remember the Alamo, From More Than One Side

In March 1836, Mexican soldiers led by General Antonio López de Santa Anna captured the Alamo, a mission building in San Antonio. Texans later shouted "Remember the Alamo!" and won the Battle of San Jacinto in April 1836. Let's ask a historian's questions. Who fought inside the Alamo? U.S. settlers, and also Tejanos. Juan Seguín had been sent out with a message before it fell. Who fought outside? Mexican soldiers defending their country's laws. Who is missing from most tellings? Enslaved people, whose freedom depended on who won. One event, many points of view.

🧭 The U.S.–Mexican War and the Border That Moved

Many Americans believed in Manifest Destiny, the idea that the United States had a right to spread across the continent. President James K. Polk wanted California, and when Mexico refused to sell it, he sent soldiers into land both countries claimed, between the Nueces River and the Rio Grande. After a clash there in 1846, Congress declared war; some Americans, like Congressman Abraham Lincoln and Frederick Douglass, said the war was wrong. In February 1848, the Treaty of Guadalupe Hidalgo ended the war: Mexico lost about half of its land, counting Texas, and the United States paid $15 million. Roughly 80,000 to 100,000 Mexican citizens suddenly lived in the United States, which is why some of their descendants say, "We didn't cross the border; the border crossed us."

👁 Watch Me Think

I compare the reasons people gave. Polk said Mexico had shed "American blood upon the American soil," but the soil was land Mexico also claimed. Critics like Lincoln asked Polk to name the exact spot. Douglass saw a war to spread slavery. When leaders disagree this much, I look for who gained: the United States gained huge lands, and enslavers hoped to spread slavery there. Then I ask who paid: Mexico, and the families and nations living on that land.

🤝 Let's Try Together: The Border Crossed Us

Imagine a Californio family living on a rancho near Monterey in 1847. They are Mexican citizens. In 1848, a treaty is signed far away, and now their home is in the United States. They did not move at all. The treaty said they could choose to become U.S. citizens and promised to protect their property. The Tohono O'odham, whose lands were later cut by the new border, faced the same thing: the line was drawn through their homeland without them. That is what the saying means: the border moved, and the people stayed.

⛏️ The Gold Rush: Who Got Rich, Who Paid?

On January 24, 1848, just nine days before the treaty was signed, James Marshall found gold at Coloma, California, on the homeland of the Nisenan people. About 300,000 prospectors poured in from 1848 to 1855, from the eastern states, Mexico, Chile, Europe and China, where people called California "Gold Mountain." California became a free state in 1850, but the new state passed a Foreign Miners' Tax: $20 a month in 1850, aimed at Mexican and South American miners, and $3 a month from 1852, collected mostly from Chinese miners. For California's Indigenous nations, such as the Nisenan, Miwok, Maidu and Yokuts, the Gold Rush was a disaster: disease, hunger and attacks by miners and state-paid militias cut their population from about 150,000 to about 30,000 by 1870, and in 2019 California's governor formally apologized and called it a genocide.

👁 Watch Me Think

The usual Gold Rush story is about lucky miners striking it rich. But most miners never got rich. The people who did best often sold supplies, like shovels, food and jeans. I also look at the laws. A tax only foreign miners must pay is discrimination, because it treats people unfairly based on where they come from. And the hardest truth is about the Indigenous nations: the gold was found on their land, and they paid the highest cost of all.

🤝 Let's Try Together: Doing the Tax Math

Let's see what the Foreign Miners' Tax cost. In 1852, a Chinese miner had to pay $3 every month. For a whole year, that is 3 × 12 = $36. In 1850, the tax on foreign miners was $20 a month, so three months cost 20 × 3 = $60. A U.S.-born miner working the same stream paid $0. Now the historian's question: who benefited? The state and its counties collected large sums from this tax over the years, mostly from Chinese miners. Who paid the cost? Miners who were treated as outsiders, even when they had come legally to work.

🔍 Source Detective: The Treaty's Promises

The Treaty of Guadalupe Hidalgo made promises to the Mexican citizens who now lived in the United States. Read a short part of the treaty, then read what happened next. A historian's job is to compare promises with results.

Primary Source: Treaty of Guadalupe Hidalgo, 1848, Articles VIII and IX, with a What Happened Next chart

Article VIII: "Mexicans now established in territories previously belonging to Mexico... shall be free to continue where they now reside, or to remove at any time to the Mexican Republic, retaining the property which they possess... Those who shall prefer to remain in the said territories may either retain the title and rights of Mexican citizens, or acquire those of citizens of the United States."

Article IX (about Mexicans who become U.S. citizens): they "shall be maintained and protected in the free enjoyment of their liberty and property, and secured in the free exercise of their religion without restriction."

Word help: established = living, settled; reside = live; remove = move away; retaining = keeping; acquire = get; free exercise of their religion = practicing their faith freely.

👁 Think Like a Historian

A treaty is a promise written down. To judge a promise, I check two things: exactly what it says, and what really happened afterward. I also notice who the promise names. This treaty speaks about "Mexicans" and their property and religion. I ask: does it say anything about the Indigenous nations living in the same lands? If a group is not named, its rights may not be protected.

🧑‍🤝‍🧑 People of the Borderlands and the Gold Rush

These people lived through the years when the border moved and gold changed California. Read each card and ask: what did this person fight for, and what did the new border cost them?

Juan Seguín

1806 – 1890

A Tejano from San Antonio who led Tejano soldiers for Texas independence and fought at San Jacinto in 1836. He later served as a Texas senator and mayor of San Antonio. But newcomers grew hostile to Tejanos, and in 1842 he was forced to flee to Mexico. He later wrote that he had become "a foreigner in my native land."

José Antonio Navarro

1795 – 1871

A Tejano leader born in San Antonio and one of two Texas-born men who signed the Texas Declaration of Independence in 1836. In 1845, he was the only Tejano delegate at the convention that wrote Texas's first state constitution, where he helped defeat a proposal that would have taken the vote away from many Tejanos.

Mariano Guadalupe Vallejo

1807 – 1890

A Californio general who commanded Mexico's northern California frontier from Sonoma. Like other rancho owners, he relied on the work of Indigenous people, often under unfair, forced conditions. In 1846, U.S. rebels jailed him for about seven weeks. He still helped write California's first constitution in 1849, but he later lost most of his land in court fights and to squatters.

Biddy Mason

1818 – 1891

Born enslaved, she was forced to walk behind her enslaver's wagons all the way to California. California was a free state, and in 1856 she went to court in Los Angeles and won freedom for herself and her family. She worked as a nurse and midwife, saved to buy land, became one of the wealthiest Black women in Los Angeles, and helped found its First AME Church.

Norman Asing

active 1850s

A Chinese American restaurant owner and community leader in San Francisco. In 1852, when Governor John Bigler called for limits on Chinese immigrants, Asing answered him in a public letter printed in the Daily Alta California newspaper. He argued that Chinese people were hardworking and law-abiding and that America itself was built by immigrants, so they deserved fair treatment.

California's Indigenous Nations

here for thousands of years

Nations such as the Nisenan, Miwok, Maidu, Yokuts, Pomo, Ohlone and Chumash lived in California long before Spain, Mexico or the United States. Many had already suffered in the Spanish missions. In the Gold Rush they lost land and lives, but they survived. Today more than 100 California tribes keep their languages, ceremonies and governments alive.
