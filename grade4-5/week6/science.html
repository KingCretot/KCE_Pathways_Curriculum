🌟 Word Wrangler

Tap each card to flip it. Tap the 🔊 to hear the word and what it means.

weathering
The slow breaking down of rock into smaller pieces, right where it is. Example: ice cracking a boulder.
physical weathering
Breaking rock into smaller pieces without changing what it is made of. Example: roots splitting a rock.
chemical weathering
Breaking down rock by changing what it is made of. Example: rainwater dissolving limestone, or iron in rock rusting.
erosion
The moving of rock pieces and soil from one place to another by water, wind, ice or gravity.
deposition
The dropping of sediment in a new place when water, wind or ice slows down. Example: sand piling up at a river’s mouth.
sediment
Loose bits of rock, sand, mud and shells that can be carried and dropped.
landform
A natural shape on Earth’s surface, such as a canyon, delta, sand dune, valley or sinkhole.

🪨 Weathering: Breaking Rock Down

Weathering is the slow breaking down of rock right where it sits. In physical weathering, rock breaks into smaller pieces but stays the same material, like when ice or plant roots crack it. In chemical weathering, the rock’s material changes. Rainwater is slightly acidic, so it can dissolve rocks like limestone, and iron in some rocks reacts with oxygen and rusts. Weathering is slow, often taking hundreds or thousands of years.

👁 Watch Me Think

Here is how I sort an example. I ask: is the rock still the same stuff, just in smaller pieces? A boulder split by ice is still granite, so that is physical weathering. Now I look at a rock with orange-red stains. The iron in it has turned into rust, a new material. The stuff changed, so that is chemical weathering.

🧩 Picture It: Two Kinds of Weathering

Physical (same material, smaller pieces)Chemical (material changes)

🤝 Let's Try Together: Ice Wedging, Step by Step

1. Rainwater seeps into a thin crack in a rock.
2. At night it gets below freezing, and the water turns to ice.
3. Water expands (takes up about one-tenth more space) when it freezes, so the ice pushes the crack wider.
4. In the day the ice melts, and more water seeps deeper into the bigger crack.
5. After many freeze-thaw cycles, the rock splits apart.
The rock is still the same kind of rock, so this is physical weathering.

🌊 Erosion and Deposition: Moving and Dropping

Once rock is broken into sediment, it can be moved. Erosion is the moving of sediment by water, wind, ice or gravity. Fast water and strong wind can carry more and bigger pieces. When the water or wind slows down, it drops what it carries, and that is deposition. Heavy pieces like gravel drop first, while tiny bits of mud travel farthest.

👁 Watch Me Think

I ask three questions in order. 1) Was rock broken down? That is weathering. 2) Was it carried somewhere? That is erosion. 3) Was it dropped in a new place? That is deposition. A river’s fast water picks up sand in the mountains (erosion). When the river reaches the calm ocean, it slows down and drops the sand (deposition).

AgentHow it erodesWhat it can build

🤝 Let's Try Together: Follow One Grain of Sand

1. Ice cracks a piece off a mountain rock. Weathering.
2. A fast mountain stream tumbles the piece downhill, knocking off corners until it is a sand grain. Erosion (and more weathering).
3. The river widens and slows near the coast.
4. At the river’s mouth, the water slows almost to a stop, and the grain sinks with millions of others. Deposition.
5. Layer after layer builds a fan of new land called a delta.

🏜️ Landforms: Reading the Land

Weathering, erosion and deposition build the landforms around us. Over millions of years, the Colorado River cut the Grand Canyon, which is about a mile deep in places. Rivers drop sediment where they meet the ocean, building deltas, like the Mississippi River Delta. Wind piles sand into dunes, and glaciers scrape out wide U-shaped valleys. Florida sits on thick limestone, so slightly acidic water dissolves caves underground, and when a cave roof collapses, a sinkhole opens.

👁 Watch Me Think

To figure out what made a landform, I look for clues. A narrow V-shaped valley with a river at the bottom points to water cutting down. A wide U-shaped valley in cold mountains points to a glacier. A fan of mud at a river’s mouth points to deposition. A round hole in Florida limestone country points to rock dissolving underground.

🧩 Picture It: How a Florida Sinkhole Forms

  • Step 1: Rain soaks through sand and soil. It picks up carbon dioxide and becomes slightly acidic.
  • Step 2: The water slowly dissolves the limestone underneath (chemical weathering), making cracks and caves.
  • Step 3: The cave grows. The ground above is held up by a thinner and thinner roof.
  • Step 4: The roof gives way, and the ground sinks into the hole (gravity).

Florida has thousands of sinkholes. Many of its lakes and springs began this way. In 1981 a sinkhole in Winter Park grew to about 300 feet across.

🤝 Let's Try Together: One River, Two Landforms

The Colorado River flows fast and steep through northern Arizona. Its water, loaded with sand and gravel, scrapes the riverbed and slowly cuts downward: erosion carves the Grand Canyon.
The Mississippi River flows slowly across flat land. Where it meets the ocean, it slows and drops its mud: deposition builds the Mississippi River Delta.
Same agent (running water), opposite results. Fast water cuts; slow water builds.
The Grand Canyon is also a homeland. The Havasupai people still live in Havasu Canyon, a side canyon of the Grand Canyon.

🔬 Stream Table Lab: What Speeds Up Erosion?

A class built a model river in a long plastic tray filled with 2 kilograms of damp sand. They propped up one end with books to make a slope, then poured 2 liters of water down the tray in 30 seconds. They weighed the sand that washed into a cup at the bottom and measured the fan of sand that built up there. They ran each test twice. In the last test, they planted grass clumps in the sand.

🧭 Geoscientists Who Read the Rocks

Geoscientists study how Earth is built and how it changes. These three scientists broke barriers to read the story written in rocks, rivers and the ocean floor.

Florence Bascom

1862–1945

In 1893 Bascom became the first woman to earn a PhD from Johns Hopkins University, and in 1896 she became the first woman hired by the U.S. Geological Survey. She hiked and mapped the rocks of the Piedmont region in Pennsylvania, Maryland and New Jersey. She started the geology department at Bryn Mawr College and trained many of the country’s first women geologists.

Marguerite Thomas Williams

1895–1991

In 1942 Williams became the first Black American to earn a PhD in geology, at Catholic University in Washington, D.C. Her research was about erosion in the Anacostia River area. She studied how people cutting forests, farming and building cities made erosion happen faster. She taught for many years in Washington, D.C.

Marie Tharp

1920–2006

Tharp turned thousands of ocean depth measurements into the first detailed maps of the ocean floor, working with Bruce Heezen. In the early 1950s she found a deep valley running down the middle of the Mid-Atlantic Ridge, an underwater mountain chain. Some scientists doubted her at first, but her evidence helped prove that Earth’s plates move.
