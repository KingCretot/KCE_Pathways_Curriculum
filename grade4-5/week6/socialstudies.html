🌟 Word Wrangler

Tap each card to flip it. Tap the 🔊 to hear the word and what it means.

colony
A settlement or territory ruled by a faraway country. Example: Virginia was an English colony.
cash crop
A crop grown to sell for money, not mainly to eat. Tobacco was Virginia's cash crop.
indentured servant
A person who agreed to work for several years, often four to seven, in return for the trip to a colony. When the time ended, the person was free.
paramount chief
A leader over many nations or towns. Wahunsenacah was paramount chief of the Powhatan.
privateer
A privately owned armed ship, or its captain, allowed by a government to attack and capture enemy ships.
alliance
An agreement between groups to help and protect each other.
treaty
A formal agreement between nations, such as an agreement about peace or land.

🗺️ Four Empires, Many Nations

In the 1600s, four European countries built colonies in North America, always on land where Indigenous nations already lived. Spain held Florida and, from about 1610, Santa Fe in New Mexico, where Pueblo people were forced to work and pushed to give up their religion. France founded Quebec in 1608 and traded for beaver furs with nations such as the Wendat (Huron). The Dutch built New Amsterdam on Manhattan Island, in the Lenape homeland, and England built Jamestown in 1607, Plymouth in 1620, and more, until there were 13 English colonies by 1732. Indigenous nations were neighbors, trading partners, allies and rivals, and each nation made its own choices about the newcomers.

👁 Watch Me Think

Maps from this time often show big colored areas labeled New Spain, New France and New England. I ask: what is missing from that map? The Indigenous nations who still controlled most of the land. In 1650, most of North America was Indigenous land. So I read a colonial map as one point of view: the view of the empire that drew it.

🤝 Let's Try Together: The Pueblo Revolt

Let's reason about resistance. In New Mexico, Spanish officials forced Pueblo people to work and punished them for practicing their own religion. In 1680, a Tewa leader named Po'pay helped many Pueblo towns rise up together, and they drove the Spanish out of New Mexico for about 12 years. What does this show? Indigenous nations were not just the background of colonial stories. They planned, united and acted.

⚓ Jamestown, the Powhatan and 1619

In May 1607, about 100 English men and boys sent by the Virginia Company built Jamestown in Tsenacomoco (sen-ah-COM-ah-co), the land of the Powhatan people, where about 30 nations followed one paramount chief, Wahunsenacah. The colonists were not ready to farm and depended on Powhatan corn; in the "Starving Time" winter of 1609 to 1610, most of them died. After John Rolfe began growing tobacco as a cash crop around 1612, the colony wanted more land and more workers, and it kept taking Powhatan land. In late August 1619, an English privateer ship, the White Lion, brought about 20 Africans to Virginia. Its crew had captured them from a Portuguese slave ship, and they traded them to Virginia's leaders for food.

👁 Watch Me Think

The 1619 Africans had been captured in Angola, in the Kingdom of Ndongo, and carried across the ocean against their will. Were they enslaved? They were taken by force and sold, so they were not free. But Virginia had no slavery law yet, and the records are thin, so historians debate how each person was treated in the first years. Some Africans in early Virginia, like Anthony Johnson, later gained their freedom. I say what the evidence shows, and I say clearly where it is uncertain.

🤝 Let's Try Together: Two Events in 1619

In July 1619, Virginia's first elected assembly, the House of Burgesses, met at Jamestown. Only white men could serve in it. In August 1619, about 20 captive Africans were traded for food. Let's reason: the same year brought the start of self-government for some colonists and forced labor for others. When a textbook celebrates only the first event, whose story is missing?

🤝 Plymouth, the Wampanoag and Pennsylvania

In 1620, English Separatists we now call the Pilgrims sailed on the Mayflower and built Plymouth on the site of Patuxet, a Wampanoag village emptied by an epidemic a few years before. In 1621, the Wampanoag leader Massasoit (Ousamequin) made an alliance with them, partly to gain help against the rival Narragansett nation, and that fall about 90 Wampanoag men joined the colonists' harvest feast for three days. In 1681, King Charles II gave William Penn, a Quaker, a huge land grant, and Penn made treaties with the Lenape and bought land from them instead of simply seizing it. But in the 1737 Walking Purchase, Penn's sons used a doubtful old deed and hired fast runners to claim a huge area of Lenape land.

👁 Watch Me Think

The Thanksgiving story many people learn shows friendly neighbors sharing a meal. The evidence shows something more complicated. The Wampanoag had just lost many people to disease, so Massasoit had good reasons to want allies. The peace lasted more than 50 years. Then, in 1675, as colonists kept taking Wampanoag land, his son Metacom led a war against the English colonies. One feast is a true moment, but it is not the whole story.

🤝 Let's Try Together: Penn, Told Honestly

William Penn believed in religious freedom and made agreements with Lenape leaders such as Tamanend, and the Lenape remembered him as fairer than most colonists. Penn also held enslaved Africans on his estate. About 55 years after his first treaties, his sons cheated the Lenape in the Walking Purchase. Let's reason: one person, or one family, can do both fair and unfair things, so historians judge each action by its evidence.

🔍 Source Detective: John Rolfe's Letter

In January 1620, John Rolfe wrote a report to a leader of the Virginia Company in London. In it, he described the arrival of the first Africans in English Virginia. Read his words carefully, then decide what they show and what they leave out.

Primary Source: John Rolfe's letter, January 1620

"About the latter end of August, a Dutch man of war of the burden of 160 tons arrived at Point Comfort… He brought not anything but 20 and odd [Africans], which the Governor and Cape Merchant bought for victuals…"

Word help: man of war = an armed ship; 20 and odd = about 20, or a few more; Cape Merchant = the colony official in charge of trade and supplies; victuals (VIT-ulz) = food. Rolfe used a 1600s word for Black people. We put [Africans] in brackets to show we changed that one word.

What other records show: The ship was the White Lion, an English privateer sailing with Dutch permission. Its crew had taken the Africans from a Portuguese slave ship carrying captives from Angola.

John Rolfe, letter to Sir Edwin Sandys, January 1620, printed in The Records of the Virginia Company of London, Volume 3 (Library of Congress). Spelling modernized and shortened; one word replaced in brackets.

👁 Think Like a Historian

First I ask who wrote this and why. John Rolfe was a tobacco planter and colony official writing a business report to Sir Edwin Sandys, a leader of the Virginia Company. He describes the Africans in a few words, the same way he lists a ship's size. That tells me his point of view: he saw the captives as goods bought for the colony, not as people with names and homes. I also corroborate. Other records show the ship was English, not Dutch, so even a primary source written at the time can get a detail wrong.

👥 People of the Early Colonies

These people shaped the early colonies. Look at each one honestly: what did they do, what was done to them, and how do we know?

Matoaka (Pocahontas)

About 1596–1617

Daughter of Wahunsenacah. Pocahontas was a nickname; her people called her Matoaka (also Amonute). She was about 11 when the English came and sometimes brought food to Jamestown. In 1613 the English kidnapped her and held her for ransom. While a captive she became a Christian, took the name Rebecca, and married John Rolfe in 1614. She died in England in 1617, at about 21. John Smith's story that she saved his life was written years later, and many historians doubt it.

Wahunsenacah (Powhatan)

About 1547–1618

Paramount chief of about 30 nations in Tsenacomoco. He traded corn with Jamestown, tested the colonists' strength, and kept his people powerful. After his daughter married John Rolfe, he agreed to a peace that lasted until 1622.

Anthony Johnson

Arrived 1621; died 1670

Brought to Virginia as a captive in 1621 and listed in records as "Antonio." He survived the 1622 war and later gained his freedom. By 1651, he and his wife Mary owned 250 acres on Virginia's Eastern Shore. After he died, a Virginia jury ruled in 1670 that because he was Black he counted as "an alien," and his family's last Virginia land went to a white neighbor.

Massasoit (Ousamequin)

About 1581–1661

Massasoit is a title meaning great leader; his name was Ousamequin. He led the Wampanoag when the Pilgrims arrived and made a 1621 alliance with Plymouth that lasted the rest of his life.

Tisquantum (Squanto)

Died 1622

A Patuxet man kidnapped by an English captain in 1614 and taken to Spain to be sold. He made his way to England and back home by 1619, only to find his village emptied by disease. He became an interpreter between the Wampanoag and Plymouth.

William Penn

1644–1718

An English Quaker who founded Pennsylvania in 1681 as a place of religious freedom. He made treaties with the Lenape and was remembered as fairer than most colonists. He also enslaved Africans on his estate, and his sons later cheated the Lenape in the 1737 Walking Purchase.
