🧠 From Senses to Signals: The Message Pathway

Animals survive by noticing what is happening around them and responding fast. Anything an animal can sense, such as light, sound, a smell, a touch or heat, is a stimulus. Special cells called receptors detect stimuli. Your eyes have receptors for light, your ears for sound, your nose and tongue for chemicals, and your skin for touch, pressure, heat and cold. A receptor turns the stimulus into a signal. The signal travels along neurons, the nerve cells that carry messages. Bundles of neurons form nerves, which connect every part of the body to the spinal cord and the brain. The brain processes the information, compares it with what it remembers and decides what to do. Then it sends signals along other nerves to muscles, which carry out the response. Some responses are so urgent that the body takes a shortcut. If you touch a hot pan, a reflex pulls your hand away before you even feel the pain, because the reflex does not wait for the brain.

👁 Watch Me Think

Bowen is helping Mr. Bertie in the nature center's yard when a ball comes flying toward him. Let me trace what happens inside him. The stimulus is light bouncing off the ball into his eyes. Receptors at the back of his eyes turn that light into signals. Nerves carry the signals to his brain. His brain processes them: it is a ball, it is coming fast, and it is coming toward me, so catch it! His brain sends signals down his spinal cord and along nerves to the muscles in his arm and hand, and he catches the ball. All of that happens in a fraction of a second. Now compare a hot pan. A reflex pulls the hand away first, and the feeling of pain comes after.

🤝 Let's Try Together: Trace a Dog Hearing Its Name

Let's trace a signal together. At the nature center, Bijou calls the name of Mr. Bertie's dog from across the yard, and the dog runs to her. Stimulus: the sound of its name. Receptors: special cells inside the dog's ears detect the sound waves and start signals. Nerves: neurons carry the signals to the dog's brain. Brain: it recognizes the sound from memory. A dog that has learned its name responds differently from a dog that never heard it before. The brain uses past experience to decide how to respond.

🦇 Super Senses: How Animals Survive

Many animals sense things that people cannot. Bats send out high-pitched calls and listen for the echoes that bounce back from insects and trees. Their brains use the echoes to build a picture of the world in the dark. This is called echolocation, a word created by scientist Donald Griffin, who showed how bats do it in 1938 with Robert Galambos, when both were college students. A barn owl's ears are lopsided: one ear opening sits higher than the other, and one sits farther forward. A sound reaches its two ears at slightly different times, so the owl's brain can pinpoint a mouse in total darkness. Pit vipers, such as rattlesnakes, have pits between their eyes and nostrils that sense the heat, or infrared light, given off by warm-blooded animals. Sharks and rays have tiny jelly-filled pores around their mouths that sense the weak electric signals made by other animals. The star-nosed mole has 22 fleshy fingers on its nose with about 25,000 tiny touch domes, and it can decide whether something is food in about a fifth of a second. Every super sense follows the same pathway: receptors, nerves, brain and response.

👁 Watch Me Think

Bijou asks Mr. Bertie how a barn owl can hunt on a moonless night. Let me think it through. A mouse rustles in the grass to the owl's left. The sound reaches the owl's left ear a tiny fraction of a second before its right ear. Its brain compares the two signals, the way you turn your head to find where a sound is coming from. Because one ear is higher than the other, the owl can also tell whether the sound is up or down. Scientist Mark Konishi showed that an owl's brain builds a map of the space around it from sounds alone. The owl does not need light at all. Its ears and brain work together to find the mouse.

🐢 Inside and Outside: Structures for Survival

Living things have structures that work together like the parts of a machine. External structures are on the outside: a hawk's sharp talons and hooked beak catch and tear food, a porcupine's quills protect it, and a turtle's shell guards its soft body. Internal structures are on the inside: the heart pumps blood, lungs or gills take in oxygen, the stomach digests food, and the brain and nerves control everything. Some structures surprise us. A turtle's shell is part of its skeleton. It is made of flattened ribs fused into bony plates, with the backbone joined to the underside of the shell, so a turtle can never crawl out of it. Porcupine quills are special hairs made of keratin, the same material as your fingernails, with barbs at the tips. A porcupine cannot shoot its quills, but they come out easily when a predator touches them. Plants have structures that respond too. The leaves of a Venus flytrap, which grows wild only in a small area of southeastern North Carolina and nearby South Carolina, snap shut when trigger hairs inside are touched twice within about 20 seconds.

👁 Watch Me Think

Mr. Bertie shows Bijou a box turtle in the nature center. When his dog sniffs it, the turtle pulls its head inside its shell. Let me trace it. The stimulus is the dog's movement and smell. Receptors in the turtle's eyes and nose detect it. Nerves carry signals to its brain, and the brain sends signals to muscles that pull its head back. Then the shell, an external structure that is also part of its skeleton, protects it. Senses, nerves, brain, muscles and structures all worked together in one quick response.

🤝 Let's Try Together: Trace a Porcupine's Defense

Let's trace a porcupine's defense together. A fox creeps close. Stimulus: the fox's smell and the sound of its steps. Receptors in the porcupine's nose and ears detect them, and nerves carry the signals to its brain. Its brain decides: danger! Signals travel to muscles in its skin that raise its quills, and to its legs so it turns its back toward the fox. If the fox bites anyway, the barbed quills come out easily and stick in the fox's mouth. Internal structures, the nerves, brain and muscles, controlled the response. External structures, the quills, did the defending. Survival takes both.

⏱️ Data Lab: How Fast Can You React?

Mr. Bertie challenged Bowen and Bijou to a ruler-drop test. One person holds a 30-centimeter ruler so the zero end hangs just above the catcher's open thumb and finger. The holder lets go at a random moment, and the catcher pinches it as fast as possible. A shorter catch distance means a faster reaction. Each cousin tested both hands, three times each. Try It at Home with a grown-up or an older partner: it is safe and needs only a ruler, but drop it from just above the catcher's fingers and never toss it.

👁 Think Like a Neuroscientist

A neuroscientist studies the nervous system. She knows a reaction time measures the whole pathway: the eyes see the ruler fall, nerves carry the signal to the brain, the brain decides, and signals travel back to the finger muscles. To make the test fair, she drops the ruler at a random moment so the catcher cannot guess, uses the same ruler and starting spot each time, and repeats the trials. Then she looks for patterns instead of trusting just one number.

🔬 Scientists Who Studied Senses and Signals

From Spain, Italy, Japan and New York City, these scientists discovered how nerve cells work and how animals sense the world.

Santiago Ramón y Cajal

1852–1934

Santiago Ramón y Cajal was born in a small town in northeastern Spain. As a boy he loved to draw and wanted to be an artist, but his father, a doctor, wanted him to study medicine. He combined both. Using a silver stain invented by the Italian scientist Camillo Golgi, which darkens a few nerve cells so they stand out, he looked at the nervous system under a microscope. He showed that it is made of many separate cells, called neurons, not one tangled web. Over five decades he made more than 2,900 drawings of the nervous system. In 1906 he shared the Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine with Golgi.

Rita Levi-Montalcini

1909–2012

Rita Levi-Montalcini was born in Turin, Italy. During World War II, unfair laws against Jewish people barred her from working at a university, so she turned her bedroom into a laboratory and kept studying how nerve fibers grow in chicken embryos. After the war, at Washington University in St. Louis, Missouri, she and Stanley Cohen discovered nerve growth factor, a substance that helps nerve cells grow. In 1986 she and Cohen shared the Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine.

Mark Konishi

1933–2020

Masakazu "Mark" Konishi was born in Kyoto, Japan, and became a professor of biology at the California Institute of Technology in 1975. His lab studied how barn owls use their sharp hearing to find prey on the ground, even in total darkness. His team showed that an owl's brain builds a map of the space around it from sounds, so the owl can "see" with its ears. He also showed that young songbirds memorize the song of an adult tutor, usually a parent, and use that memory to learn their own song.

Erich Jarvis

neuroscientist

Erich Jarvis grew up in Harlem, in New York City, and trained as a ballet dancer at the High School of Performing Arts. In college at Hunter College, he switched from dance to biology, and he earned his doctoral degree at Rockefeller University, where he is now a professor. He studies vocal learning, the ability to learn to make new sounds by copying. Among birds, only songbirds, parrots and hummingbirds can do it. His research looks for the brain pathways and genes that let birds learn songs and let people learn to speak.
