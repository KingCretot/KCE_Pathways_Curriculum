📻 Radios, Cars and a Changing Country

After World War I, the 1920s brought fast change, and people later nicknamed the decade the Roaring Twenties. On November 2, 1920, station KDKA in Pittsburgh, Pennsylvania, began the first scheduled commercial radio broadcast, sharing live results of the presidential election between Warren G. Harding and James Cox. Within about four years, the country had about 600 commercial radio stations. Cars changed life too. Henry Ford's company introduced the Model T in 1908 and began building it on a moving assembly line in 1913. A Model T cost $850 in 1909, but by 1924 the price had dropped to $260. By the time Ford stopped making it in May 1927, the company had built more than 15 million.

👁 Watch Me Think

I ask who could share in these new things. Radio carried jazz into homes across the country, including music by Black bandleaders like Duke Ellington, who began broadcasting live from Harlem's Cotton Club in 1928. But the Cotton Club was strictly segregated: Black musicians played on its stage, yet Black people were not allowed in its audience. So I notice that new machines spread quickly, but fairness did not spread with them.

🎷 The Harlem Renaissance

During the Great Migration, so many Black families moved to Harlem, a neighborhood in New York City, that it became a center of Black culture. In the 1920s, writers, musicians and artists there created so much new work that we call it the Harlem Renaissance. In 1925, Alain Locke, a philosopher, published The New Negro, a book of stories, poems and essays that introduced the movement. (In the 1920s, "Negro" was a common word for Black Americans. Today we say Black or African American.) Langston Hughes, born in Joplin, Missouri, in 1901, published his poem "The Negro Speaks of Rivers" in the magazine The Crisis in 1921. Louis Armstrong, a trumpet player born in New Orleans, moved to Chicago in 1922 to join King Oliver's band. Duke Ellington, from Washington, D.C., became the bandleader at Harlem's Cotton Club in 1927.

👁 Watch Me Think

Now I look at a Florida writer you met in Week 1. Zora Neale Hurston grew up in Eatonville, Florida, one of the first towns founded and governed by Black Americans. She studied anthropology at Barnard College with Franz Boas and graduated in 1928. She went back to Florida to collect the folklore of Black communities and published it in Mules and Men in 1935. Her novel Their Eyes Were Watching God came out in 1937. Yet when she died in Fort Pierce, Florida, in 1960, her grave had no marker. Years later, the writer Alice Walker found it and placed a marker, and readers rediscovered her books. So I ask: who decides which artists are remembered?

🕊️ Tulsa, 1921, and Who Still Faced Barriers

Not everyone could share in the new freedoms. In Tulsa, Oklahoma, Black families had built Greenwood, a busy neighborhood of homes, churches, newspapers, theaters and Black-owned businesses, also known as "Black Wall Street." On May 30, 1921, a Black shoe shiner named Dick Rowland and a white elevator operator named Sarah Page were in an elevator together. The next day, a Tulsa newspaper falsely reported that he had attacked her. That night, a white mob gathered, and on May 31 and June 1, 1921, it attacked Greenwood. In about 18 hours, homes, churches and stores were burned. The American Red Cross counted 1,256 buildings burned and about 10,000 people left homeless. Historians estimate that between 50 and 300 people were killed, most of them Black. No white attackers were sent to prison. Black Tulsans began rebuilding within days.

👁 Watch Me Think

How do we know what happened? In 1921, the Red Cross wrote that it could not give a number of deaths: "Figures are omitted for the reason that NO ONE KNOWS." In 1997, Oklahoma formed a commission to investigate. Survivors' memories are evidence too. Viola Fletcher was 7 years old in 1921. In 2021, at age 107, she told Congress, "I felt safe. I had everything a child could need," and then the massacre took it away. She also said, "Our country may forget this history. I cannot. I will not." She died in 2025 at age 111. So I ask: whose memories keep a history alive when others try to forget it?

🤝 Let's Try Together: Rebuilding Greenwood

Devin asks, "If Greenwood burned, how did anyone stay?" Let's look at the evidence with him. Black lawyer B. C. Franklin set up a tent office in Greenwood and defended the rights of massacre victims. When the city passed a new fire rule that blocked rebuilding, Franklin took the city and the mayor to court, and he won. Families rebuilt, though thousands spent the winter of 1921 to 1922 in tents, and many never fully recovered what they lost. Dionne notices something: the people of Greenwood were not only victims; they were also builders who refused to give up.

🔍 Source Detective: A Poem, an Essay and a Report from the 1920s

Here are three short primary sources: a poem from 1925, an essay from 1928 and a Red Cross report from 1921. Read each part, then compare them.

Primary Sources: 1921, 1925 and 1928

Part 1 (Langston Hughes, "I, Too," 1925): "I, too, sing America. / I am the darker brother. / They send me to eat in the kitchen / When company comes," ... The poem ends: "I, too, am America."

Part 2 (Zora Neale Hurston, "How It Feels to Be Colored Me," 1928): "But I am not tragically colored. There is no great sorrow dammed up in my soul, nor lurking behind my eyes."

Part 3 (American Red Cross report on Tulsa, 1921): The report lists 1,256 buildings burned and about 10,000 people left homeless. About the dead, it says: "Figures are omitted for the reason that NO ONE KNOWS."

Word help: darker brother = a Black American, part of the American family; company = guests; tragically = in a deeply sad way; dammed up = held back, like water behind a dam; lurking = hiding; figures = numbers; omitted = left out.

Part 1: Langston Hughes, "I, Too," first published in Survey Graphic, March 1925, later in The Weary Blues (1926). Part 2: Zora Neale Hurston, "How It Feels to Be Colored Me," The World Tomorrow, May 1928. Part 3: American Red Cross summary report on the Tulsa disaster relief, 1921 (National Archives, DocsTeach); the numbers are listed in the report, and the quoted sentence is its exact wording. Real wording; short parts.

👁 Think Like a Historian

First I check the type of each source: a poem, an essay and a report. A poem and an essay show how people felt and what they believed. A report gives numbers, but it can also tell us what its writers did not know. Then I compare. Hughes and Hurston were both shut out by racism, yet both claimed their place with pride. The Red Cross report shows how much was destroyed in Greenwood, and it admits that no one could count the dead.

🎶 People of the 1920s

Some wrote, some played music, some kept history alive. Read each card and ask: what did this person create or do, and what did they push against?

Langston Hughes

1901–1967

Born in Joplin, Missouri, he grew up mostly in Kansas. He published "The Negro Speaks of Rivers" in The Crisis in 1921, and his first book of poems, The Weary Blues, came out in 1926. He wrote about everyday Black life in Harlem in the rhythms of jazz and the blues. He died in New York City in 1967.

Zora Neale Hurston

1891–1960

Born in Notasulga, Alabama, she grew up in Eatonville, Florida, where her father became one of the town's first mayors. She graduated from Barnard College in 1928, collected Florida folklore for Mules and Men (1935) and wrote Their Eyes Were Watching God (1937). She died in Fort Pierce, Florida.

Louis Armstrong

1901–1971

Born in New Orleans, Louisiana, he moved to Chicago in 1922 to play in King Oliver's band. In 1925 he began making records under his own name with his Hot Five band. His trumpet playing and singing helped make jazz famous around the world.

Duke Ellington

1899–1974

Born Edward Kennedy Ellington in Washington, D.C., he moved to New York in 1923. He became the bandleader at Harlem's Cotton Club in 1927 and broadcast on the radio from there starting in 1928. But at the Cotton Club, Black people were not allowed in the audience.

Viola Fletcher

1914–2025

She was 7 years old when her family had to flee Greenwood during the 1921 Tulsa Race Massacre. Her family lost their home, and she never finished school past the fourth grade. In 2021, at age 107, she spoke to Congress and asked the country to acknowledge what happened. She died in 2025 at age 111.
