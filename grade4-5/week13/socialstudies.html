🏮 Freedom Seekers and the Underground Railroad

By 1860, about 4 million people were enslaved in the United States, most of them in the South. Many resisted, and thousands became freedom seekers who escaped north to free states and Canada, or south to Mexico, which had ended slavery. The Underground Railroad was not a train but a secret network of people and safe houses; guides were called "conductors" and hiding places were "stations." Most of the work was done by Black Americans, free and enslaved, with help from white allies such as Quakers. After the Fugitive Slave Act of 1850 forced people in free states to help capture freedom seekers, many kept going all the way to Canada.

👁 Watch Me Think

When I hear "railroad," I picture trains, so I remind myself it was a code name. Then I ask a historian's question: who took the biggest risks? The freedom seekers did. They walked at night, hid in swamps and attics, and could be captured and sold away from their families. Most escaped on their own or with help from other Black people, so I make sure their courage is the center of the story.

🤝 Let's Try Together: Harriet Tubman's First Escape

In 1849, Harriet Tubman escaped from slavery on Maryland's Eastern Shore and traveled about 90 miles to Philadelphia, in the free state of Pennsylvania. She moved mostly at night and steered by the North Star. Let's think it through: Pennsylvania had ended slavery, so she was free under that state's law. But after the Fugitive Slave Act of 1850, freedom seekers could be captured even in free states. So when Tubman went back to guide others, she often led them all the way to St. Catharines, in Canada. Over about 13 trips she guided about 70 people to freedom.

📜 Why Was the Civil War Fought?

In the 1850s, Americans argued over whether slavery could spread into the new western lands. In November 1860, Abraham Lincoln was elected president; he promised not to end slavery where it already existed, but he opposed letting it spread. Starting with South Carolina in December 1860, Southern states voted to secede and formed the Confederacy, and in April 1861, Confederate forces fired on Fort Sumter in Charleston Harbor, starting the Civil War. In their own official statements, the seceding states said the main reason they left was to protect slavery.

👁 Watch Me Think

Some people today say the war was about "states' rights." A historian checks evidence written at the time. When I read the states' own declarations, I ask: a right to do what? Those documents answer clearly: the right to keep people enslaved and to take slavery west. On the Union side, most Northerners first fought to keep the country together. Ending slavery became a Union war goal later.

🤝 Let's Try Together: A Confederate Leader's Own Words

Let's test the "states' rights" claim with a speech by Alexander Stephens, the Confederacy's vice president. In March 1861, he said the new Confederate government's "cornerstone" was the idea that Black people were not equal to white people and that slavery was their natural condition. A cornerstone is the first stone a building rests on. So a top Confederate leader said that slavery and racism were the foundation of the Confederacy. This is strong evidence because it comes from the Confederacy itself, at the time.

⛓️‍💥 Emancipation and the Fight for Freedom

At first, Lincoln said his main goal was to save the Union, not to end slavery. But enslaved people forced the question by escaping to Union lines by the thousands, and Black and white abolitionists kept pushing. On January 1, 1863, Lincoln's Emancipation Proclamation declared free the enslaved people in Confederate areas and allowed Black men to join the Union army and navy. About 180,000 Black men served in the Union army, including the 54th Massachusetts, and about 19,000 more served in the navy. The Union won in 1865, and that year the 13th Amendment ended slavery in the whole country.

👁 Watch Me Think

The Proclamation sounds like it freed everyone, so I check the details. It did not free people in the border states that stayed loyal, and it could only be enforced where the Union army reached. Still, it changed the war: every Union victory now meant freedom, and Black soldiers could fight for their own people. So I call it a turning point, not the finish line.

🧩 Picture It: The Emancipation Proclamation: Did and Did Not

It didIt did not
Declare free the enslaved people in Confederate areasFree people in the loyal border states
Let Black men join the Union army and navyFree anyone the Union army could not yet reach
Make ending slavery a Union war goalEnd slavery forever (the 13th Amendment did that in 1865)

🤝 Let's Try Together: The 54th Massachusetts at Fort Wagner

The 54th Massachusetts was one of the first official Black regiments raised in the North, and two of Frederick Douglass's sons joined it. On July 18, 1863, the 54th led an attack on Fort Wagner near Charleston, South Carolina. The fort was not captured, many soldiers were killed or wounded, and their white colonel, Robert Gould Shaw, was killed. Sergeant William Carney saved the regiment's flag and later received the Medal of Honor. Let's weigh it: they lost the battle, but they proved their courage to a doubting nation, and more Black men enlisted. Black soldiers were also paid less than white soldiers, so the 54th refused any pay for more than a year until Congress made pay equal in 1864.

🔍 Source Detective: In Their Own Words

Did the Southern states leave the Union to protect slavery? Instead of guessing, let's read what their leaders wrote at the time, and what Lincoln promised when he took office. Read three short parts.

Primary Sources: Two Secession Declarations and Lincoln's First Inaugural Address (1860–1861)

Part 1, South Carolina (December 24, 1860): "...an increasing hostility on the part of the non-slaveholding States to the institution of slavery..." The states of the North "have united in the election of a man to the high office of President of the United States, whose opinions and purposes are hostile to slavery."

Part 2, Mississippi (January 1861): "Our position is thoroughly identified with the institution of slavery, the greatest material interest of the world."

Part 3, Abraham Lincoln (March 4, 1861): "I have no purpose, directly or indirectly, to interfere with the institution of slavery in the States where it exists."

Word help: hostility = strong dislike; non-slaveholding States = states without slavery; institution = an established system; thoroughly identified with = completely tied to; material interest = something that brings money; interfere = get in the way.

South Carolina's Declaration of the Immediate Causes of Secession (December 24, 1860) and Mississippi's Declaration of the Immediate Causes of Secession (January 1861), Avalon Project, Yale Law School; Abraham Lincoln, First Inaugural Address, March 4, 1861, Library of Congress. Real wording, shortened; ... marks show where words were left out, and the words before each quotation are a summary.

👁 Think Like a Historian

First I check who wrote each part and when. Parts 1 and 2 were written by the leaders of states that were leaving the Union, so they show their own reasons, in their own words, at the time. Part 3 is Lincoln's promise. Then I compare: if Lincoln promised not to touch slavery where it existed, why did the states still leave? Their own words show they feared slavery would be limited and, in time, ended.

✊🏾 People Who Fought Slavery, and a President Told Honestly

Some fought slavery with words, some with daring escapes, and some on the battlefield. Read each card and ask: what did this person do, and what risks did they take?

Frederick Douglass

about 1818–1895

Born enslaved in Maryland, he taught himself to read and escaped in 1838 disguised as a sailor. His 1845 book, Narrative of the Life of Frederick Douglass, made him famous, and in 1847 he started an antislavery newspaper, The North Star. During the war he recruited Black soldiers, including two of his sons, and met with Lincoln to demand equal treatment for them.

Harriet Tubman

about 1822–1913

Born Araminta Ross in Maryland, she escaped in 1849 and returned about 13 times to guide about 70 people to freedom. She later said she "never lost a passenger." In the Civil War she worked as a Union scout, spy and nurse, and in June 1863 she helped lead the Combahee River Raid in South Carolina, which freed more than 700 enslaved people.

Sojourner Truth

about 1797–1883

Born Isabella in New York, where her first language was Dutch, she walked away from slavery with her baby daughter in 1826. In 1828 she won a court case to get her young son Peter back after he was illegally sold to Alabama. She renamed herself Sojourner Truth and spoke for abolition and women's rights. Her famous 1851 speech exists in two versions, and the best-known one was written down 12 years later by someone else.

Robert Smalls

1839–1915

An enslaved ship pilot (his official job title was "wheelman") in Charleston, South Carolina. Before dawn on May 13, 1862, while the white officers were ashore, he and the enslaved crew took the Confederate ship Planter, picked up their families, gave the right signals as they passed Confederate forts, and handed the ship to the Union navy. His escape helped convince Union leaders to let Black men fight.

William Lloyd Garrison

1805–1879

A white abolitionist in Boston who started the newspaper The Liberator on January 1, 1831. He demanded an immediate end to slavery and wrote, "I will not retreat a single inch, and I will be heard." He helped found the American Anti-Slavery Society and printed the words of Black writers and speakers.

Abraham Lincoln

1809–1865

The 16th president. He believed slavery was wrong and opposed its spread, but in 1858 he said he did not support full equality for Black Americans, and in 1862 he still urged Black Americans to move to a colony outside the country. In 1862 he wrote that saving the Union was his "paramount object." Pushed by events and by Black leaders, he issued the Emancipation Proclamation, fought for the 13th Amendment, and in his last speech supported voting for some Black men. He was assassinated in April 1865.
