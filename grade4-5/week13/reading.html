🖼️ Picture It: Figurative Language

Literal words mean exactly what they say. Figurative language uses words in a creative way to paint a picture or make a point. When a poet writes, “The river is a silver ribbon,” nobody tied a ribbon in the valley. The poet means the river is long, thin, and shiny. To understand a figure of speech, ask: What is really being described, and what does the comparison tell me about it?

DeviceHow to spot itExampleWhat it really means
SimileCompares two different things using like or as.The terraces rise like giant green stairs.The fields are stacked in steps up the mountain.
MetaphorSays one thing is another, with no like or as.The river is a silver ribbon.The river is long, thin, and shiny.
PersonificationGives a non-human thing human actions or feelings.The wind grabbed Killa’s wool hat and ran off with it.The wind blew her hat away.
HyperboleA huge exaggeration that is not meant literally.This hill is a million miles high!The hill is very tall and tiring to climb.
ImagerySensory details that help you see, hear, smell, taste, or feel.Warm, salty potato soup steamed in the clay bowl.You can almost smell, taste, and feel the hot soup.
⚠️

Simile or Metaphor? Check the Signal Word

Both similes and metaphors compare two things. If you see like or as making the comparison, it's a simile. If the writer says one thing simply is (or was) the other, it's a metaphor.

🖼️ Picture It: The Parts of a Poem

Poems are built differently from stories. Instead of sentences in paragraphs, a poem is made of lines grouped into stanzas. Many poems use rhyme and a steady rhythm, but some don't. Study this stanza about llamas carrying loads down a mountain trail.

WordWhat it means
LineOne row of words in a poem. A line can stop before the sentence ends.
StanzaA group of lines, like a paragraph in a story. A blank space separates stanzas.
RhymeWords that end with the same sound, like trail and fail.
Rhyme schemeThe pattern of rhymes at the ends of lines, shown with letters. Give the first ending sound the letter A. A new sound gets B, then C. A line that rhymes with an earlier line gets that line's letter. So ABAB means lines 1 and 3 rhyme and lines 2 and 4 rhyme; ABCB means only lines 2 and 4 rhyme; AABB means each two lines in a row rhyme.
RhythmThe beat you hear when you read a poem aloud. Clap it: the LLA-mas CLIMB the ROCK-y TRAIL has 4 beats.
Free verseA poem with no set rhyme pattern. It still uses lines, stanzas, and strong images.

🌫️ Poem 1: Morning Fog

🏞️

About the Village

Long ago, the Inca and earlier Andean peoples carved terraces into the steep mountainsides: flat fields stacked like steps, held up by stone walls. Many families in villages near Cusco, Peru, still farm terraces today, growing potatoes, corn, and quinoa. On cool mornings, fog often fills the valleys below.

Morning Fog

The fog creeps up the valley
like an alpaca, slow and sweet.
It tucks the terraced fields in white,
a blanket soft and neat.

The sun climbs up the mountain
and stretches out its arms.
The fog lets go of every field
and drifts off past the farms.

🧶 Poem 2: Grandma’s Loom

🧵

About the Weaving

Many Andean weavers use a backstrap loom. One end is tied to a post or tree, and the other end is fastened to a strap around the weaver's back, so she leans back to keep the threads tight. The yarn is often spun from alpaca or sheep wool and colored with natural dyes. A deep red can come from cochineal, tiny insects that live on cactus plants.

Grandma’s Loom

Grandma ties her loom up to a post
and sits where sunlight warms her most.
The wooden sticks go tap, tap, tap.
A rainbow grows across her lap.

Her yarn is a river, red and gold,
that flows into blankets for the cold.
Her fingers fly as quick as birds
and weave our stories without words.

She’s woven enough to blanket every peak,
and still she weaves on, week by week.

🛒 Poem 3: Market Day

🥔

About the Market

Many Andean towns hold a weekly market in the main plaza. Families bring potatoes (the Andes are home to thousands of kinds!), corn, cheese, and woven cloth to sell or trade. In the mountains of Peru, many people speak Quechua, a language spoken in the Andes for centuries, as well as Spanish. Alpacas really do hum, a soft sound they make to each other.

Market Day

On Sunday the plaza wakes up early,
yawning under bright blue tarps.
Potatoes tumble out of woven sacks:
purple, yellow, red, and speckled,
more colors than the sky has stars.

I smell steaming corn and fresh cheese.
I hear Quechua and Spanish
stirred together like soup.
An alpaca hums beside its owner,
its wool as soft as a cloud,
and I bury my fingers in its warm coat.

💡 What Does It Really Mean?

🔎

Three Steps to Explain a Figure of Speech

1. Name it. Is it a simile, metaphor, personification, or hyperbole?
2. Find the two things. What is being described, and what is it compared to or given?
3. Say it plainly. What does the comparison tell you? Example: “The terraces rise like giant green stairs” is a simile. The terraces are compared to stairs. Plainly: the fields are stacked in flat steps up the mountain.
