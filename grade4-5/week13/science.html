⚖️ Balanced and Unbalanced Forces

A force is a push or a pull, and every force has a strength and a direction. Scientists measure force in units called newtons, named after Isaac Newton. When the forces on an object are balanced, they are equal and opposite, so they cancel out: a still object stays still, and a moving object keeps the same speed and direction. When the forces are unbalanced, the object starts, stops, speeds up, slows down or turns toward the stronger force. Newton's first law says an object keeps doing what it is doing until an unbalanced force acts on it, and this resistance to change is called inertia.

👁 Watch Me Think

Two teams play tug-of-war. Team A pulls left with 300 newtons, and Team B pulls right with 300 newtons. The rope does not move. Does that mean there are no forces? No! Two big forces are acting, but they cancel, so they are balanced. Now Team B pulls with 350 newtons. I subtract: 350 minus 300 is 50 newtons left over, pointing right. That leftover is called the net force. It is unbalanced, so the rope moves toward Team B. Every time, I draw the arrows, compare their sizes and find what is left over.

🤝 Let's Try Together: Why Does a Rolling Ball Stop?

For about 2,000 years, many people believed what the Greek thinker Aristotle taught: moving things stop on their own because stopping is natural. Let's test that idea. Roll the same ball with the same push across grass, then a gym floor, then smooth ice. On grass it stops quickly, on the gym floor it rolls much farther, and on ice it glides farther still. Each time there is less friction, the ball goes farther. Now imagine a surface with no friction and no air at all. The ball would never stop! In the early 1600s, Galileo Galilei reasoned this way with balls rolling on ramps, and Newton built his first law on Galileo's idea. Things do not stop on their own; a force stops them.

🛝 Friction and Gravity

Friction is a force that acts when two surfaces rub or slide against each other, and it always works against the motion. Rough surfaces like carpet and sandpaper make more friction than smooth ones like ice and polished wood. Friction is often helpful: it lets shoe soles grip the floor, brakes stop a bike, and your fingers hold a pencil. It can also cause problems, because it wears parts down and changes motion energy into heat, which is why your hands feel warm when you rub them together fast. Gravity is the pull between all objects with mass, and Earth's gravity pulls everything toward Earth's center, which is why dropped objects always fall down.

👁 Watch Me Think

If I drop a book and a flat sheet of paper at the same time, the book lands first, and the paper drifts down slowly. Does gravity pull harder on heavy things, so they fall faster? Let me test it. I crumple the paper into a tight ball and drop both again. Now they land at almost the same time! The paper did not get heavier, so what changed? The flat paper was slowed by air resistance, a kind of friction from the air. The evidence shows that objects fall at nearly the same rate when air does not get in the way. In 1971, astronaut David Scott tested this on the Moon, where there is no air: a hammer and a feather hit the ground together.

🧩 Picture It: Friction: Helpful or a Problem?

Helpful frictionFriction that causes problems

To reduce friction, people use oil, wheels, ball bearings and smooth surfaces. To increase it, they use rough treads, rubber and sand.

🤝 Let's Try Together: The Slipping Shoe Test

Which slides first on a tilted board: a sneaker with bumpy rubber tread or a shoe with a smooth sole? Let's plan a fair test. We use the same board and put each shoe at the same starting spot. We raise one end of the board slowly and measure how high the end is when the shoe starts to slide. We repeat it three times for each shoe. Suppose the smooth shoe slides when the board is raised about 10 inches, and the sneaker holds on until about 25 inches. The sneaker needed a steeper slope before it slipped, so its sole makes more friction. That is why gym shoes have rubber tread.

🔧 Simple Machines

A simple machine is a tool with few or no moving parts that changes the size or direction of a force to make work easier. There are six: the lever, the wheel and axle, the pulley, the inclined plane (a ramp), the wedge and the screw. Simple machines do not give you something for nothing: they let you use a smaller force, but you must push or pull over a longer distance. Movers push a piano up a long, gentle ramp instead of lifting it straight up, and a long bar lets a small push lift a heavy rock. Most tools, like scissors, bicycles and can openers, are compound machines made of two or more simple machines working together.

👁 Watch Me Think

I want to lift a heavy rock with a long board. I slide one end under the rock and rest the board on a small log, which is the fulcrum, the point the lever turns on. If the log is close to the rock, my end of the board is long. I push down with a small force, but my hands move a long way down while the rock rises only a little. If I move the log closer to me, I have to push much harder. So a lever trades force for distance: less force, more distance. Around 250 BCE, the Greek scientist Archimedes explained how levers work and is said to have boasted that with a long enough lever he could move the world.

🤝 Let's Try Together: Ramp Math

A mover needs to get a 100-pound box onto a truck bed 3 feet high. Lifting it straight up takes about 100 pounds of force over 3 feet. Now she uses a ramp 12 feet long. The ramp is 4 times longer than the height, so, if we ignore friction, she needs only about one-fourth of the force: 25 pounds. But she pushes for 12 feet instead of 3. Let's check: 100 × 3 = 300, and 25 × 12 = 300. The work comes out the same; the ramp just spreads it over a longer distance with a smaller force. In real life, friction adds a little extra force, so the push is a bit more than 25 pounds.

🚗 Data Lab: The Friction Test

A class wanted to know how different surfaces change the motion of a toy car. They let the same toy car roll down the same ramp, from the same starting height, onto four different surfaces. Then they measured how far the car rolled past the bottom of the ramp. They ran three trials on each surface and found the average.

👁 Think Like a Physicist

A physicist first checks that the test was fair: only one variable, the surface, was changed, and the car, ramp and starting height stayed the same. Next she looks at the averages and lines the surfaces up from shortest roll to longest. A shorter roll means more friction stopped the car sooner. Finally she checks the three trials for each surface. If they are close together, she trusts the average more.

🍎 People Who Moved Our Understanding of Motion

A scientist, an astronaut, an inventor and an engineer: each one used forces and motion in a different way.

Isaac Newton

1642–1727

Isaac Newton grew up on a farm in Woolsthorpe, England. In 1687 he published a book now called the Principia, which explained his three laws of motion and his law of gravity: the same force that pulls an apple to the ground also keeps the Moon in its orbit. Late in life, he told his friend William Stukeley that watching an apple fall in a garden started him thinking about gravity. There is no evidence that it hit him on the head! Newton also showed that white sunlight is a mix of colors. He wrote that if he had seen further, it was "by standing on the shoulders of giants," meaning he built on earlier scientists like Galileo.

Ellen Ochoa

born 1958

Ellen Ochoa was born in Los Angeles and grew up in La Mesa, California. She earned a doctorate in electrical engineering from Stanford University and is a co-inventor on three patents for optical systems that use light to study objects. In April 1993, aboard the space shuttle Discovery, she became the first Hispanic woman to go to space. On four shuttle flights she spent nearly 1,000 hours in space, ran the shuttle's robotic arm, and even played her flute in orbit. In space, a floating object keeps drifting until a force stops it, so astronauts see Newton's first law every day. From 2013 to 2018 she was director of NASA's Johnson Space Center in Houston.

Elijah McCoy

1844–1929

Elijah McCoy was born in Canada to parents who had escaped slavery in Kentucky on the Underground Railroad. He studied mechanical engineering in Scotland, but when he moved to Michigan, the only railroad job offered to him, a Black engineer, was fireman and oiler. Trains had to stop often so workers could oil the moving parts by hand; without oil, friction made the parts grind and wear out. In 1872 McCoy patented an automatic lubricator that dripped oil into the engine while the train kept running. He earned more than 50 patents. Some people say the phrase "the real McCoy" comes from him, but historians are not sure.

Ismail al-Jazari

about 1136–1206

Al-Jazari was an engineer who worked for the Artuqid rulers in what is now southeastern Türkiye. In 1206 he finished The Book of Knowledge of Ingenious Mechanical Devices, with careful drawings and instructions for about 50 machines. His designs used gears, levers, pulleys, valves and the force of flowing water, including machines that raised water for farms and towns and a famous "elephant clock." Because he explained exactly how to build each machine, engineers today can still study and rebuild his inventions.
