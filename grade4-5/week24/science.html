🗺️ Patterns on the Map

If you mark every strong earthquake on a world map, the dots do not land at random. They line up in long, narrow belts, and active volcanoes line up in many of the same belts. The biggest belt circles the edges of the Pacific Ocean. It is called the Ring of Fire, and the U.S. Geological Survey (USGS) says about 90 percent of the world's earthquakes happen there. Another belt runs down the middle of the Atlantic Ocean along an undersea mountain range called the Mid-Atlantic Ridge. Tall, young mountains such as the Andes and the Himalayas follow belts, too. In 1912, a German scientist named Alfred Wegener argued that the continents had once been joined and had slowly drifted apart. The coasts of South America and Africa fit together like puzzle pieces, and the same fossils, such as Mesosaurus, a reptile that lived in fresh water, are found on continents now separated by an ocean. Most scientists rejected his idea, mainly because he could not explain what force could move whole continents. In the 1950s, Marie Tharp's maps of the ocean floor showed a deep valley running along the top of the Mid-Atlantic Ridge, right where earthquakes cluster. Clues like these led to the theory of plate tectonics in the 1960s.

🤝 Let's Try Together: Weigh Wegener's Clues

Let's weigh Wegener's clues together. Clue one: the coastlines of South America and Africa fit like puzzle pieces. That is interesting, but shapes alone could be a coincidence. Clue two: fossils of Mesosaurus, a reptile that lived in fresh water, are found in both South America and Africa. A freshwater reptile was very unlikely to swim across a salty ocean, so the two continents were probably joined when it lived. Clue three: fossils of the same fern, Glossopteris, are found on all the southern continents. Together, the clues are strong. What was missing? An explanation of how the continents could move. The ocean-floor maps of the 1950s and the idea of plates gave scientists that explanation.

🌋 Plates and Their Boundaries

Earth's outer shell, the crust and the stiff top of the mantle, is broken into large pieces called plates. The National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration counts about 15 to 20 of them, including roughly a dozen major plates. Below them is hotter mantle rock that is solid but can flow very slowly. Plates move only a few centimeters a year, about as fast as your fingernails grow, but over millions of years that adds up to thousands of kilometers. Most earthquakes, volcanoes and young mountains form at plate boundaries, where plates meet. At a divergent boundary, plates pull apart, and magma rises to make new crust. The Mid-Atlantic Ridge spreads about 2.5 centimeters a year, and the country of Iceland sits right on top of it. At a convergent boundary, plates push together. When an ocean plate meets a continent, the denser ocean plate sinks under the other plate, a process called subduction. Subduction makes deep ocean trenches, strong earthquakes and chains of volcanoes, like the Andes in South America and the Cascade Range in Washington and Oregon. When two continents collide, their rock is too light to sink, so the crust crumples and is pushed up. India colliding with Asia pushed up the Himalayas, the highest mountains on land. At a transform boundary, plates slide past each other. Along the San Andreas fault in California, the Pacific Plate grinds past the North American Plate at about 5 centimeters a year. And the Hawaii puzzle? Its volcanoes sit near the middle of the Pacific Plate, more than 2,000 miles from the nearest boundary, above a hot spot deep below.

👁 Watch Me Think

Cedric wonders why Washington State has big volcanoes like Mount St. Helens, but Florida has none. Here is how I reason. Off the coast of Washington, an ocean plate is sinking under the North American Plate. That is subduction at a convergent boundary. As the plate sinks, magma forms deep below and rises, so a chain of volcanoes, the Cascade Range, grows inland from the coast. Florida sits in the middle of the North American Plate, far from any boundary, so there is no sinking plate to make magma. Then I check the Hawaii puzzle: Hawaii is in the middle of a plate too, but it sits over a hot spot, a special case that is not a boundary at all.

🏗️ Living with Moving Plates

We cannot stop plates from moving, but scientists and engineers can reduce the damage. Earthquakes are described by magnitude. The U.S. Geological Survey explains that each whole-number step up in magnitude means ten times more ground motion measured on a seismogram and about 32 times more energy released. Since about 1900, the world has averaged about 18 earthquakes of magnitude 7.0 or larger each year. Engineers design buildings to bend without breaking, using steel frames, bracing and building codes, which are safety rules for construction. Some important buildings sit on base isolators, thick pads that let the ground shift beneath the building. During the 1994 Northridge earthquake in California, the 149 isolators under the University of Southern California's University Hospital cut the shaking that reached the building by about two-thirds, and the hospital was not damaged. On the West Coast, the U.S. Geological Survey runs ShakeAlert. Sensors detect an earthquake that has already started and send a warning, usually seconds before strong shaking arrives, because signals travel much faster than earthquake waves. Those seconds give people time to Drop, Cover and Hold On, and let trains slow down. At volcanoes, scientists use seismometers to sense small earthquakes, Global Positioning System (GPS) receivers and tiltmeters to measure the ground swelling as magma collects, gas sensors and cameras. In 2018, the U.S. Geological Survey listed 161 volcanoes in the United States that are active or could become active, with 18 rated very high threat, including Kilauea in Hawaii and Mount St. Helens and Mount Rainier in Washington.

👁 Watch Me Think

Chiara visits her cousin in Seattle, Washington, and his phone has ShakeAlert. She asks, "If it cannot predict earthquakes, how can it warn anyone?" Here is how I think about it. ShakeAlert does not know an earthquake is coming days ahead. Nobody can do that. Instead, sensors near the start of an earthquake feel the first waves. A computer figures out where the earthquake is and how big it is, and sends a message. Messages travel far faster than earthquake waves, so people farther away may get a few seconds of warning. A few seconds is enough to Drop, Cover and Hold On under a sturdy table.

🤝 Let's Try Together: Compare Magnitudes

Let's compare earthquakes together. A magnitude 7 earthquake has ten times more ground motion on a seismogram than a magnitude 6, and releases about 32 times more energy. What about a magnitude 8 compared with a magnitude 6? That is two steps. Ground motion: 10 times 10 is 100 times more. Energy: 32 times 32 is 1,024, so about 1,000 times more energy. Each step makes a huge difference. Try It at Home: build a tower of blocks or index cards on a tray on the floor and gently slide the tray back and forth. Then add cross braces with tape and test again, or rest the tray on a few round pencils as a model base isolator. Use only light, soft materials. 👁 Think Like a Seismologist

A seismologist reads magnitude carefully. Magnitude is one number for the whole earthquake, but the shaking you feel depends on where you are. Distance matters, including how deep the earthquake starts. That is why a deep earthquake can be large yet cause little damage. I also remember that magnitude grows fast: each whole-number step means ten times more ground motion and about 32 times more energy.

🌍 Scientists Who Discovered Moving Plates

It took scientists from many countries, over many decades, to discover that Earth's surface moves.

Alfred Wegener

1880–1930

Alfred Wegener was born in Berlin, Germany. He earned a doctorate in astronomy but became a scientist who studied weather and climate, and an explorer of Greenland. On January 6, 1912, he told a meeting of scientists that the continents had once been joined and had drifted apart. His 1915 book, The Origin of Continents and Oceans, used fitting coastlines and matching fossils as evidence. Most scientists rejected the idea because he could not explain the force that moved continents. He died on an expedition across the Greenland ice cap in 1930, decades before plate tectonics proved him partly right.

Marie Tharp

1920–2006

Marie Tharp was an American geologist and mapmaker at Columbia University's Lamont Geological Laboratory in New York. Early in her career, women were not allowed on the research ships, so she made maps from the depth measurements that others collected. In 1952 she noticed a V-shaped notch along the top of the Mid-Atlantic Ridge and concluded it was a rift valley where the seafloor was spreading apart. Her partner Bruce Heezen first dismissed the idea, but he was convinced. She first went to sea in 1968, and in 1977 she and Heezen finished a map of the whole world's ocean floor.

Kiyoo Wadati

1902–1995

Kiyoo Wadati was a Japanese seismologist who worked at the Central Meteorological Observatory of Japan, now the Japan Meteorological Agency. In papers he wrote before 1936, he gave the first convincing evidence that some earthquakes start very deep inside Earth. He also gave the first accurate description of a slanting zone of deep earthquakes that reaches down from ocean trenches beneath island arcs of volcanoes, like Japan. Decades later, scientists saw that this zone traces a plate sinking into the mantle. It is now called the Wadati-Benioff zone.

Waverly Person

died 2022

Waverly Person was an African American seismologist who served in the U.S. Army in Korea and later studied geophysics. He helped found the U.S. Geological Survey's National Earthquake Information Center in Golden, Colorado, and became its chief. His center located earthquakes around the world and shared the news fast, including the powerful 2004 earthquake near Sumatra. He explained earthquakes so often on television and in classrooms that people called him "Mr. Earthquake." He was the first African American to hold such a senior position in the U.S. Department of the Interior, and he retired in 2006.
