💥 Pearl Harbor and a Nation at War

World War II began in Europe on September 1, 1939, when Nazi Germany, led by Adolf Hitler, invaded Poland. For two years the United States stayed out of the fighting. Then, on the morning of December 7, 1941, Japanese planes attacked the U.S. Navy base at Pearl Harbor, on the island of Oahu in Hawaii. The attack killed 2,403 Americans and sank or damaged many ships. The next day, President Roosevelt asked Congress to declare war on Japan, and Congress did that same afternoon. Days later, Germany and Italy declared war on the United States. The United States joined the Allies, including Great Britain, the Soviet Union and China, against the Axis: Germany, Italy and Japan. About 16 million Americans served in the armed forces. At home, families rationed sugar, meat and gasoline, planted victory gardens and collected scrap metal for the war.

👁 Watch Me Think

I notice the date math. The war in Europe began in 1939, but the United States did not join until the end of 1941. So I ask why. Many Americans remembered World War I and wanted to stay out of another war. Pearl Harbor changed their minds almost overnight. One event can be a turning point. I also look for people we do not always hear about. Doris Miller, a Black sailor on the USS West Virginia, worked as a mess attendant, a kitchen and serving job, because the Navy kept most Black sailors out of other jobs. During the attack, he helped wounded shipmates and then fired an anti-aircraft machine gun at the planes. He received the Navy Cross.

🤝 Let's Try Together: Reading the Home Front

Evander, a fourth grader, found a World War II ration book in his great-grandmother's trunk. Let's help him read it like a historian. A ration book held stamps. To buy rationed foods such as sugar, a family needed money and the right stamps. Why ration at all? The military needed huge amounts of food, metal, rubber and fuel, so the government limited what each family could buy so there would be enough to go around. Let's weigh it: rationing asked everyone to give something up, but the war also created millions of new factory jobs, and the Great Depression finally ended. One question for Evander to ask his family: who in our family worked or served during the war?

🪖 Serving a Country That Did Not Treat Them Equally

Americans of every background served, often while facing unfair treatment at home. In 1942, the Marines recruited 29 young Navajo men to create a secret code based on the Navajo language. About 400 Navajo Code Talkers served in battles across the Pacific, including Iwo Jima, and the code was never broken. At Tuskegee, Alabama, the Army trained the first Black military pilots, the Tuskegee Airmen. About 1,000 pilots trained there, and their fighter group painted the tails of its planes red, so they were nicknamed the Red Tails. Almost all Black troops served in segregated units throughout the war. On the home front, more than six million women went to work, many building ships, planes and tanks. Rosie the Riveter, a made-up factory worker from a 1942 song, became their symbol. About 350,000 women served in the military, including nearly 1,100 Women Airforce Service Pilots who tested and delivered military planes.

👁 Watch Me Think

I notice a hard question hiding here. Chester Nez, one of the first 29 Code Talkers, had been punished as a boy at a boarding school for speaking Navajo. Remember our Week 18 lesson about those schools? The language the government once tried to erase helped win battles. In 1942, the Pittsburgh Courier, a Black newspaper, began the Double V campaign: victory over enemies abroad and victory over racism at home. So I ask: what does it mean to fight for freedom overseas when you are not treated as equal at home?

🤝 Let's Try Together: The Code That Was Never Broken

Let's see why the Navajo code worked so well. Navajo had no written alphabet at the time, and very few people outside the Navajo Nation spoke it. The Code Talkers also made up code words for military terms. For example, names of birds stood for different kinds of airplanes. At Iwo Jima in 1945, six Code Talkers worked around the clock during the first two days and sent more than 800 messages without a mistake. Major Howard Connor, a Marine signal officer, said, "Were it not for the Navajos, the Marines would never have taken Iwo Jima." For many years the code stayed a military secret. In 2001, the first 29 Code Talkers received the Congressional Gold Medal. Let's weigh it: why might it matter that they were honored more than 55 years after the war?

🏚️ Japanese American Incarceration and the Holocaust

After Pearl Harbor, fear and racism turned against Japanese Americans. On February 19, 1942, President Roosevelt signed Executive Order 9066. The Army then forced about 120,000 people of Japanese ancestry on the West Coast to leave their homes, farms and businesses. About two-thirds of them were U.S. citizens. Families could bring only what they could carry. They were held in 10 large camps surrounded by barbed wire and guard towers, in places like Manzanar in California and Minidoka in Idaho. A government commission later found no documented acts of spying or sabotage by Japanese Americans. Fred Korematsu, age 23, refused to go. He was arrested, and in 1944 the Supreme Court ruled against him. In 1983, a federal judge overturned his conviction. In 1988, Congress passed the Civil Liberties Act, apologized and paid $20,000 to each survivor.

👁 Watch Me Think

Now we come to the hardest part of the war, and we will go slowly. In Europe, Nazi Germany's leaders blamed Jewish people for Germany's problems. Step by step, they took away Jewish people's rights, jobs and homes, forced them into crowded neighborhoods called ghettos and sent them to camps. By 1945, the Nazis and those who helped them had murdered about six million Jewish men, women and children. They also killed Roma people, people with disabilities and many others. We call this the Holocaust. Before the war, the United States and other countries let in only a small number of Jewish refugees. In 1939, a ship called the St. Louis carried 937 passengers, almost all of them Jewish refugees, but Cuba, the United States and Canada turned them away. Four European countries took them in, but 254 of them were later killed in the Holocaust. I ask: what could people have done?

🤝 Let's Try Together: Anne Frank and Her Helpers

Anne Frank was a Jewish girl born in Germany in 1929. Her family moved to Amsterdam, in the Netherlands, to escape the Nazis, but the Nazis invaded there too. In July 1942, Anne's family went into hiding in a secret apartment behind her father's office. Eight people hid there for about two years. Helpers, including Miep Gies, risked their own lives to bring food and supplies. Anne kept a diary about her fears and hopes. On August 4, 1944, police found the hiding place. Anne became sick and died in the Bergen-Belsen camp in early 1945, at age 15. Her father, Otto, survived and later had her diary published. Let's be careful historians: the U.S. camps for Japanese Americans were deeply unjust prisons, but they were not death camps. The Holocaust was a planned effort to murder an entire people. We honor the victims by learning their names and stories.

🔍 Source Detective: A Speech, an Order and an Apology

Here are three short primary sources: a president's speech from 1941, a government order posted on walls in 1942, and a law from 1988. Read each part, then compare them.

Primary Sources: 1941, 1942 and 1988

Part 1 (President Franklin D. Roosevelt, speech to Congress, December 8, 1941): "Yesterday, December 7, 1941—a date which will live in infamy—the United States of America was suddenly and deliberately attacked by naval and air forces of the Empire of Japan."

Part 2 (Civilian Exclusion Order No. 41, San Francisco, May 1942): The poster ordered that "all persons of Japanese ancestry, both alien and non-alien, will be evacuated from the above area." Families were told to bring "Bedding and linens (no mattress) for each member of the family."

Part 3 (Civil Liberties Act of 1988): Congress said the incarceration was "motivated largely by racial prejudice, wartime hysteria, and a failure of political leadership," and Congress "apologizes on behalf of the Nation."

Word help: infamy = lasting shame; deliberately = on purpose; ancestry = the family line you come from; alien = a person who is not a citizen; non-alien = a citizen; evacuated = forced to leave; motivated = caused; prejudice = unfair judgment of a whole group; hysteria = wild, out-of-control fear.

Part 1: Joint Address to Congress Leading to a Declaration of War Against Japan, December 8, 1941 (National Archives). Part 2: Western Defense Command, Civilian Exclusion Order No. 41, Presidio of San Francisco, May 1942 (J. Paul Getty Museum teaching collection). Part 3: Civil Liberties Act of 1988, Public Law 100-383, signed by President Ronald Reagan. Real wording; short parts.

👁 Think Like a Historian

First I check who wrote each source and why. Part 1 is a president asking Congress for war. Part 2 is an order from the Army. Notice its careful word "non-alien." That was a way of saying "citizen" without saying it, which hid the fact that the government was removing its own citizens. Part 3 was written 46 years later by Congress, looking back. Then I compare: Part 2 treated people as a danger because of their ancestry, and Part 3 admits that was wrong. How do we know the truth? By comparing sources from different times.

🎖️ People Who Served and Stood Up

Some fought overseas, some fought in court, and some kept going through great unfairness. Read each card and ask: what did this person face, and how did they respond?

Doris Miller

1919–1943

A Black sailor from Waco, Texas, he was a mess attendant on the USS West Virginia at Pearl Harbor. He helped wounded shipmates and fired an anti-aircraft gun during the attack, and he received the Navy Cross. He died in 1943 when a Japanese submarine sank his ship, the USS Liscome Bay.

Chester Nez

1921–2014

Born in New Mexico in the Navajo Nation, he was punished at a boarding school for speaking Navajo. In 1942 he became one of the first 29 Navajo Code Talkers. He was the last of the original 29 when he died in 2014 at age 93.

Benjamin O. Davis Jr.

1912–2002

He was one of the first group of Black pilots trained at Tuskegee in 1941, and he commanded the 332nd Fighter Group, the Red Tails. He later became the first Black general in the U.S. Air Force.

Fred Korematsu

1919–2005

Born in Oakland, California, he refused the order to leave his home in 1942 and was arrested at age 23. The Supreme Court ruled against him in 1944. In 1983 a judge overturned his conviction, and in 1998 he received the Presidential Medal of Freedom, the nation's highest civilian honor.

Aki Kurose

Born in Seattle, Washington

She was 16 and a U.S. citizen when Pearl Harbor was attacked. Her family was forced to the Minidoka camp in Idaho, where she finished high school. She later became a teacher in Seattle and worked for fair treatment for all people. Her story is told in today's video.
