🌉 Why Bridge Designers Sort Shapes

Nonfiction Connection
📖

Real Talk: The City of Bridges

Pittsburgh sits where the Allegheny River and the Monongahela River meet to form the Ohio River, so the city needs a lot of bridges. It is often called the “City of Bridges” because it has 446 of them. Three bright yellow-gold bridges across the Allegheny, built in the 1920s, are known as the Three Sisters. Today they are named for three famous people with ties to Pittsburgh: baseball star Roberto Clemente, artist Andy Warhol, and scientist and writer Rachel Carson. Many bridges, like the Smithfield Street Bridge over the Monongahela, use a truss, a frame of beams joined in triangles. A triangle keeps its shape when it is pushed, so trusses are strong. Engineers draw every beam and plate as a flat, two-dimensional figure first, and they check the sides, the angles, and the symmetry before anything is built.

🖼️ Picture It: Sorting Triangles Two Ways

👁

Sides First, Then Angles

Every triangle gets two names: one for its sides and one for its angles. Matching red tick marks show sides of equal length, and a small square shows a right angle. A triangle whose sides are all different lengths is a scalene triangle.

Equilateral triangle3 equal sides
Isosceles triangleat least 2 equal sides
Scalene triangleno equal sides
Acute triangle3 acute angles (each less than 90°)
Right triangle1 right angle (exactly 90°)
Obtuse triangle1 obtuse angle (more than 90°)
Two names for one triangle
Example: a corner brace has sides of 3 feet, 3 feet, and about 4 1/4 feet, and one 90° angle. Two sides are equal, so it is isosceles. It has a right angle, so it is right. Its full name is a right isosceles triangle.
Good to know: an isosceles triangle has at least two equal sides, so an equilateral triangle is a special isosceles triangle. All three angles of an equilateral triangle are 60°, so it is always acute.
Look at the biggest angle: a triangle can have only one right angle or one obtuse angle. If one angle is obtuse, it is an obtuse triangle, even though its other two angles are acute.

🌳 The Quadrilateral Family Tree

📐

Ms. Whitaker’s Panel Check

Ms. Whitaker says, “A square is a rectangle, a rhombus, and a parallelogram all at once.” A quadrilateral is any closed figure with four straight sides. The family tree shows how the four-sided shapes are related.

Trapezoidat least 1 pair of parallel sides
Parallelogram2 pairs of parallel sides; opposite sides are equal
Rectanglea parallelogram with 4 right angles
Rhombusa parallelogram with 4 equal sides
Square4 right angles AND 4 equal sides
Kite2 pairs of equal sides that are next to each other
💡

Going Down the Tree

Every shape keeps all the features of the shapes above it. So every square is a rectangle, but not every rectangle is a square. A rhombus is a parallelogram with four equal sides, and it becomes a square only when it also has four right angles. (Some books say a trapezoid has exactly one pair of parallel sides. In KCE Pathways we use “at least one pair,” which is why the parallelogram sits under the trapezoid.)

📏 Parallel and Perpendicular Sides

🏗️

Checking Aunt Winnie’s Panels

In Week 18 you learned that parallel lines never meet and perpendicular lines meet at right angles. Bridge designers look for them inside every shape. Two sides that meet at a right angle are perpendicular sides.

Count the pairs
Rectangle and square: 2 pairs of parallel sides and 4 right angles, so 4 pairs of perpendicular sides (one pair at each corner).
Rhombus or parallelogram with no right angles: 2 pairs of parallel sides, but 0 perpendicular sides.
Right trapezoid: 1 pair of parallel sides and 2 right angles. In a trapezoid, right angles come in twos, because the side that meets one parallel side at 90° meets the other parallel side at 90° too.
Triangles: a triangle never has parallel sides, because any two of its sides meet. A right triangle has exactly 1 pair of perpendicular sides.
Regular hexagon: its 6 equal sides make 3 pairs of parallel sides, each side facing the side across from it.

🪞 Lines of Symmetry

🪞

Mr. Wojcik’s Steel Plates

Many bridges are designed to look the same on both sides of a center line, so the parts often come in matching pairs. A line of symmetry splits a figure into two halves that match exactly when you fold along the line.

Fold it in your mind
Test every possible fold: straight across the middle, straight up and down, and corner to corner. Count only the folds where the halves match exactly.
Squares vs. rectangles: a square has 4 lines of symmetry, but a rectangle that is not a square has only 2. Its diagonals are NOT lines of symmetry.
Triangles: equilateral has 3, isosceles (with only two equal sides) has 1, and scalene has 0.
Slanted shapes: a parallelogram with no right angles and unequal sides has 0 lines of symmetry, but a rhombus has 2, along its diagonals.
Regular shapes: a regular polygon has as many lines of symmetry as it has sides. A regular hexagon has 6.
