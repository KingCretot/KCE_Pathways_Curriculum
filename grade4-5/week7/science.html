💧 The Sun Powers the Water Cycle

Earth’s water moves in a never-ending loop called the water cycle. The Sun heats oceans, lakes and puddles, and liquid water evaporates into water vapor; plants also release water vapor from their leaves. As warm, moist air rises, it cools, and the vapor condenses into tiny droplets that form clouds. When droplets join and grow too heavy, they fall as precipitation. The water then collects in oceans, lakes, rivers and underground, and the cycle starts again.

👁 Watch Me Think

When I see water change, I ask two questions. Is it changing from liquid to gas, or gas to liquid? Is it being heated or cooled? A puddle shrinking on a hot day is liquid turning to gas from heat, so that is evaporation. A bathroom mirror fogging up is gas turning to liquid on a cool surface, so that is condensation.

🧩 Picture It: The Water Cycle Loop

StageWhat happensHeat or cool?
EvaporationLiquid water becomes water vaporHeated by the Sun
CondensationWater vapor becomes tiny droplets (clouds)Cooled as air rises
PrecipitationHeavy drops or ice fall to the groundPulled down by gravity
CollectionWater gathers in oceans, lakes, rivers and groundwaterWaits to be heated again

About 97 percent of Earth’s water is salty ocean water.

🤝 Let's Try Together: Follow One Drop Across Florida

1. The Sun warms the Atlantic Ocean. A water molecule evaporates and rises as vapor. The salt stays behind in the ocean.
2. Over land, the air rises and cools. The vapor condenses into a tall afternoon storm cloud.
3. The droplets grow heavy and fall as rain: precipitation.
4. The rain soaks into the ground or runs into a river and flows toward Lake Okeechobee: collection.
5. Next sunny day, the cycle starts again. Notice that water in the cycle is never used up; it just keeps moving.

🌦️ Weather: Measuring the Air Right Now

Weather is what the air is like at a certain place and time. Meteorologists, scientists who study weather, measure temperature, precipitation, wind speed and direction, humidity (how much water vapor is in the air) and air pressure (how hard the air pushes down). Each one has a tool, such as a thermometer, rain gauge or barometer. Falling air pressure often means clouds and storms are coming, while rising pressure usually brings clearer skies.

👁 Watch Me Think

When I read a weather report, I look for change, not just one number. If humidity is climbing and the air pressure is falling, I predict rain is on the way. Then I check what happens. If it rains, my clues worked. If it doesn’t, I look for what I missed. That is how forecasts get better.

🌎 Weather vs. Climate

Weather is what is happening in the air today or this week, and it can change in hours. Climate is the usual pattern of weather in a place, averaged over many years, usually about 30. A saying helps: climate is what you expect, weather is what you get. Florida’s climate has hot, humid summers with many afternoon thunderstorms and mild, drier winters. The Atlantic hurricane season runs from June 1 to November 30.

👁 Watch Me Think

To sort a statement, I look at the time it covers. “It snowed in Tallahassee yesterday” is one day, so it is weather. “Tallahassee rarely gets snow” describes many years, so it is climate. One unusual cold day does not change a climate, just like one bad grade does not change a whole report card.

🧩 Picture It: Weather or Climate?

Weather (short time)Climate (many years)
A thunderstorm hit Tampa at 3 p.m.Tampa gets most of its rain in summer

A hurricane is approaching the coastFlorida’s hurricane season runs June to November

🤝 Let's Try Together: Does One Cold Week Prove Anything?

Claim: “It was 38°F in Miami last week, so Miami’s climate is getting colder.”
Step 1: How long is the evidence? One week. That is weather.
Step 2: Climate needs many years of data, about 30.
Step 3: To test the claim, we would compare average temperatures over decades, not one week.
Conclusion: the evidence is too short to support the claim.

🌀 Scientists of the Sky

Weather science grew because people collected careful data about the air and shared it. These meteorologists broke barriers and changed how we understand storms and climate.

June Bacon-Bercey

1928–2019

Born in Wichita, Kansas, she earned a degree in meteorology from UCLA in 1954, and she is recognized as the first African American woman to earn a degree in meteorology. She forecast weather for the government, then in the early 1970s became chief meteorologist at a TV station in Buffalo, New York. She was the first woman and first Black person to earn the American Meteorological Society’s seal for TV weathercasting. In 1977 she won $64,000 on a TV quiz show and used it to start a scholarship for women studying weather and climate.

Warren M. Washington

1936–2024

Washington grew up in Portland, Oregon. In 1964 he earned a PhD in meteorology from Penn State, only the second African American to do so. At the National Center for Atmospheric Research in Boulder, Colorado, he helped build some of the first computer models of Earth’s climate. These models use math, physics and many years of data to show how adding carbon dioxide to the air changes Earth’s temperature. He received the National Medal of Science, one of the highest honors for a U.S. scientist.

Tetsuya “Ted” Fujita

1920–1998

Born in Japan, Fujita became a weather scientist at the University of Chicago. He studied tornado damage from the air and on the ground, and in 1971 he created the Fujita Scale, which rates tornadoes by the damage they cause. He also discovered microbursts, sudden blasts of air from storms that can endanger airplanes, which led to safer airports.
