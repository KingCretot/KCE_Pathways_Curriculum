📜 How Slavery Became Law

Early Virginia's workers included English indentured servants and captive Africans, and some of those Africans, like Anthony Johnson, later gained their freedom. As tobacco plantations grew, planters wanted workers they could control for life, and lawmakers, many of them enslavers, began writing laws based on race. In 1662, Virginia passed a law saying a child would be free or enslaved according to the mother's status, so every child born to an enslaved woman was enslaved from birth. In 1667, another law said that being baptized as a Christian did not free an enslaved person, and Virginia's 1705 slave codes treated enslaved people as property. Step by step, lawmakers turned slavery into a lifelong, hereditary, race-based system.

👁 Watch Me Think

English law usually said a child's status followed the father. Virginia's lawmakers changed that rule. Under the 1662 law, every child of an enslaved mother was enslaved, even if the father was a free Englishman. Who benefited? Enslavers, who gained more enslaved workers with every birth. So whenever I read a law, I ask: who wrote it, and whom did it help?

🤝 Let's Try Together: Same Escape, Different Punishment

In 1640, three servants ran away from a Virginia farm: a Dutchman, a Scot, and an African man named John Punch. When they were caught, the court added four more years of service for the two Europeans, but ordered John Punch to serve for the rest of his life. Let's reason: same act, different punishment, and the difference was race. Historians use this court record as early evidence that race-based slavery was forming in law.

🌾 Skills Africans Brought

Enslaved Africans were not only workers; they were experts. In South Carolina, planters grew rich from rice, and many enslaved people came from West Africa's rice-growing regions, such as Senegal, Gambia and Sierra Leone, where farmers had grown rice for centuries. Many historians argue that their knowledge of planting, flooding fields, and cleaning rice with fanner baskets helped make rice the colony's most valuable crop. Africans also worked as blacksmiths, carpenters, brickmakers and boat pilots, and enslaved builders helped construct colonial homes, churches and roads. By the early 1700s, more than half the people in the South Carolina colony were Africans.

👁 Watch Me Think

Who got the credit for South Carolina's rice wealth? Old histories praised the planters. But many planters came from England and the island of Barbados, where rice was not grown. Many of the people who knew how to grow it came from West Africa. So I ask three questions: who had the knowledge, who did the work, and who kept the money? Enslaved Africans built much of the colony's wealth, but the profits went to enslavers.

🤝 Let's Try Together: Follow the Fanner Basket

A fanner basket is a wide, flat, coiled basket. After rice was pounded, workers tossed it into the air from the basket so the wind carried away the light husks. Very similar coiled baskets are made in Senegal and Sierra Leone. Today, Gullah Geechee artists near Mount Pleasant, South Carolina, still sew sweetgrass baskets in this style. Let's reason: one kind of object carried knowledge across the ocean and through many generations.

✊🏾 Resistance, the Gullah Geechee and Stono

Enslaved people resisted every day. Some worked slowly, broke tools or pretended to be sick; others escaped, and some reached Spanish Florida, where Spain promised freedom to people who fled the English colonies. Many kept their cultures alive through names, music, stories and faith. Along the coast and Sea Islands from North Carolina to northeast Florida, descendants of enslaved Africans, the Gullah Geechee people, created their own language, Gullah, which blends English with words and grammar from African languages. In September 1739, about 20 enslaved men near the Stono River in South Carolina rose up and marched south toward Florida, gathering followers; colonial militia stopped them that same day, and many people on both sides were killed.

👁 Watch Me Think

Escapes and rebellions are easy to find in records. Quiet resistance, like working slowly or keeping a language, is harder to find, because enslavers wrote most of the records. So historians look for other evidence: words like goober, meaning peanut, which comes from the Kikongo word nguba; songs; Br'er Rabbit stories, which have roots in African trickster tales; and baskets. Culture that survived is evidence of resistance.

🧩 Picture It: The Road South, 1739

Picture the Atlantic coast. Near the top is Charleston, South Carolina. About 20 miles southwest is the Stono River, where the uprising began. Below it lie the young colony of Georgia and its Sea Islands. Farther south is Spanish Florida, with St. Augustine on the coast. Just north of St. Augustine stands Fort Mose, founded in 1738: the first legally recognized free Black town in what is now the United States.

🤝 Let's Try Together: Why March South?

In 1738, Spanish Florida founded Gracia Real de Santa Teresa de Mose, called Fort Mose, just north of St. Augustine. It was a town of free Black people who had escaped from the English colonies. Let's reason: the Stono marchers headed south in 1739, the very next year, so the evidence suggests they knew freedom might be possible in Florida. After Stono, South Carolina passed harsh new laws in 1740 that banned enslaved people from gathering in groups, earning money, or learning to write.

🔍 Source Detective: Phillis Wheatley's Letter

In February 1774, the poet Phillis Wheatley, freed the year before, wrote to Samson Occom, a Mohegan minister who had spoken out against Christians who held people in slavery. Her letter was soon printed in newspapers. Read her words, then decide what she wanted readers to understand.

Primary Source: Phillis Wheatley's letter to Samson Occom, February 11, 1774

"…in every human Breast, God has implanted a Principle, which we call Love of Freedom; it is impatient of Oppression, and pants for Deliverance; and by the Leave of our modern Egyptians I will assert, that the same Principle lives in us."

"How well the Cry for Liberty, and the reverse Disposition for the exercise of oppressive Power over others agree, … I humbly think it does not require the Penetration of a Philosopher to determine."

Word help: Breast = heart; Principle = a deep belief; Oppression = cruel, unfair control; pants for Deliverance = longs to be set free; modern Egyptians = enslavers (in the Bible story of Exodus, Egypt's rulers enslaved the Israelites); reverse Disposition = the opposite wish; Penetration of a Philosopher = a great thinker's wisdom.

Phillis Wheatley, letter to the Reverend Samson Occom, February 11, 1774, printed in the Connecticut Gazette, March 11, 1774. Shortened; original capital letters kept.

👁 Think Like a Historian

First, who and when: Phillis Wheatley, a young African poet in Boston, writing in 1774, when colonists were loudly demanding liberty from Britain. Second, audience: she wrote to Samson Occom, but the letter was printed in newspapers, so many colonists read it. Third, purpose: she points out that people crying for liberty were holding others in slavery. She makes the point politely, using a Bible story her readers knew, which made her argument hard to brush aside.

👥 Voices of Freedom

These people lived through slavery in the colonies and found ways to speak, write, sue, save and fight for freedom. As you read, ask: how do we know their stories?

Phillis Wheatley

About 1753–1784

Kidnapped from West Africa at about age 7 and sold in Boston in 1761 to the Wheatley family, who named her after the ship that carried her, the Phillis. She learned English and Latin, and in 1773 her book Poems on Various Subjects, Religious and Moral was published in London, the first book of poetry by an African American. Eighteen Boston men signed a statement in it saying she really wrote the poems. She was freed in 1773 and died in 1784, at about 31.

Elizabeth Freeman (Mum Bett)

About 1744–1829

Enslaved in Sheffield, Massachusetts. In 1780 she heard the new Massachusetts constitution, which said "All men are born free and equal." She asked lawyer Theodore Sedgwick to help her sue for freedom, and in 1781 a jury ruled that she and a man named Brom were free. She chose the name Elizabeth Freeman. Her case helped lead to the end of slavery in Massachusetts.

Lucy Terry Prince

About 1730–1821

Taken from Africa as a small child and enslaved in Deerfield, Massachusetts. In 1746, at about 16, she composed "Bars Fight," a poem about an attack on her town during a war between the English and the French and their Indigenous allies. People passed it down by memory until it was printed in 1855. It is the oldest known poem by an African American. In 1756 she married Abijah Prince, a free Black man, who bought her freedom. She became known for speaking up for her family's rights.

Venture Smith

About 1729–1805

Born Broteer Furro in West Africa, he was kidnapped as a young child and carried to Rhode Island. He was enslaved in New York and Connecticut. By fishing, cutting wood and doing extra work for years, he saved enough money to buy his own freedom in 1765. Then he bought freedom for his sons and for his wife, Meg. In 1798 he told his life story to a writer, and it was printed as a book, so we can read his experience in his own words.
