🌱 Plant Structures: A Team of Parts

A plant's structures work together like a team. Roots anchor the plant and absorb water and minerals from the soil, and some roots, like carrots, store food. The stem holds the plant up and works like a bundle of pipes: tiny tubes carry water up from the roots and carry food from the leaves to the rest of the plant. Leaves are the plant's food factories. Flowers make seeds: pollen must move from one flower part to another, often carried by bees, birds, bats or the wind, and then the flower can form a fruit that protects the seeds and helps spread them.

👁 Watch Me Think

When I see a strange plant part, I ask: what job is it doing? A potato grows underground, so I might call it a root. But a potato has eyes that sprout new stems and leaves, and roots do not do that. So scientists classify a potato as an underground stem that stores food. I use evidence about what a part does and what grows from it, not just where it is.

☀️ Photosynthesis: How Plants Make Food

Plants do not eat food from the soil; they make their own. In photosynthesis, leaves use energy from light to turn water (brought up from the roots) and carbon dioxide (a gas from the air) into sugar, the plant's food. Oxygen is released into the air. A green pigment called chlorophyll captures the light energy, which is why most leaves are green. Carbon dioxide gets into the leaf through tiny openings called stomata.

👁 Watch Me Think

In the 1600s, a scientist named Jan Baptist van Helmont wondered where a tree's mass comes from. He planted a 5-pound willow in a pot holding 200 pounds of dried soil and gave it only water for five years. At the end the tree weighed about 169 pounds, but the soil had lost only about 2 ounces! So the tree's mass did not come from the soil. Van Helmont guessed it all came from water. Later scientists discovered that much of a plant's mass comes from carbon dioxide in the air, built into sugar by photosynthesis.

🤝 Let's Try Together: Trace a Sunflower's Day

Let's trace it. The sunflower's roots take in water, and the stem carries it up. Its leaves take in carbon dioxide through the stomata. Chlorophyll captures sunlight. The leaf makes sugar, which travels through the stem to feed the roots, the stem and the growing seeds. Oxygen goes out into the air, where animals breathe it in. This connects to Week 2: plants are producers because of photosynthesis.

🌽 Plant Science in the Field: The Three Sisters and Crop Rotation

For many centuries, Indigenous farmers across North America, including the Haudenosaunee of what is now New York and the Cherokee of the Southeast, planted the Three Sisters together: corn, beans and squash. Corn stalks give bean vines a pole to climb. Beans, with help from bacteria living on their roots, add nitrogen, a nutrient plants need, to the soil. Squash spreads big leaves that shade the ground, keeping moisture in and crowding out weeds. Much later, George Washington Carver, a scientist at Tuskegee Institute in Alabama, taught Southern farmers to restore soil worn out by years of cotton by rotating in peanuts and cowpeas, which also add nitrogen.

👁 Watch Me Think

Is the Three Sisters system only a tradition, or is there scientific evidence? I look at each plant's structures. Corn has a tall, strong stem. Beans have climbing vines and roots that work with nitrogen-making bacteria. Squash has broad leaves and spreading vines. Each structure solves a problem for another plant. Farmers figured this out by observing their fields carefully over many generations. That is Indigenous agricultural science.

🤝 Let's Try Together: Carver's Crop Rotation

Let's follow one field. Year 1, the farmer grows cotton, and the soil loses nitrogen. Year 2, the farmer plants peanuts, and bacteria on the peanut roots add nitrogen back. Year 3, cotton grows better again. Now predict: what if the farmer planted cotton every single year? The soil would keep losing nutrients, and the harvests would likely shrink. That is the problem Carver saw across the South.

🔬 Data Lab: Does Light Affect Bean Plant Growth?

A class planted 12 bean seeds of the same type in the same size pots and soil. Each plant got 50 mL of water every 2 days and stayed in the same warm room. The only difference was how many hours of light each group of 3 plants got each day. After 14 days the class measured each plant and recorded the averages.

👁 Think Like a Scientist

A scientist first names the variables: what was changed, what was measured, and what was kept the same. Then she looks at ALL the measurements, not just one. If one result looks surprising, she asks what could explain it before deciding what the data shows.

🧑‍🌾 People Who Grew Plant Science

Plant science comes from many people: farmers who observed fields for generations, and scientists who studied soil and collected plants around the world.

George Washington Carver

about 1864–1943

Born enslaved in Diamond, Missouri, near the end of the Civil War, Carver became the first Black student and then the first Black faculty member at Iowa State. In 1896 Booker T. Washington invited him to Tuskegee Institute in Alabama. There he taught farmers to rotate crops, wrote simple guides for farm families and promoted hundreds of uses for peanuts and sweet potatoes, and built a movable school, a wagon that brought lessons to farmers who could not travel to class.

Haudenosaunee Farmers

for many centuries

The six nations of the Haudenosaunee Confederacy (Mohawk, Oneida, Onondaga, Cayuga, Seneca and Tuscarora) grew corn, beans and squash together in small mounds of soil. Women led the farming in Haudenosaunee communities. The Three Sisters are honored in their stories and ceremonies, and the system fed large towns for generations.

Ynés Mexía

1870–1938

A Mexican American botanist (a scientist who studies plants), Mexía began her science career in her 50s. She traveled through Mexico and South America, and even to Alaska, collecting plants. She gathered well over 100,000 plant specimens for museums and universities, and hundreds of the plants she found were species new to science. Some plants are named in her honor.
