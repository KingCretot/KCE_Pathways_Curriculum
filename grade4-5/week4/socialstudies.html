⛵ 1492: Columbus Arrives in the Taíno World

The Taíno lived on Caribbean islands such as Hispaniola, Cuba, Puerto Rico and Jamaica, in villages led by leaders called caciques (kah-SEE-kays). In October 1492, Christopher Columbus, a sailor from Genoa working for the king and queen of Spain, arrived on an island in the Bahamas. He believed he had reached Asia, so he called the people "Indios." Columbus forced Taíno people to search for gold, and he sent hundreds of them to Spain as enslaved people. Forced labor, violence and new diseases caused a huge loss of life, yet Taíno descendants and culture survive today.

👁 Watch Me Think

I want to keep two true facts together. Fact one: Columbus was a skilled sailor who crossed the Atlantic and came back. Fact two: he enslaved Taíno people and ruled so harshly that Spain removed him as governor in 1500 and sent him home in chains. A historian does not pick only the fact they like. I hold both, and I ask: whose story gets told on holidays, and whose gets left out?

🧩 Picture It: The Caribbean in 1492

Picture the warm Caribbean Sea. Florida is at the top. Below it stretches the chain of the Bahamas, then the large islands: Cuba, Hispaniola (today Haiti and the Dominican Republic), Jamaica and Puerto Rico. Taíno villages dot the coasts, linked by large dugout canoes that could carry many people. Columbus's three ships, the Niña, the Pinta and the Santa María, left Spain in August 1492 and reached the Bahamas on October 12.

🤝 Let's Try Together: Taíno Words We Still Use

English still uses words that came from the Taíno language: hammock, canoe and hurricane. Let's reason: what does that tell us? It shows Europeans learned from the Taíno, and that Taíno culture lasted. Today, many people in Puerto Rico, Cuba and the Dominican Republic have Taíno ancestors and keep Taíno traditions alive.

🌽 The Columbian Exchange

After 1492, ships carried plants, animals, people and diseases across the Atlantic in both directions. Historians call this the Columbian Exchange. From the Americas came corn, potatoes, tomatoes, peanuts, chili peppers and cacao (chocolate), which changed diets around the world. From Europe, Africa and Asia came horses, cattle, pigs, wheat, sugarcane and coffee. Diseases like smallpox and measles came too, and because Indigenous people had no immunity, millions died. Europeans also forced millions of enslaved Africans across the ocean to work on plantations.

👁 Watch Me Think

The word exchange sounds fair, like trading cards with a friend. So I check: did both sides benefit equally? Europe gained new foods that helped its population grow. Indigenous nations gained horses but lost millions of people to disease, and enslaved Africans were taken by force. So exchange describes the movement, but not fairness. A good historian asks who benefited and who paid the cost.

🏰 Conquest, Resistance and St. Augustine

Spanish soldiers called conquistadors took over huge Indigenous empires. Hernán Cortés and thousands of Indigenous allies captured Tenochtitlan in 1521, and Francisco Pizarro attacked the Inca Empire in 1532. Indigenous people resisted in many ways: Taíno leaders like Hatuey and Enriquillo fought back, and the Calusa of Florida drove off Juan Ponce de León in 1521. In 1565, Pedro Menéndez de Avilés founded St. Augustine, Florida, on land where the Timucua people lived, and Africans, both free and enslaved, were among its first settlers. St. Augustine is the oldest continuously inhabited city founded by Europeans in the continental United States.

👁 Watch Me Think

How could a few hundred Spanish soldiers defeat empires of millions? I list the causes: steel weapons and horses; thousands of Indigenous allies who wanted to escape Mexica rule; and smallpox, which had already killed many people. One cause alone does not explain it. Historians look for several causes working together.

Which cause would Spanish writers talk about most? Their own weapons and courage. Which causes are often left out? The allies and the disease. Asking whose story is told changes what we think happened.

🔍 Source Detective: Columbus's Journal

Columbus kept a journal on his first voyage. Here are his words about the Taíno people he met in the Bahamas in October 1492. Read carefully, then decide what they tell us about his plans.

Primary Source (English translation): Christopher Columbus's journal, October 1492

October 12: "They should be good servants and of quick intelligence, since I see that they very soon say all that is said to them, and I believe that they would easily be made Christians."

October 14: "With fifty men they can all be subjugated and made to do what is required of them."

Word help: servants = people who work for others; subjugated = brought under control by force.

Journal of the First Voyage of Christopher Columbus, as copied and summarized by Bartolomé de las Casas. English translation as published in the Internet Medieval Sourcebook (Fordham University); shortened. Wording differs slightly between translations.

👁 Think Like a Historian

First, I check how we have this source. Columbus's original journal is lost. What survives is a copy and summary made decades later by Bartolomé de las Casas, who quoted parts of it word for word. So these are probably Columbus's words, passed through another writer. Next, I ask about purpose: Columbus was writing for the king and queen who paid for his trip, and he wanted to show them the voyage would pay off.

👥 People of the Encounter

These people lived through the meeting of worlds. Look at each one honestly: what did they do, and whom did it help or hurt?

Christopher Columbus

1451–1506

A skilled sailor from Genoa who made four voyages across the Atlantic for Spain. He also enslaved Taíno people and ruled Hispaniola so harshly that Spain removed him as governor in 1500. He believed he had reached Asia.

Hatuey

Died 1512

A Taíno cacique from Hispaniola who crossed to Cuba to warn people about the Spanish. He led resistance there until Spanish soldiers captured and executed him. Today he is honored in Cuba as a hero of resistance.

Enriquillo

About 1495–1535

A Taíno cacique on Hispaniola who led resistance from the mountains from 1519 to 1533. In the end, Spain signed a peace agreement with him, one of the few times it did.

Bartolomé de las Casas

1484–1566

A Spanish priest who at first held Taíno workers himself, then changed his mind and spent about 50 years speaking out against cruelty to Indigenous people. Early on, he suggested using enslaved Africans instead. Later he wrote that he deeply regretted it, because their enslavement was just as wrong.

Estevanico (Esteban)

About 1500–1539

An enslaved man from Azemmour, Morocco, who landed in Florida in 1528 with a Spanish expedition. He was one of only four survivors who crossed Texas and the Southwest to Mexico City with Cabeza de Vaca, learning Indigenous languages and serving as a guide and translator.
