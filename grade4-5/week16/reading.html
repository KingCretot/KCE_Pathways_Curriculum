🖼️ Picture It: How Words Are Built

Many English words are built from parts that came from Greek and Latin, two very old languages. The root is the main part that carries the meaning. A prefix is added to the front, and a suffix is added to the end. Prefixes and suffixes together are called affixes. If you know the parts, you can figure out the whole word, like solving a puzzle.

💡

The Prefix That Changes Its Spelling

The prefix in- (meaning “not”) changes to match the next letter: im- before b, m, or p (impossible, imbalance), il- before l (illegal), and ir- before r (irregular). That is why some of these words have double letters. Careful: in- can also mean “into,” as in inspect (to look into something).

meteorologist
meteor (high in the air) + -ologist (person who studies): a weather scientist. “For thirty years she had worked as a meteorologist.”
thermometer
therm (heat) + meter (measure): a tool that measures heat. “This is a thermometer.”
predict
pre- (before) + dict (say): to say what will happen before it happens. “We need to predict how strong it will be.”
impassable
im- (not) + pass + -able (can be): cannot be traveled through. “The coast road may be impassable.”
inaudible
in- (not) + aud (hear) + -ible (can be): not able to be heard. “His reply was inaudible through the crackling static.”
visible
vis (see) + -ible (can be): able to be seen. “The lighthouse beam was barely visible.”

📖 The Story: The Storm Watchers

Read the story carefully. The bold words are today’s word-study words. Notice the paragraph numbers; you will use them.

1Every morning before school, Desmond and his cousin Noor climbed the hill to the old lighthouse keeper’s house in Harbor Ridge, a small fishing village on the coast of Maine. Inside, Ms. Adaeze Nwosu ran the village weather station. For thirty years she had worked as a meteorologist, a scientist who studies the air and predicts the weather.

2“Weather words are puzzles,” Ms. Nwosu told them on their first day. She tapped a glass tube on the wall. “This is a thermometer. Therm means heat, and meter means measure. Put the pieces together, and the word tells you its job.” Beside it hung a barometer, a tool that measures air pressure, or the weight of the air pressing down on us.

3On Tuesday morning, the barometer began to drop. Ms. Nwosu studied the numbers and frowned. “Falling pressure often means a storm is coming,” she said. “We need to predict how strong it will be.” Noor smiled, because she knew that pre means before and dict means say. To predict is to say what will happen before it happens.

4By afternoon, the sky that had been clear and bright turned murky. The harbor, usually so tranquil, was now wild and churning, with whitecaps slapping against the docks. Desmond watched the cups of the anemometer spin faster and faster on the roof.

5Ms. Nwosu picked up the radio and called the fishing boats. “Bring your boats in early,” she warned. “By tonight, the coast road may be impassable. No car or truck will be able to get through.” Captain Halvorsen answered, but his reply was inaudible through the crackling static, so Ms. Nwosu asked him to repeat it. This time they heard him clearly: “Heading in now!”

6That night, the storm was ferocious. Wind howled, rain pounded the windows, and waves crashed over the seawall. Through the sheets of rain, the lighthouse beam was barely visible. Yet the next morning, every boat was safe in the harbor, and the damage was minimal: only a few branches down and one broken fence. Desmond wrote in the weather log, “Our prediction was right. Clues made the storm predictable.” Under his line, Noor added, “Words have clues, too.”

Informational Text • Station GuideHarbor Ridge Weather Station: Tools of the Trade

by Ms. Adaeze Nwosu

Thermometertherm (heat) + meter (measure). It measures how hot or cold the air is, in degrees.
Barometerbaro (weight) + meter (measure). It measures air pressure. Falling pressure often signals stormy weather. Rising pressure usually means fair skies.
Anemometeranemo (wind) + meter (measure). Its cups catch the wind and spin. The faster they spin, the stronger the wind.
Hygrometerhygro (wet) + meter (measure). It measures humidity, the amount of water vapor in the air. On muggy summer days, the reading is high.
Rain GaugeA gauge is a tool for measuring. The rain gauge collects rain so we can measure how many inches fell. Tuesday’s storm: 2.4 inches.
Satellite Photographsphoto (light) + graph (record). Satellites far above Earth record pictures of clouds so meteorologists can track storms.

🔦 Context Clues and Spelling Rules

🧭

Five Kinds of Context Clues

When word parts are not enough, look at the words and sentences around the new word. That is its context. Here are five kinds of clues, all from today’s story.

ClueHow it worksFrom the story
DefinitionThe writer tells you the meaning, often after a comma.a meteorologist, a scientist who studies the air
Synonym or restatementA word with the same meaning, often after “or.”air pressure, or the weight of the air
Antonym or contrastA word with the opposite meaning, often near “but,” “usually,” or “now.”usually so tranquil, was now wild and churning
ExampleExamples show what the word looks like.the storm was ferocious. Wind howled, rain pounded...
InferenceYou figure it out from what happens.his reply was inaudible... so she asked him to repeat it
🔤

Spelling Rules for Adding Affixes

  1. A prefix never changes the base word. mis + spell = misspell; un + necessary = unnecessary. That is where double letters come from.
  2. Drop a silent e before a suffix that starts with a vowel. believe + able = believable; use + able = usable.
  3. Keep the silent e before a suffix that starts with a consonant. hope + ful = hopeful; agree + ment = agreement.
  4. Change y to i when a consonant comes before the y. rely + able = reliable; happy + ly = happily.
  5. Double the last consonant when the word ends in one vowel + one consonant, the last part is stressed, and the suffix starts with a vowel. stop + ed = stopped; forget + able = forgettable.
🎯

-able or -ible?

Use -able most of the time when the part before it is a whole word you already know: predictable, passable, breakable. Use -ible most of the time when the part before it is a root that cannot stand alone: visible, audible, edible. This works for most words, and a dictionary can settle the rest.
