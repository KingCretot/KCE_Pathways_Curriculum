🌊 Waves: Energy on the Move

A wave is a disturbance that carries energy from one place to another. Drop a pebble in a pond and rings of ripples spread out. Sound travels through the air as a wave, and light is a wave too. Every wave has parts. The crest is the highest point and the trough is the lowest point. The amplitude is how far the wave rises from its middle rest line up to the crest, and the wavelength is the distance from one crest to the next. A wave with a bigger amplitude carries more energy: a tall ocean wave can knock you over, and a loud sound has a bigger amplitude than a soft one. Here is the surprising part: a wave carries energy, not matter. Ocean scientists at NOAA explain that the water in a wave mostly moves in small circles and does not travel far. Sound and water waves need matter to travel through, but light can cross empty space, which is how sunlight reaches Earth.

👁 Watch Me Think

How do we know a wave moves energy and not water? I picture a toy boat floating in a pool. A wave rolls past. If the wave carried the water along, the boat would ride all the way to the other side. But when I watch, the boat rises on the crest, dips into the trough, and ends up close to where it started. The energy moved across the pool, while the water and the boat mostly stayed put. That is my evidence. I can test it with a jump rope too: shake one end, and a bump travels to the far end, but each bit of rope only moves up and down.

🤝 Let's Try Together: Measure a Wave

Let's read a wave drawn on graph paper. The rest line is in the middle. The crest reaches 3 squares above the rest line, and the trough drops 3 squares below it. Amplitude is measured from the rest line, so the amplitude is 3 squares, not 6. Next, one crest sits at square 2 and the next crest sits at square 10. The wavelength is 10 minus 2, which is 8 squares. Watch for two common mistakes: measuring amplitude from the trough all the way to the crest, and measuring wavelength from a crest to a trough. Remember: rest line to crest for amplitude, and crest to crest for wavelength.

👀 Light: How We See

Light travels in straight lines from a light source, such as the Sun, a lamp or a flashlight. Most things you see, like a book, a tree or a friend's face, do not make their own light. You see them because light reflects, or bounces, off them and travels into your eyes. The lens in your eye focuses that light onto the back of the eye, and nerves carry signals to your brain, which makes sense of what you see. In a completely dark room you cannot see anything, because no light is reflecting into your eyes. Color works the same way. White sunlight is really a mix of colors. A red apple reflects mostly red light and absorbs most of the other colors, so red light is what reaches your eyes. Of the colors we can see, red light has the longest wavelength and violet light has the shortest. Smooth, shiny surfaces like mirrors reflect light in a neat, even way, which is why you can see your reflection in a mirror but not in a sheet of paper.

👁 Watch Me Think

How do we know light goes INTO our eyes? Some famous Greek thinkers, such as Euclid and Ptolemy, taught that the eyes send out rays that touch objects. About 1,000 years ago, a scientist named Ibn al-Haytham tested ideas about light with experiments. He showed that light travels in straight lines, and he argued that we see when light from an object enters the eye. I can check the reasoning myself: if my eyes sent out their own rays, I should see just as well in a pitch-dark room. But I don't. The evidence points to light coming in, not going out.

🥤 Bending Light: Refraction

Light does not always go straight through things. When light passes at an angle from one material into another, like from air into water or glass, it changes direction. This bending is called refraction. It happens because light travels at different speeds in different materials: it slows down when it goes from air into water. Refraction is why a straw in a glass of water looks broken where it meets the water, and why a swimming pool looks shallower than it really is. People put refraction to work. The curved glass or plastic lenses in eyeglasses, magnifying glasses and cameras bend light to make things look clearer or bigger. Raindrops refract sunlight too, spreading it into its colors, which helps make a rainbow. Remember the difference: reflection bounces light back, and refraction bends light as it passes into a new material.

👁 Watch Me Think

Is the straw in my glass really broken? I pull it out of the water, and it is perfectly straight. So the straw did not change; the path of the light did. Light from the underwater part of the straw bends as it leaves the water and enters the air on its way to my eyes. My brain assumes light always travels in straight lines, so it places the underwater part of the straw in the wrong spot. When something looks strange, I ask: did the object change, or did the light's path change?

📡 Patterns That Send Messages

People have always used patterns to send messages over distances, like drumbeats or flashes of light. In the 1830s and 1840s, Samuel Morse and his partner Alfred Vail worked on the electric telegraph and a code of short and long signals, called dots and dashes. On May 24, 1844, Morse sent the message "What hath God wrought?" by wire from the U.S. Capitol in Washington, D.C., to Vail in Baltimore. Today, phones and computers turn words, pictures and sounds into digital signals: patterns made of only two signals, like on and off, or 1 and 0. A video call is a very long string of 1s and 0s. Some of those signals travel through the air as radio waves, which are invisible relatives of light. Others travel as flashes of light through optical fibers, glass threads thinner than a hair. The light stays inside the fiber by reflecting off its inside walls again and again, so it can carry huge amounts of information over long distances.

👁 Watch Me Think

Why use only two signals? I imagine sending a message with a flashlight across a noisy, foggy field. If I tried to use ten different brightness levels, my friend might mix up level 6 and level 7. But on and off are easy to tell apart, even when the signal gets weaker. That is why Morse code uses just dots and dashes, and why computers use just 1s and 0s. A simple pattern is hard to misread, and a long string of simple pieces can still carry a whole song or photo.

🔦 Mirror Lab: Testing the Law of Reflection

Scientists draw an imaginary line straight out from a mirror, called the normal. The law of reflection says the angle of the incoming light, measured from the normal, equals the angle of the reflected light. A class tested it. They shone a narrow flashlight beam at a flat mirror standing on paper, traced the beams, and measured both angles from the normal with a protractor. They did five trials.

👁 Think Like a Physicist

A physicist does not expect perfect numbers. A beam of light is a little wide, and protractor lines are thin, so small measuring errors happen. Instead of judging one trial, she looks at the pattern across all the trials: are the two angles always close? If one trial were far off, like 30 and 50, she would check her setup and repeat that trial before deciding anything.

💡 Scientists and Inventors of Light and Signals

Over 1,000 years, these four people helped us understand light and use waves to send information. Each one asked questions and tested ideas with evidence.

Ibn al-Haytham

about 965–about 1040

Ibn al-Haytham was born around the year 965 in Basra, in what is now Iraq, and did most of his work in Cairo, Egypt. Between about 1011 and 1021 he wrote the Book of Optics, seven volumes about light and vision. He used experiments to show that light travels in straight lines, studied how mirrors reflect light and how light bends, and argued that we see when light from an object enters the eye. This disagreed with famous Greek thinkers who taught that the eyes send out rays. In 2015, UNESCO, part of the United Nations, celebrated the International Year of Light and honored 1,000 years since his work on optics.

Charles Kao

1933–2018

Charles Kao was born in Shanghai, China, in 1933, and later worked as an engineer at a laboratory in England. In 1966 he worked out why glass fibers lost so much light: tiny impurities in the glass. He showed that with very pure glass, light signals could travel more than 100 kilometers (over 60 miles) through a fiber, instead of only about 20 meters. Engineers then built those fibers. Today optical fibers carry phone calls, videos and internet data around the world. In 2009 he won the Nobel Prize in Physics, and he is often called the father of fiber optics.

James West

born 1931

James West was born in 1931 in Prince Edward County, Virginia, and joined Bell Labs, a famous research laboratory, in 1957. In 1962 he and Gerhard Sessler invented the electret microphone, a small, low-cost microphone that turns sound waves into electrical signals. The National Inventors Hall of Fame reports that the design is used in about 90 percent of the roughly 2 billion microphones made each year, in things like phones and hearing aids. He joined the Hall of Fame in 1999 and became a professor of engineering at Johns Hopkins University.

Hedy Lamarr

1914–2000

Hedy Lamarr was born in Vienna, Austria, in 1914 and became a famous Hollywood movie actress. She was also an inventor. During World War II, she and composer George Antheil designed a way for a radio signal to hop quickly between different radio channels, called frequencies, so an enemy could not easily jam, or block, the signal guiding a torpedo. They received a patent in 1942. The U.S. Navy did not use it during the war, but similar frequency-hopping ideas are used today in technology such as Bluetooth. In 2014 she was added to the National Inventors Hall of Fame.
