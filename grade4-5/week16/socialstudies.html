🚂 Railroads, Steel and Andrew Carnegie

After 1869, railroads spread across the whole country. In 1870 the United States had about 53,000 miles of railroad track, and by 1900 it had about 193,000 miles. Trains needed steel for rails and bridges, and factories needed trains to ship their goods, so railroads and industry grew together. One of the richest steelmakers was Andrew Carnegie. His family came from Dunfermline, Scotland, in 1848, when he was 12, and at 13 his first job was as a bobbin boy in a cotton mill for $1.20 a week. By the 1890s his company, Carnegie Steel, ran giant mills near Pittsburgh, Pennsylvania. Railroads also drew inventors: Granville T. Woods, a Black engineer from Columbus, Ohio, whom you may remember from Science, patented an induction telegraph in 1887 that let moving trains send messages to stations.

👁 Watch Me Think

I do not stop at "who got rich." I ask who did the work and what it cost them. Carnegie gave away about $350 million in his lifetime and paid for 2,509 free public libraries, 1,679 of them in the United States. That was a real gift. But thousands of workers made his steel, and on the next slide you will see what happened when they formed a union. So I collect both sides of his story before I decide what I think.

✍️ Your Turn, Part 2: Railroads and Steel

🤝 Let's Try Together: The Homestead Strike, 1892

In 1892, at Carnegie's steel mill in Homestead, Pennsylvania, the manager, Henry Clay Frick, refused to bargain with the workers' union and locked about 3,800 men out of the mill. Carnegie was away in Scotland, but he had approved Frick's plan behind the scenes. On July 6, about 300 guards from the Pinkerton Detective Agency arrived by boat, and a long battle broke out. People on both sides were killed. The governor sent 8,500 state troops, and in November the union gave up. Afterward the company cut wages and made 12-hour workdays the rule. Let's weigh it: the same man who paid for libraries also backed breaking his workers' union. A historian keeps both facts in the story.

🗽 New Americans: Ellis Island and Angel Island

Millions of people moved to the United States in these years to find work and safety. On January 1, 1892, an immigration station opened on Ellis Island in New York Harbor. The first person processed there was Annie Moore, a 17-year-old from Ireland who crossed the ocean with her two younger brothers. Over the next 62 years, more than 12 million immigrants passed through Ellis Island, most of them from Europe. On the West Coast, the Angel Island Immigration Station opened on January 21, 1910, in San Francisco Bay. People from more than 80 countries came through it, but it was built mainly to enforce the Chinese Exclusion Act of 1882, a law that kept out most Chinese workers. In the Southwest and Midwest, Mexican and Mexican American workers called traqueros built and repaired railroad track; one historian found they made up almost two-thirds of track workers in those regions. After the Mexican Revolution began in 1910, more families crossed the border looking for work.

👁 Watch Me Think

I compare the two stations. Both took in newcomers, but the rules were not the same for everyone. At Ellis Island, most people were allowed in. At Angel Island, a law aimed at one group, the Chinese, meant long questioning and waiting. So when someone calls the United States "a nation of immigrants," I ask: whose story is being told, and who is missing?

✍️ Your Turn, Part 2: Ellis Island and Angel Island

🤝 Let's Try Together: Reading the Walls

At Angel Island, some Chinese detainees carved poems in Chinese into the wooden walls of the barracks. Most of the poets were Cantonese-speaking men from villages in Guangdong province, the same province many railroad workers came from. Most poems are unsigned and undated. In 1970, a park ranger named Alexander Weiss noticed them, and many can still be read today. Let's reason: an official record tells us what the government wrote down. A poem on the wall tells us what a detainee felt, in his own words, at the time. Historians use both, and they ask whose voice each source carries.

🏭 Children at Work, and Workers Organize

In 1900, the census counted about 1,750,000 children ages 10 to 15 with jobs, about 18 out of every 100 children that age. Most worked on farms, but many worked in cotton mills, coal mines, canneries and glass factories, or sold newspapers on city streets. The National Child Labor Committee, founded in 1904, hired a photographer named Lewis Hine in 1908. Hine traveled the country photographing working children and writing down facts about them; the Library of Congress now holds more than 5,100 of the committee's photographs. Adults organized too. Mary Harris Jones, called Mother Jones, an immigrant from Ireland, organized coal miners. In 1903 she led a march of child mill workers and adults from Philadelphia toward President Theodore Roosevelt's summer home in Oyster Bay, New York. Roosevelt refused to see them, but the march drew attention to child labor.

👁 Watch Me Think

I read the census table carefully. The share of children working stayed near 18 percent from 1890 to 1910, then fell to 8.5 percent in 1920. But I check how the numbers were collected. The 1910 census was taken in a busy farm season, and the 1920 census was taken in winter, so some farm work in 1920 was not counted. Historians say the drop in factory work was real, but the whole drop may look bigger than it really was.

✍️ Your Turn, Part 2: Kids at Work and Unions

🤝 Let's Try Together: The Uprising of the 20,000 and the Triangle Fire

On November 22, 1909, at a packed meeting at Cooper Union in New York City, 23-year-old Clara Lemlich, an immigrant from Ukraine, stood up and called in Yiddish for a general strike. Starting the next day, more than 20,000 shirtwaist makers walked out, most of them young Jewish immigrant women, along with some Italian women. When the strike ended in February 1910, most shops had signed contracts with a shorter 52-hour work week, but the Triangle Waist Company refused. On March 25, 1911, a fire broke out at the Triangle factory. Some doors were kept locked, and 146 workers died, most of them young immigrant women. New York then created a Factory Investigating Commission, and dozens of new laws for safer workplaces followed. Let's trace the chain of cause and effect: unsafe work led to a strike, the strike won only part of what workers wanted, a shop that refused to change had a disaster, and the disaster led to new laws.

🔍 Source Detective: What Lewis Hine Wrote Down

Lewis Hine did not only take pictures. For each photo he wrote a caption with facts he gathered: names, sizes, places and dates. Read three of his real captions from a mill, a coal mine and a city street. (With a parent, you can see the photos on the Library of Congress and National Archives websites.)

Primary Source: Three Lewis Hine photo captions, 1908 to 1911

Part 1 (a cotton mill, Lancaster, South Carolina, November 30, 1908): "Sadie Pfeifer, 48 inches high, has worked half a year. One of the many small children at work in Lancaster Cotton Mills."

Part 2 (a coal mine, South Pittston, Pennsylvania, January 1911): "View of the Ewen Breaker of the Pa. Coal Co. The dust was so dense at times as to obscure the view. This dust penetrated the utmost recesses of the boy's lungs."

Part 3 (a city street, Nashville, Tennessee, November 14, 1910): "A young truant newsie, during school hours. Nashville, Tenn."

Word help: 48 inches high = 48 inches tall; breaker = a building where coal was broken and sorted, and boys picked out the rock; Pa. Coal Co. = Pennsylvania Coal Company; dense = thick; obscure = hide; penetrated = went into; utmost recesses = deepest parts; truant = missing school without permission; newsie = a child who sold newspapers on the street.

Lewis W. Hine, photographs for the National Child Labor Committee. Part 1: Lancaster Cotton Mills, Lancaster, South Carolina, November 30, 1908 (Library of Congress, National Child Labor Committee Collection). Part 2: January 10, 1911, National Archives Identifier 523378. Part 3: November 14, 1910, National Archives Identifier 523343. Real wording; Part 2 is the first three sentences of a longer caption.

👁 Think Like a Historian

First I ask who made this source and why. Hine worked for a group that wanted to end child labor, so he chose what to photograph and what to write. That does not make his facts false: he wrote down names, sizes, places and dates that others could check. But I also ask whose voice is missing. In these captions, the children almost never speak for themselves.

🏛️ People Who Shaped the Age of Industry

Some built fortunes, some built unions, some built evidence. Read each card and ask: what did this person build, and what did they have to fight against?

Andrew Carnegie

1835–1919

Came from Scotland at 12 and rose from bobbin boy to steel giant. In 1889 he wrote an essay, "The Gospel of Wealth," saying rich people should use their fortunes to help the public. In 1901 he sold his company to banker J. P. Morgan for $480 million and gave away about $350 million in his lifetime. An honest story of Carnegie includes both the libraries and Homestead.

Mother Jones (Mary Harris Jones)

about 1837–1930

Born in County Cork, Ireland; historians are not sure of her exact birthday. In 1867 yellow fever took her husband and all four of their children, and in 1871 she lost everything in the Chicago fire. She became a fearless organizer for coal miners and, in 1903, led a march of child mill workers to protest child labor.

Clara Lemlich

1886–1982

Came from Ukraine, then part of the Russian Empire, to New York in 1903 and sewed shirtwaists. She was arrested many times while organizing. In 1909 she spoke in Yiddish; an English version of her words is: "I am one of those who suffers from the abuses described here, and I move that we go on a general strike."

Lewis Hine

1874–1940

A photographer hired by the National Child Labor Committee in 1908. He carried camera equipment that weighed about 50 pounds into mills, mines, fields and streets. The Library of Congress holds more than 5,100 of the committee's photographs, most of them by Hine.

Granville T. Woods

1856–1910

A Black engineer and inventor born in Columbus, Ohio. He earned nearly 60 patents, many for electric railways. His 1887 induction telegraph let moving trains exchange messages with stations. He joined the National Inventors Hall of Fame in 2006.

The Angel Island Poets

1910–1940

Mostly Cantonese-speaking men from villages in Guangdong province, held at Angel Island while officials questioned them. They carved poems in Chinese into the barracks walls, most unsigned and undated. Hundreds of poems were found, and scholars have recorded and translated many of them.
