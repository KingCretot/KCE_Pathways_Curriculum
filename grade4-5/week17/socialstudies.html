🚃 Jim Crow Laws and Plessy v. Ferguson

After federal troops left the South in 1877, Southern states passed laws that forced Black and white people apart. People called them Jim Crow laws, a name taken from a mocking song-and-dance act that a white performer, Thomas Dartmouth Rice, first put on in 1828. Starting with Florida in 1887, Southern states passed laws requiring separate train cars for Black and white passengers. Louisiana's Separate Car Act of 1890 required "equal but separate" train cars. A group of citizens in New Orleans formed a committee to test the law in court. On June 7, 1892, Homer Plessy, who had one Black great-grandparent and was counted as Black under Louisiana law, sat in a car for white passengers and was arrested. On May 18, 1896, the Supreme Court ruled 7 to 1 in Plessy v. Ferguson that "separate but equal" was allowed.

👁 Watch Me Think

I read the majority and the dissent side by side. The majority, written by Justice Henry Brown, said that separation did not mark Black citizens as lower. Justice John Marshall Harlan was the only judge to disagree. He wrote, "Our constitution is color-blind, and neither knows nor tolerates classes among citizens." But I keep reading. In the same dissent, Harlan called Chinese people "a race so different from our own." So even the judge who defended equal rights had a blind spot. Historians read the whole source, not just the famous line.

✍️ Your Turn, Part 2: Jim Crow and Plessy

🤝 Let's Try Together: Was "Separate" Ever "Equal"?

Let's test the court's reasoning with evidence. The majority said separation did not mark anyone as lower. But who wrote the law? White lawmakers, after Black voters were being pushed out. Who got a say? Not the Black passengers it controlled. And what did it do? It told one group of citizens where they could not sit. Plessy's committee argued that a law like that is unequal by its very nature. It took 58 years, until Brown v. Board of Education in 1954, for the Supreme Court to reject "separate but equal" in public schools.

🗳️ Taking Away the Vote

The 15th Amendment said no one could be denied the vote because of race. Southern states found ways around it without ever naming race. Louisiana is one example. First, new registration laws in the 1890s cut the number of voters. Then its 1898 state constitution set up a poll tax of $1 a year, with receipts from the two years before. It added a literacy test given by a registrar, who could decide who passed with no set standards. A man who could not pass the test could register only if he owned at least $300 in property, a large amount then. A "grandfather clause" let a man register anyway if his father or grandfather had been a registered voter in 1867, which helped many white men who could not meet the other rules. Only 111 Black men registered that way. The Supreme Court struck down grandfather clauses in 1915.

👁 Watch Me Think

I read the table for change over time, then ask what caused it. In 1897, Louisiana had 130,344 registered Black voters. In 1898, after new voting laws, it had 12,902. That is a drop of 117,442 people in about a year, about 9 out of every 10. The 15th Amendment did not change, and the people did not change. The rules changed. When numbers drop that fast, I look for a new law.

✍️ Your Turn, Part 2: Taking Away the Vote

🤝 Let's Try Together: Who Gets Blocked?

Let's check each rule. The poll tax blocked people who could not pay, and many Black families had little cash after slavery, though poor white men were blocked too. The literacy test let the registrar decide, so he could fail Black men and pass white men. The property rule let a man who failed the test register only if he owned $300 in property, so it helped richer men and blocked poor ones. Then the grandfather clause opened a side door, mostly for white men, because their fathers or grandfathers had been voters in 1867. Put together, the rules were written to look fair but to work against Black voters.

📰 Speaking Out and Organizing

Ida B. Wells was born enslaved in Holly Springs, Mississippi, on July 16, 1862. She became a teacher and then a journalist who co-owned a Memphis newspaper. In 1884 she refused to give up her first-class seat on a train and was forced off; she sued and won $500, but the Tennessee Supreme Court overturned the decision. In 1892, three of her friends who ran a grocery store in Memphis were taken from jail and killed by a white mob, with no trial. A killing by a mob like this is called a lynching. Wells began collecting facts about lynchings and publishing them. While she was in New York that year, a mob wrecked her newspaper's office, so she kept writing from the North and later settled in Chicago. In 1909, Wells, W. E. B. Du Bois, Mary Church Terrell and others founded the NAACP, the National Association for the Advancement of Colored People, after a deadly race riot in Springfield, Illinois, in 1908.

👁 Watch Me Think

Wells used evidence as her tool. In the preface to her 1892 pamphlet Southern Horrors, she called her work "a contribution to truth, an array of facts." When people in power told one story, she gathered facts so readers could not look away. That is what a historian does too: check the evidence before believing the story.

✍️ Your Turn, Part 2: Speaking Out and Organizing

🤝 Let's Try Together: The Great Migration Begins

Some families fought Jim Crow by leaving. Starting around 1910, Black Southerners began moving to Northern and Midwestern cities such as New York, Chicago, Detroit and Pittsburgh. During World War I, many factory workers were drafted and fewer immigrants arrived from Europe, so factories needed workers. From about 1910 to 1940, about 1.6 million Black Southerners moved, and by 1970 about 6 million had. Let's sort the causes: "push" reasons, like Jim Crow laws and violence, and "pull" reasons, like jobs and schools. The National Archives says the driving force was escaping racial violence, finding better jobs and schools, and getting free of Jim Crow.

🔍 Source Detective: Two Leaders, Two Plans

In 1895, Booker T. Washington gave a famous speech at a fair in Atlanta. In 1903, W. E. B. Du Bois answered him in a book. Read two short parts from each. (One older word is replaced with a word in [brackets].)

Primary Source: Booker T. Washington (1895) and W. E. B. Du Bois (1903)

Part 1 (Washington, 1895): "Cast it down in agriculture, mechanics, in commerce, in domestic service, and in the professions."

Part 2 (Washington, 1895): "In all things that are purely social we can be as separate as the fingers, yet one as the hand in all things essential to mutual progress."

Part 3 (Du Bois, 1903): "Mr. Washington distinctly asks that black people give up, at least for the present, three things,—First, political power, Second, insistence on civil rights, Third, higher education of [Black] youth . . ."

Part 4 (Du Bois, 1903): "Such men feel in conscience bound to ask of this nation three things: 1. The right to vote. 2. Civic equality. 3. The education of youth according to ability."

Word help: cast it down = put your effort into (Washington compared this to lowering a bucket into water); agriculture = farming; mechanics = skilled trades with tools and machines; commerce = business; domestic service = working in other people's homes; purely social = about friendships and private life; essential to mutual progress = needed for both groups to move ahead; distinctly = clearly; . . . = some words are left out here; such men = Black leaders who disagreed with Washington; insistence = firmly demanding; civic equality = equal rights as citizens; according to ability = as far as a student's talent can go.

Booker T. Washington, Atlanta Exposition Address, September 18, 1895, at the Cotton States and International Exposition (Fordham University Modern History Sourcebook). W. E. B. Du Bois, "Of Mr. Booker T. Washington and Others," in The Souls of Black Folk, 1903 (National Constitution Center). Real wording; short parts.

👁 Think Like a Historian

Both men wanted Black Americans to rise. I compare WHEN and HOW. Washington said: build skills, farms and businesses first, and do not push for social equality now. Du Bois said Black Americans could not wait: they needed the vote, equal rights and the best schools now. Then I check the dates. Du Bois wrote eight years later, after more Jim Crow laws had passed, so he could judge whether Washington's plan was working.

🏛️ People Who Fought for Equal Rights

They used speeches, books, courts, clubs and newspapers. Read each card and ask: what tool did this person use, and what were they fighting against?

Booker T. Washington

1856–1915

Born enslaved in Hale's Ford, Virginia. In 1881 he became the head of a new school for Black teachers in Tuskegee, Alabama, and stressed training in trades and farming. His 1895 Atlanta speech made him the best-known Black leader of his time. In 1900 he founded the National Negro Business League to support Black-owned businesses.

W. E. B. Du Bois

1868–1963

Born free in Great Barrington, Massachusetts. In 1895 he became the first Black American to earn a PhD from Harvard University. He wrote The Souls of Black Folk in 1903, helped start the Niagara Movement in 1905 and the NAACP in 1909, and started its magazine, The Crisis, in 1910.

Mary Church Terrell

1863–1954

Born in Memphis, Tennessee, to parents who had been enslaved, and educated at Oberlin College. In 1896 she helped found the National Association of Colored Women and served as its first president; its motto was "Lifting as we climb." She was a founding member of the NAACP. In 1950, at age 86, she protested a segregated restaurant in Washington, D.C., and in 1953 the Supreme Court ruled such segregation illegal.

Wong Kim Ark

Supreme Court case, 1898

A cook born in San Francisco to Chinese parents. In 1895, coming home from a family visit to China, he was refused entry to his own country. He went to court, and on March 28, 1898, the Supreme Court ruled 6 to 2 that, under the 14th Amendment, a person born in the United States is a citizen.

Jovita Idár

1885–1946

A Mexican American teacher and journalist from Laredo, Texas, who wrote for her family's Spanish-language newspaper, La Crónica. In 1911 her family organized the First Mexican Congress to fight unfair treatment of Mexican Texans, and she became the first president of the League of Mexican Women. In 1914 she stood in the doorway of the newspaper El Progreso to stop Texas Rangers from shutting it down.
