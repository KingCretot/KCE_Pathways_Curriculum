Remember These?

Last week you worked with Greek and Latin roots and context clues. A root is the main part of a word that carries its meaning, like Greek therm (heat), Greek hydro (water), Greek tele (far), Latin port (carry), and Latin dict (say). A prefix such as pre- (before) or un- (not) changes a word’s meaning. When a word is new, context clues, the words and sentences around it, help you figure it out. Play scripts are full of tricky words, so keep these tools handy!

🖼️ Picture It: How a Play Is Built

A play (also called a drama) is a story written to be acted out on a stage. A play is not written in paragraphs like a novel. It is written as a script: the name of each character, followed by the words that character says, plus notes that tell the actors what to do. There is no narrator to explain things, so you, the reader, have to figure out what the characters feel from what they say and do. Here is one moment from Frost Watch written three ways.

📜 How a script looks (Frost Watch, Act One)

ACT ONE, Scene 1
(The Delgado kitchen, 8:00 in the evening.)
TEO: (saluting) Yes, Captain Abuela!

🎭 In the PLAY: a script

TEO: (saluting) Yes, Captain Abuela!
The speaker’s name comes first, then a colon. No quotation marks. The stage direction tells the actor what to do.

📖 In PROSE: a story

Teo snapped a salute. “Yes, Captain Abuela!” he said.
A narrator describes the action. Spoken words go inside quotation marks, with a tag like “he said.”

🚫 MIXED UP: don’t do this

TEO: “Yes, Captain Abuela!” he said, saluting. (A script never puts quotation marks around lines or adds tags like “he said.” The action belongs in a stage direction.)

Part of a PlayWhat It Does
1. Acts and scenesA play is divided into big sections called acts. Each act is divided into smaller parts called scenes. A new scene usually means a new time or a new place.
2. Cast of charactersThe list at the very beginning that names everyone in the play, often with a short description of each one.
3. SettingThe time and place, usually described at the start of each scene in the stage directions.
4. Stage directionsWords in italics and (parentheses) that tell actors how to move, what to do, and how to say their lines, like (whispering). Actors do NOT say them out loud.
5. DialogueThe words characters speak. Each line starts with the speaker’s name (often in CAPITAL letters) and a colon. No quotation marks.
6. PropsObjects the actors use on stage, like a flashlight or a toolbox.
💡

Read Like an Actor

When you read a play, read the stage directions as carefully as the lines. A single word like (calmly) or (gruffly) can change what a line means and show you what kind of person is speaking.

🌟 Vocabulary Round-Up

Flip each card to review today's words. Many backs show an example from Frost Watch.

script
The written text of a play: every line of dialogue plus all the stage directions.
playwright
A person who writes plays. Spelling tip: it ends in -wright, an old word for “maker,” like a shipwright.
cast of characters
The list at the start of a play naming every character. Example: “TEO DELGADO, Paloma’s brother, age 8.”
scene
A part of an act set in one time and place. Example: “Scene 2: The strawberry field, 3:00 in the morning.”
stage directions
Words in parentheses telling actors how to move or speak. Example: “(TEO yawns and pulls his hood over his ears.)”
dialogue
The words characters say to each other. Example: “TEO: Black strawberries!”

🎬 The Play, Part 1: Cast and Scene 1

A script starts with the cast of characters and the setting. Then the action begins. Read the stage directions (in italics) as carefully as the lines.

The Play • Act One, Scene 1Frost Watch

a play by Lorena Vance

Cast of CharactersABUELA CARMEN DELGADO, a strawberry farmer who has worked the same field for forty years
PALOMA DELGADO, her granddaughter, age 11
TEO DELGADO, Paloma’s brother, age 8
MR. PRYOR, the farmer next door
RADIO ANNOUNCER (a voice only)
SettingA small strawberry farm near Plant City, Florida, on a January night. Plant City is known as the Winter Strawberry Capital of the World, because its berries ripen from December to March.

ACT ONE, Scene 1

(The Delgado kitchen, 8:00 in the evening. A radio sits on the counter. ABUELA CARMEN shells peas at the table. PALOMA writes in a notebook. TEO slides across the floor in his socks.)

RADIO ANNOUNCER: (crackling) . . . a freeze warning for eastern Hillsborough County tonight. Temperatures could drop to 29 degrees before sunrise.

(ABUELA stops shelling. She sets down the bowl.)

PALOMA: (looking up fast) Twenty-nine? Abuela, the berries can’t take twenty-nine. I wrote it down last year: at 30 degrees or colder, the open flowers turn black in the middle.

TEO: Black strawberries! Maybe we could sell them as a new flavor. Midnight Berry!

PALOMA: Teo, this isn’t a joke. If the flowers die, there won’t be any berries in February.

ABUELA CARMEN: (calmly) Paloma is right. And Teo is right too. We will need a sense of humor tonight. (She stands.) Paloma, check the pump. Teo, find the big flashlight.

TEO: (saluting) Yes, Captain Abuela!

(TEO races off. PALOMA flips through her notebook, chewing on her pencil.)

PALOMA: The pump made a funny noise on Sunday. What if it doesn’t start?

ABUELA CARMEN: Then we will knock on Mr. Pryor’s door. That is what neighbors are for.

(Lights fade.)

🎬 The Play, Part 2: Scene 2

Notice how the stage directions change the setting. It is now a new time and a new place, so it is a new scene.

The Play • Act One, Scene 2Frost Watch

a play by Lorena Vance

Scene 2

(The strawberry field, 3:00 in the morning. Rows of low plants stretch into the dark. A thermometer hangs on a post. PALOMA, wrapped in a blanket, shines the flashlight on it. TEO yawns and pulls his hood over his ears.)

PALOMA: (whispering) Thirty-four degrees. Abuela said to start the water at thirty-four.

TEO: Why do we spray water on them if it’s freezing? Won’t that make ice?

PALOMA: That’s the point. When water freezes, it gives off a little heat. As long as we keep the water coming, the ice keeps the flowers at about thirty-two degrees, even if the air gets colder.

TEO: (squinting at her) So ice is a blanket. That’s backward.

(ABUELA CARMEN turns the valve. The pump coughs, sputters, then goes silent.)

ABUELA CARMEN: (quietly) Ay. Not tonight.

(PALOMA grips the flashlight with both hands. Her voice shakes.)

PALOMA: What do we do? If we wait, the flowers will freeze.

TEO: (already running) I’ll get Mr. Pryor! I’m the fastest one here!

(TEO disappears into the dark. A long pause. A truck’s headlights sweep across the field. MR. PRYOR climbs out, carrying a toolbox.)

MR. PRYOR: (gruffly) Your brother pounded on my door like a woodpecker. Where’s the pump?

(MR. PRYOR kneels by the pump, tightens something with a wrench, and pulls the cord. The pump roars to life. Water hisses through the sprinklers.)

PALOMA: (letting out a long breath) Thank you, Mr. Pryor.

MR. PRYOR: Thank your brother. Now, nobody turns this off until the sun melts every bit of ice. Understood?

TEO: (proudly, out of breath) Understood, Captain Pryor.

(Lights fade as the sprinklers glitter in the flashlight beam.)

🎭 Dialogue Reveals Character

🔎

How to Read Character in a Play

A play has no narrator to tell you what the characters are like. You figure it out from four clues: (1) what they say, (2) how they say it, shown in stage directions like (calmly) or (gruffly), (3) what they do, and (4) how other characters react to them. Ask yourself: what kind of person would say this, in this way?

+5

TEO: Black strawberries! Maybe we could sell them as a new flavor. Midnight Berry! What does this line reveal about Teo?

+5

PALOMA: I wrote it down last year: at 30 degrees or colder, the open flowers turn black in the middle. Which trait does this line BEST show?

+5

ABUELA CARMEN: (calmly) Paloma is right. And Teo is right too. We will need a sense of humor tonight. Which explanation of Abuela’s character is correct?

+5

MR. PRYOR: (gruffly) Your brother pounded on my door like a woodpecker. Where’s the pump? What do his words AND actions reveal about him?

+5

Which line BEST shows that Teo is brave and quick to act?

+5

In Scene 1 Paloma asks, “What if it doesn’t start?” In Scene 2 she asks, “What do we do?” What do these questions reveal about her?

📖 Same Scene, Two Ways

🔁

From Script to Story

Here is part of Scene 2 rewritten as prose, the ordinary writing of stories, with sentences and paragraphs. Read it, then compare it with the script you just read.

Prose Version • Part of Scene 2Frost Watch (told as a story)

1At three o’clock in the morning, the strawberry field was dark and silent. Paloma pulled her blanket tighter and aimed the flashlight at the thermometer on the post. Thirty-four degrees. Her stomach tightened. That was the number Abuela had warned her about.

2Abuela Carmen turned the valve. The pump coughed, sputtered, and then went quiet. In the silence, Paloma could hear her own heart pounding. She gripped the flashlight with both hands so her grandmother would not see them tremble.

3“What do we do?” she asked. “If we wait, the flowers will freeze.”

4Before anyone could answer, Teo was gone, his sneakers slapping down the dirt road toward Mr. Pryor’s farmhouse. Paloma had always thought of her little brother as the family clown. Tonight, she realized, he was the one who did not hesitate.

FeatureIn the PlayIn the Prose Version
Who tells itNo narrator. The characters speak for themselves.A narrator tells the story.
Spoken wordsSpeaker’s name + colon. No quotation marks.Quotation marks plus a tag, like “she asked.”
ActionsStage directions in parentheses.The narrator’s sentences.
Thoughts and feelingsShown through lines, actions, and HOW lines are spoken.The narrator can tell private thoughts directly: “she realized.”
Organized byActs and scenes.Paragraphs (and chapters in a long book).
🎯

Comparing Tip

When you compare a scene with its prose version, ask three questions: What stayed the same? What did the prose ADD (often thoughts and feelings)? What did it LEAVE OUT (here, Teo’s question about ice and the whole ending with Mr. Pryor)?

📝 Writer’s Turn: Stage Directions to Prose

🖊️

Write Like a Narrator

A stage direction SHOWS what a character does. A narrator can also TELL what the character feels. Turn the stage direction into one or two prose sentences. Your writing is checked by fair rules: (1) use enough words, (2) do not copy 4 or more words in a row from the stage direction (names can stay), (3) name the character, and (4) keep the key ideas listed. If a rule is missed, the feedback tells you which one.

+6

Rewrite this stage direction as prose. Tell what Paloma does AND how she feels.

Stage direction (Scene 2): (PALOMA grips the flashlight with both hands. Her voice shakes.)
  • Use at least 10 words.
  • Do not copy 4 or more words in a row from the stage direction.
  • Name the character: include Paloma.
  • Keep the key idea: mention the flashlight.
  • Tell how Paloma feels, using a feeling word such as worried, nervous, scared, or afraid.

  • Keep the key idea: mention his hood.
  • Tell how Teo feels, using a word such as tired, sleepy, cold, or chilly.
