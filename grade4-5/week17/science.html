⛏️ Fossil Fuels and Other Nonrenewable Resources

Most of the electricity and fuel people use comes from nonrenewable resources, which cannot be replaced in a human lifetime. The biggest group is the fossil fuels: coal, oil (also called petroleum) and natural gas. The U.S. Energy Information Administration, or EIA, explains that coal formed from plants that lived hundreds of millions of years ago in swampy forests. Layers of dirt and rock buried the plants, and over millions of years heat and pressure turned them into coal. Oil and natural gas formed in a similar way, mostly from tiny plants and animals, such as diatoms, that lived in ancient seas. Because fossil fuels take millions of years to form, once we burn them, they are gone. Nuclear energy is nonrenewable too, but it is not a fossil fuel. Nuclear power plants split atoms of uranium, a metal mined from the ground. This splitting, called fission, releases heat. Many power plants, whether they burn coal or use nuclear fission, use that heat to boil water into steam. The steam spins a turbine, which turns a generator that makes electricity.

👁 Watch Me Think

A common myth says oil comes from dinosaurs. Let me check the evidence before I repeat it. EIA says coal came from ancient swamp plants, and oil and natural gas came mostly from tiny plants and animals, such as diatoms, that lived in ancient seas. So the gasoline in a car is made mostly from tiny sea life and plants, not from dinosaurs. When I hear a "fact" that everyone repeats, I still ask: what does the evidence say, and who collected it?

🤝 Let's Try Together: Renewable or Nonrenewable?

Let's sort energy resources with one question: can nature replace it quickly, in a human lifetime or less? Coal took millions of years to form, so it is nonrenewable. Uranium is a metal mined from the ground, and nature does not quickly make more, so it is nonrenewable even though it is not a fossil fuel. The Sun shines every day, so sunlight is renewable. Wood comes from trees, which can regrow in years, so wood is renewable, as long as people plant new trees. The key question is always: how fast does nature replace it?

☀️ Renewable Resources: Sun, Wind, Water, Earth's Heat and Plants

Renewable resources are naturally replaced in a short time. Solar panels change sunlight directly into electricity, as you saw in Week 12. Wind turbines have giant blades that the wind spins, turning a generator. Hydropower plants use moving water, often from a river held back by a dam, to spin turbines. Geothermal power plants use heat from deep inside Earth. EIA explains that most big geothermal resources are near the edges of Earth's tectonic plates, so most U.S. geothermal power plants are in western states and Hawaii, and California makes the most geothermal electricity. Biomass is energy from plants and animal wastes, such as wood, crop waste and garbage. It can be burned for heat or to make electricity. Renewable resources will not run out, but each one depends on nature: the Sun sets, the wind slows down, and a river can run low in a drought.

👁 Watch Me Think

Is solar energy totally free? The sunlight is free, and it will not run out. But let me think it through. Someone had to build the solar panel, and EIA notes that making solar panels uses hazardous chemicals that must be handled carefully. A big solar farm also needs land, which can disturb the plants and animals that live there. And at night, a solar panel makes no electricity at all. So "renewable" does not mean "no costs." It means nature keeps replacing the energy source itself.

⚖️ Trade-Offs and Reducing Our Impact

Every energy resource has trade-offs: benefits and costs. Fossil fuel power plants can run day or night, but EIA explains that burning fossil fuels releases carbon dioxide, a greenhouse gas, and that greenhouse gases trap heat and tend to warm the planet. Burning fuels can also pollute the air. Nuclear plants do not produce air pollution or carbon dioxide while they run, but their used fuel stays radioactive and dangerous for thousands of years, and the United States does not yet have a permanent place to store it. Solar and wind make no air pollution while they run, but they depend on the weather, and EIA notes that birds and bats can be hurt by turbine blades. Dams make electricity without burning fuel, but they can block fish such as salmon from swimming upstream; fish ladders help. Burning biomass releases carbon dioxide too, though the plants it comes from took in almost as much carbon dioxide as they grew. People can shrink their impact in two ways. Energy conservation means using less energy, like turning off lights when you leave a room. Energy efficiency means using technology that needs less energy to do the same job, like LED light bulbs.

👁 Watch Me Think

How do I decide which energy source is "best"? I make a fair comparison by asking the same questions about every source. Will it run out? Does it pollute the air while it runs? Does it work day and night? What does it do to land, water and wildlife? When I fill in my chart, no source gets a perfect score. That is why many places use a mix of sources, and why saving energy helps no matter which source we use.

✍️ Your Turn, Part 2: Trade-Offs

🤝 Let's Try Together: Conservation or Efficiency?

Let's sort some energy-saving actions. Turning off the TV when no one is watching: you are using less energy, so that is conservation. Replacing an old bulb with an LED bulb that gives the same light using less energy: same job, better technology, so that is efficiency. Riding your bike instead of getting a car ride: conservation. Choosing a refrigerator with the ENERGY STAR label, which marks energy-efficient products: efficiency. EIA also lists recycling aluminum cans as conservation. Both kinds of actions mean less energy has to be made in the first place.

👁 Think Like an Energy Analyst

An energy analyst reads data carefully. She notices the word "about": the numbers are rounded. She checks what is counted: big power plants, not rooftop panels. She checks the year, because the mix of sources changes over time. Then she asks questions the data can answer, such as which sources are largest, and she avoids claims it cannot answer, such as which source is "best."

🌞 People Who Powered New Ideas

These people and this Native nation found new ways to use energy, sometimes while facing danger, hunger or unfair barriers.

Mária Telkes

1900–1995

Mária Telkes was born in Budapest, Hungary, in 1900 and moved to the United States in 1925. She spent her career finding ways to use the Sun's energy. During World War II she invented a small solar still that used sunlight to turn seawater into drinking water for sailors and pilots stranded at sea. In 1948, with money from the sculptor Amelia Peabody, she helped create the Dover Sun House in Massachusetts, a home heated by the Sun. It stored the Sun's heat in about 21 tons of a salt that melted on sunny days and gave off heat as it hardened again in cold weather. She also invented solar ovens, and people called her the Sun Queen.

Lise Meitner

1878–1968

Lise Meitner was born in Vienna, Austria, in 1878 and became a physicist who worked for many years in Berlin, Germany. In 1938 she had to escape Nazi Germany because she was Jewish, and she moved to Sweden. Soon after, her longtime research partner Otto Hahn and his colleague Fritz Strassmann found puzzling results in experiments with uranium. Working with her nephew Otto Frisch, Meitner explained that the uranium atoms had split apart and released energy, and in early 1939 the process was named fission. Otto Hahn alone received the 1944 Nobel Prize in Chemistry, and many people argue she deserved to share it. The element meitnerium is named in her honor.

William Kamkwamba

born 1987

William Kamkwamba grew up on a farm in Malawi, in southeastern Africa. During a terrible famine in the early 2000s, his family could no longer pay his school fees, so he had to leave school. He kept learning at his village library, where a book called Using Energy showed him how windmills make electricity. At age 14 he built a windmill from scrap, including bicycle and tractor parts and bottle caps, and it made electricity for his family's home. Later he helped bring a well with a solar-powered pump to his village, and he graduated from Dartmouth College in the United States in 2014.

Navajo Tribal Utility Authority

Kayenta Solar Facility, 2017

The Navajo Nation, whose people call themselves the Diné, stretches across parts of Arizona, New Mexico and Utah. Its own utility, the Navajo Tribal Utility Authority, worked with a power company called the Salt River Project to develop the Kayenta Solar Facility near Kayenta, Arizona. When it opened in 2017, it was the first large solar farm on the Navajo Nation, turning the region's strong sunshine into electricity. It shows how a Native nation can make its own choices about which energy resources to use on its land.
